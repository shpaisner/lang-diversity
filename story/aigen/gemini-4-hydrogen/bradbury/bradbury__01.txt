In Staffordshire, the sky was never truly blue; it was the color of a tarnished kettle, stained with the soot of a thousand chimneys that breathed gray wool into the air. William Leadford walked the cobbles with coal dust ground into the creases of his knuckles and a penny newspaper folded like a blade inside his coat. In his cramped attic room, a cheap tin clock ticked away the narrow hours, its face yellowed like an old tooth, while the taste of sulfur lingered on his tongue. The headlines screamed of dreadnoughts steaming through the North Sea, of treaties tearing like dry parchment, of men marching toward the borders with brass buttons shining in the summer sun. War was coming, they said, a great red harvest. Yet when William tilted his chin upward, past the black silhouettes of the blast furnaces, he saw no red at all. There, pinned to the velvet dusk above the slag heaps, hung a green star. It was no bigger than a child’s marble, yet it burned with a cold, emerald clarity that made the streetlights look like dying tallow. He hated it. He hated the furnaces, the damp rooms smelling of boiled cabbage and cheap paraffin, and most of all he hated the small, fierce ache behind his ribs that beat out a single name with every pulse: Nettie. While Europe oiled its rifles and the comet crept closer through the dark, William stood in the cinders of his youth, listening to the distant, metallic clatter of a world preparing to burn.

Nettie had been the only green thing in his soot-stained life, a sprig of sweetbriar growing out of a cracked brick wall. She smelled of rain-washed linen and tart orchard apples, and when she laughed, the gray Staffordshire afternoons turned briefly to gold. Now she was gone, spirited away in a cloud of motor-car dust by young Verrall, whose father owned the earth beneath their boots and whose hands had never known the bite of iron. Verrall had a voice like warm honey and pockets full of silver, and he had taken Nettie south to the sea, leaving William nothing but a cold hearth and a fever in his blood. On a Tuesday evening, beneath the widening emerald smear of the comet, William walked into a pawnbroker’s shop that smelled of old wool and tarnished brass. He laid down his last saved shillings and took up a revolver. It was heavy, oiled, and shaped like a question. In his attic room, he sat by the window and spun the cylinder—click, click, click—while the green starlight pooled across his bare floorboards like spilled absinthe. Down in the street, boys were shouting extra editions about mobilization in the east, their voices shrill as crickets. William did not listen. He had his own small war to fight, a private thunderclap waiting in his coat pocket, and tomorrow he would take the morning train to find them.

The train south rattled through England like a tin toy shaken by an impatient child. William sat wedged between a nervous clerk and a red-faced major who tapped his cane and muttered about the Kaiser’s fleet, though neither man looked out the window for long. Outside, the summer countryside was transforming. The meadows of wheat and clover no longer looked golden; under the swollen, luminous fan of the comet, the fields glowed with a strange, bruised luminescence, as if the earth itself were holding its breath. At every station, crowds stood silent on the platforms, faces tilted skyward, washed in pale jade. There was a smell in the carriage now, faint at first, creeping past the coal smoke—something like crushed mint and ozone after a summer lightning storm. William kept his right hand buried deep in his coat pocket, his fingers curled tight around the checkered grip of the revolver. He thought of Nettie’s small white throat, of Verrall’s easy, mocking smile, and tried to stoke the familiar furnace of his rage. Yet the green light slipping through the train’s smudged glass made his anger feel small and brittle, like dried leaves. When he stepped onto the platform at Shaphambury, the salt wind hit his face, cool and vast, and above the dark horizon of the English Channel, the comet hung like a great, silent peacock of emerald fire, filling half the sky.

He found them at the edge of the shingle, where the sea sighed against the pebbles and a row of wooden bungalows sat dark against the dunes. A single Chinese lantern hung from a porch, swaying in the salt breeze, its paper ribs glowing a soft, earthly orange against the immense green cathedral of the night. Nettie was standing by the water’s edge in a pale dress, her hair blowing across her cheek, and Verrall stood beside her, his arm resting lightly on her waist. They were not laughing now; they were looking up at the sky, two small silhouettes painted against the luminous sea. William stepped out from the shadow of the tamarisk bushes, the pebbles crunching loud beneath his worn boots. He drew the revolver. The iron felt suddenly absurd in his fist, a dark little beetle crawling on his palm. "Nettie," he called, and his voice cracked like dry kindling. She turned, her eyes wide, catching the green fire from the heavens. Verrall stepped half a pace in front of her, his jaw tightening, though his face looked pale and hollowed out in the strange light. William raised the barrel. His finger found the cold curve of the trigger. He wanted to shout about his broken life, about the soot, the hunger, the stolen years—but before the words could leave his throat, the wind died completely, and a heavy, sweet-smelling mist began to drift down from the stars.

It did not fall like rain; it settled like a sigh. A thick, emerald vapor, cool as spring water and fragrant as a thousand crushed herbs, rolled across the shingle and wrapped them in a luminous fog. William tried to pull the trigger, tried to summon the hot, red fury that had carried him all the way from Staffordshire, but his arm felt heavy, weighted with a sudden, unbearable peace. He drew a breath, and the green air flooded his lungs. It was as if someone had opened a window in a stifling, smoke-filled room he had lived in his whole life. The iron grip of his jealousy dissolved like salt in warm water. Across from him, Verrall swayed, his hand falling from his side, his eyes fluttering shut as he sank gently to his knees on the smooth stones. Nettie gave a soft, wondering cry—not of fear, but of exhaustion—and folded down beside him like a flower closing at dusk. William let the revolver slip from his fingers. It struck the pebbles with a dull, forgotten clink. He sank down into the cool, sweet-smelling dark, watching the green mist swirl around his boots. Far away, across the Channel, the great guns were waiting to speak, the kings were signing their papers, the fleets were steaming through the night—but here, and everywhere beneath that emerald shroud, the fever was breaking, and the world was falling into a deep, dreamless sleep.

When William woke, the sky was a clear, washed blue, brighter than any morning he had ever seen, as though the air itself had been scrubbed clean of centuries of smoke. A gull wheeled overhead, its wings flashing white as fresh paper. He sat up slowly, rubbing his eyes, and looked down at his hands. They were still stained with Staffordshire coal, yet he felt no shame in them, no bitterness at the world that had put the soot there. A few yards away, the revolver lay among the pebbles, already dull with salt dew, looking like some foolish, rusted relic from a forgotten tribe. William picked it up by the barrel and tossed it lightly into the curling surf, watching it vanish beneath the green-clear water. Turning, he saw Verrall sitting up, blinking in the sunlight, a look of bewildered humility on his handsome face. Nettie was awake too, sitting with her knees drawn up, looking at William—not with terror, not with pity, but with the clear, unclouded gaze of a sister greeting a brother after a long voyage. William walked over and held out his hand to Verrall, helping the young man to his feet. They stood together on the bright shingle, the three of them, while down in the town a church bell began to ring, slow and sweet. Somewhere, the wind blew yesterday’s newspapers across the empty sands, their bold black headlines of war and hatred tumbling away like dry autumn leaves into the sea.
