The tall bottle-kilns of Staffordshire breathed black wool into the twilight, and William Leadford walked through the soot with a pocket full of stones and a heart like a clinker. All his twenty-two years tasted of coal-dust and boiled cabbage, of cheap ink and the bitter sweat of the pottery sheds. Yet he had owned one bright, impossible thing: Nettie. Nettie with her hair like spun cider and a laugh that could turn a wet Tuesday into July.

Now she was gone. The note on his mother’s oilcloth table had smelled faintly of lavender and betrayal. Forgive me, Will. Edward understands.

Edward Verrall. A gentleman’s son, all cream flannel and motorcar brass, smelling of petrol and easy money.

William stood in the cinder alley behind his house and looked up. Above the jagged teeth of the slag heaps, a new star hung in the east. It had been a pinpoint in April, a pale pea in May; now, in the thick heat of August, it was a cold green lantern, burning with the steady, unblinking gaze of a cat’s eye. The old men in the pubs said it was a sign of plague. The vicars said it was God’s thumbprint. William didn’t care what it was, so long as it lit his way south.

He walked down to the pawnshop on the corner where the brass balls hung like three tarnished moons. In the velvet tray lay a small, dark revolver, oiled and patient. William laid down his last silver shillings—money saved for a parlor rug, for a wedding—and slipped the cold iron into his coat. It felt heavy and right, like an answer to a question he had been shouting all his life.

The train south was a rattletrap dragon breathing sparks across the darkened shires. William sat in a third-class carriage, his knees pressed against the knees of two silent clerks who rustled their evening newspapers like dry autumn leaves.

War, the black headlines whispered. Ultimatum. Ironclads steaming in the North Sea.

All across Europe, men in braided coats were winding up the great clockwork of slaughter, oiling the gears with pride and old grudges. One of the clerks tapped the paper with a nicotine-stained finger. "They’ll be at each other’s throats by Friday," he said to no one in particular. "The Germans, the French, all of them. Madness in the air."

William stared out the grimy window. Madness? Yes, he knew that tune. It hummed in his own blood, a low, red static that drowned out the clack of the iron wheels. Let Europe burn; let the dreadnoughts sink to the green mud. His war was smaller, sharper, and it waited for him by the sea, in a rented bungalow where a rich man held a poor man’s girl.

Every mile south, the comet grew. Its light spilled through the carriage glass, washing the clerks’ tired faces in pale jade, turning their stiff collars the color of river moss. Even the locomotive’s smoke seemed tinged with emerald as it tore past sleeping villages and dark orchards. William’s fingers closed around the revolver in his pocket. The metal was no longer cold; it had drunk the fever of his palm, warm and pulsing, ready to speak a single, loud syllable in the dark.

He smelled the sea before he saw it—a sudden, sharp gift of salt and rotting kelp that cut through the coal-smoke of his clothes. By midnight, he was walking the shingle shore of the quiet coast, where whitewashed bungalows crouched in the dunes like sleeping gulls.

There was no true night anymore. The comet filled half the sky, a vast, silent waterfall of green fire suspended over the world. The English Channel lay beneath it like a sheet of hammered pewter, each wave catching a phosphor gleam before it broke upon the stones. Far out on the dark horizon, thin white fingers of light swept the clouds—searchlights from the great warships patrolling the narrow seas, probing for enemies in the luminous dark.

William found the house by the yellow motorcar parked in the sandy lane, its brass lamps dulled by sea-mist. Through a half-drawn curtain, a paraffin lamp glowed warm and amber against the alien green of the sky. A shadow moved across the glass. Nettie. He knew the tilt of her head, the quick, bird-like turn of her shoulders.

A terrible, freezing stillness settled into his chest. He crept into the prickly gorse bushes across the lane and sat down among the sand-burrs. He did not shiver, though the sea wind was keen. He waited as a stone waits, his eyes fixed on the painted door, his hand resting on the grip of the gun. Somewhere beyond the curve of the earth, cannons were being loaded, telegrams were racing along copper wires, and kings were signing their names in red ink. Here, in the gorse, only the crickets sang, and their song sounded brittle, like tiny glass bells about to shatter.

The false dawn came not with rose, but with a deeper, stranger emerald. The door of the bungalow clicked open, and they stepped out into the lane—Nettie in a pale shawl, Verrall beside her, his arm tucked lightly through hers as if they owned the morning.

William rose from the gorse like a man climbing out of a grave. His boots crunched on the gravel.

"Nettie!" His voice was rusted iron.

They stopped. Nettie’s hand flew to her throat, her face a pale oval in the comet’s glare. Verrall stepped half a pace in front of her, his jaw tightening, the old arrogance rising to meet the moment. "Leadford," he said, low and steady. "Put that away. Don't be a fool."

William raised the revolver. His arm was stiff, locked at the shoulder. He looked at Verrall’s clean collar, at Nettie’s terrified eyes, and all the soot of Staffordshire, all the hungry years, all the smallness of his life surged into his trigger finger.

Then the sky exhaled.

There was no thunder, no shriek of wind. Only a vast, soft sigh that rolled in from the east, across the sea, across the waking towns. A mist—thick, luminous, and sweet as crushed mint and summer rain—tumbled down from the green heavens. It swallowed the searchlights on the horizon. It wrapped the dunes, the whitewashed walls, the three figures frozen in the lane.

William tried to pull the trigger, tried to hold onto his precious, burning hate, but his lungs drank the cool green air. His knees buckled. The gun slipped from his fingers into the sand. Across the lane, Nettie and Verrall sank gently down, like children overtaken by sleep in a meadow, and the green silence folded over them all.

The world slept.

In the great iron foundries of the Midlands, the furnaces burned low and untended, their red mouths cooling to ash while the stokers slumbered on the cinders. On the decks of the North Sea dreadnoughts, admirals and cabin boys alike lay curled beside the brass breech-blocks, their hands uncurled, their dreams suddenly empty of flags and borders. In the palaces of Berlin and London and St. Petersburg, ministers slumped across their maps, ink drying on the nibs of their pens, while the green vapor drifted through the tall windows like a tide washing out a dirty room.

It was a long, breathless sleep, outside the ticking of clocks.

And as the comet’s tail swept softly over the spinning Earth, something ancient and crooked was burned away in the quiet dark. Not the houses, not the trees, not the beating hearts of men—only the fever. The small, sour smoke of jealousy; the blind, strutting pride of nations; the dark notion that one man’s joy must be bought with another man’s misery. All of it lifted like marsh-fog in the sun, dissolved into the great, cool chemistry of the stars.

For hours—or perhaps it was days, for time had lost its sharp edges—the Earth lay bathed in emerald twilight, breathing slow and deep. Then, far to the east, the green veil thinned. A wind from nowhere stirred the sea-grass. The comet was passing on, leaving behind a sky washed clean as a child’s slate, and a morning that had never existed before.

William woke to the sound of larks.

He lay on his back in the sand, blinking up at a sky of pure, astonishing blue, so clear it hurt his eyes like cold spring water. He sat up and took a breath. The air tasted of salt, of wild thyme, of apples—clean of coal-dust, clean of the sourness that had lived in his ribs since boyhood.

He looked down at his hand. Beside his knee lay the small black revolver, half-buried in the bright sand. He picked it up, turned it over in the sunlight, and felt a strange, gentle wonder. How small it was. How ugly and absurd, like a dead beetle he had once carried in his pocket as a boy. He stood up, walked a few paces to the low sea-wall, and tossed the thing far out into the incoming tide. It made a tiny plop and vanished beneath the foam.

Behind him, someone stirred in the lane.

Nettie was sitting up, pushing the cider-colored hair from her forehead, looking around with wide, bewildered eyes. Beside her, Edward Verrall slowly rose to his knees, brushing sand from his flannel coat, his face stripped of its old, hard mask.

William walked back toward them. There was no trembling in his legs, no knot in his throat. When Nettie saw him, she did not flinch; she looked at him as if she were seeing a brother returned from a long, dusty journey.

"Will," she whispered, her voice clear as a bell. "What happened to the sky?"

"It broke," William said softly, and smiled. "Or maybe we did."

Verrall stood beside her, looking out over the glittering, empty sea where no searchlights swept and no distant guns muttered. "The ships," Verrall murmured, as if remembering a bad dream. "There was going to be a war."

"Not today," William said.

They stood together in the bright, salt-washed morning, the three of them, with the whole wide, quiet world waiting to be begun again.
