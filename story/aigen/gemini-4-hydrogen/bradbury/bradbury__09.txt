In Staffordshire, the kilns burned all night like the open mouths of dragons, breathing soot and orange fire against the low, bruised belly of the sky. William Leadford walked the cobblestones with coal dust ground into his knuckles and a bitter taste like copper pennies on his tongue. He was twenty-two, thin as a winter fence-rail, his mind a cramped attic full of broken furniture. Down in the narrow streets, gaslamps hissed, and men stood outside the public houses waving damp newspapers whose headlines screamed of ultimata, of dreadnoughts steaming through the gray Channel, of Europe sliding like a runaway tram toward the cliff-edge of war.

William did not care about the Kaiser, though the same red fever hummed in his own blood. He cared only for the phantom of Nettie’s laughter, ringing in his ears like a silver bell struck in an empty room. She had smelled of lavender soap and warm bread; she had promised him a life beyond the Potteries. Then Verrall had come, sweeping down the lane in a roaring brass-and-leather motorcar, wearing crisp linen and the careless smile of a man who never counted pennies in the dark. Nettie had stepped into that machine and vanished into the dust.

Above the chimneys, higher than the soot could climb, the comet hung. Weeks ago it had been a chalk smudge on the blackboard of night. Now it was a great, pale-green feather, luminous and terrible, drifting closer with every tick of the town-hall clock. Men pointed at it with trembling pipes and whispered of judgment. William stared at its cold emerald heart, thinking how much it looked like the blade of a knife.

The revolver sat on the oilcloth table in his mother’s kitchen, heavy and dull, smelling of machine oil and finality. It had cost three weeks’ wages, a small, ugly lump of Birmingham iron. Upstairs, his mother coughed—a dry, papery sound, like autumn leaves scraping across a cellar floor. Forty years of Staffordshire clay had settled in her lungs, turning her slow and gray. William listened, and a terrible, righteous fury rose in his throat. Why should the Verralls have the green lawns, the sea air, and Nettie’s soft hands, while his mother choked in the dark?

Outside, a brass band marched past, playing a tinny, frantic tune. Young men in cloth caps cheered, their faces flushed with cheap ale and the sudden, intoxicating glory of hating strangers across the sea. The whole world had gone mad, William thought, wrapping the pistol in a handkerchief and sliding it into his coat. Every nation was sharpening its bayonets; every man’s heart was a little iron foundry forging spite. He was merely keeping pace.

He knew where they had gone. A railway telegrapher had seen them boarding the southern express for Shaphambury, where the rich went to breathe salt wind and forget the smoke. William blew out the kerosene lamp, plunging the kitchen into a ghostly half-light. Even through the grime-streaked pane, the comet poured its pale green radiance, casting double shadows across the floorboards—one black, one tinged with an underwater glow. He locked the door softly so his mother would not wake, and walked toward the station.

The night train rattled south like a string of iron beads, its wheels hammering a relentless chant against the rails: Find them, kill him, find them, kill him. William sat wedged in a corner of the third-class carriage, his collar turned up, his fingers curled around the cold metal in his pocket. Across from him, two young soldiers in stiff new khaki slept with their mouths open, rifles resting between their knees like tall, awkward umbrellas. When the train slowed at dark country junctions, William saw more of them on the platforms—boys with pink cheeks and feverish eyes, singing music-hall songs to drown out the quiet of the English fields.

Beyond the smudged glass, the countryside looked drowned. The comet had swollen until it filled half the southern sky, a vast, trailing veil of emerald and silver fire. The hedgerows, the sleeping haystacks, the winding rivers all gleamed beneath it like things seen at the bottom of a deep, still pond. There was no darkness left in the world, only this eerie, breathless luminescence that made the skin prickle and the teeth ache.

William pressed his forehead against the vibrating pane. He tried to summon Nettie’s face—the curve of her cheek, the way she tucked a stray curl behind her ear—but all he could see was Verrall’s gloved hand on the steering wheel of that gleaming motorcar. Jealousy was a physical thing, a hot, acid smoke filling his chest until he could scarcely breathe. He thought of the guns waiting in France, the battleships in the North Sea, and felt a grim kinship with them all. Tonight, everything would break.

Shaphambury smelled of kelp, wet shingle, and ozone, a sharp scent that startled William’s soot-trained lungs. Past midnight, the seaside town was wide awake, bathed in the uncanny green daylight of the falling star. People stood in nightshirts on the esplanade, silent as ghosts, staring up where the comet’s head burned like a blinding emerald sun, its tail sweeping from horizon to horizon. Out in the bay, three gray cruisers lay at anchor, signal lamps blinking frantic, silent messages across the water.

William walked quickly along the cliff path, his boots crunching on pebbles. His heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird. He had peered through the tall, lighted windows of the grand hotels until a porter chased him off, and then he had followed the coastal path where lovers always walked.

There, where the chalk cliff dropped away to the whispering tide, he saw them. Nettie wore a pale summer cloak that caught the green starlight; Verrall stood close beside her, his arm around her waist, both gazing out over the luminous sea.

William stepped from behind a clump of gorse. The revolver was in his hand before he knew he had drawn it.

“Nettie!” he cried, his voice cracked and raw.

She spun around, her face drained of color in the weird glow, eyes widening in sudden terror. Verrall thrust her behind him, stepping forward with raised fists, shouting a warning the rising sea-wind tore from his lips. William raised the iron barrel, sighting down its length at Verrall’s chest, his whole cramped, bitter life narrowing to the tiny, trembling notch of the sight.

William’s finger tightened on the trigger. He saw Verrall brace himself; he heard Nettie’s sharp, broken gasp.

Then the sky sighed.

It was not thunder, nor the roar of cannon, though every battleship in the bay seemed to shiver on the dark glass of the sea. It was a vast, rushing exhalation, as if some immense sleeper in the heavens had turned and breathed out across the spinning earth. A curtain of luminous green mist swept down from the zenith, swallowing the stars, rolling over the cliffs like a tide of cool, fragrant milk.

William tried to pull the trigger, but his hand had turned to wax. The green vapor washed over his face, and it did not choke him like the Staffordshire kilns. It smelled of crushed mint, of summer rain on hot slate, of pine needles and the first clean morning of the world. He drew a deep, involuntary breath, and a strange, singing coolness rushed into his chest, quenching the hot ash that had burned there for months.

The revolver slipped from his fingers and vanished into the tall grass without a sound. Across the path, Nettie’s knees buckled softly; Verrall sank down beside her, his raised fists opening like flowers in the dark. Along the esplanade, the silent crowds were falling gently to the pavement, and out on the water, the signal lamps of the warships flickered once and went dark. William’s legs gave way. He dropped into the cool clover, watching the emerald fog swirl above him like a gentle sea, and slipped into a dreamless, silver sleep.

When William opened his eyes, the world had been washed clean.

A pure, golden dawn was breaking over the Channel, brighter and clearer than any morning he had ever known, as if the air itself were made of crystal. Dew glittered on every blade of grass, on his frayed cuffs, on the pale clover beside his cheek. A few inches from his hand lay the Birmingham revolver. He looked at it for a long time. It seemed small and absurd, a rusty, childish toy left behind from a nightmare he could scarcely remember.

He sat up, drawing a long breath that tasted of salt and sweetness. His mind felt vast and quiet, like a great, sunlit room from which all the broken furniture had been cleared away. A few yards off, Nettie was stirring, pushing herself up on one elbow, her hair tangled with grass and dew. Beside her, Verrall blinked at the sky, rubbing his forehead with the bewildered air of a man waking from a fever.

William stood up. He looked at Nettie—truly looked at her—and saw not a prize stolen from him, nor a promise broken, but simply a young woman, frightened and wondering, under a new sky. There was no hatred left in him for Verrall, either; only a vast, aching tenderness for the two of them, for the sleeping town behind them, and for the silent gray warships resting peacefully on the sunlit water, their crews waking to the same quiet grace.

William walked across the grass. Verrall looked up, tensing for a moment out of old habit, then his shoulders dropped as he saw William’s face. William reached down, offering his hand, and Verrall took it without a word. Together, they helped Nettie to her feet, and the three stood on the chalk cliff, watching the morning light spread across a forgiven world.
