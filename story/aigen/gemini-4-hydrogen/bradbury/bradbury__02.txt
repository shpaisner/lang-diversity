In those last days, the sky over Staffordshire was the color of a bruised plum, smeared with the soot of a thousand brick kilns. William Leadford stood by his narrow window, his knuckles white against the sill, and watched the green star grow. It hung above the chimney pots like a drop of cold poison, brightening as the sun bled out behind the slag heaps. On the table behind him lay the morning paper, its ink still damp with the names of distant cities and marching armies. Europe was a dry forest waiting for a spark.

William did not care about Europe. His own war was smaller, sharper, and fit neatly inside his coat pocket.

He reached into his pocket and touched the revolver. It was cheap, heavy iron, bought with coins he had starved himself to save. Cold as winter water, yet it burned his palm. Nettie was gone. She had taken her bright ribbons, her quick, breathless laugh, and run south with Verrall—Verrall with his soft hands, his motorcar, his easy way of speaking as though the world were a parlor built for his comfort. All week, the thought of them had rattled in William’s ribs like a trapped bird.

He picked up his cap. The room smelled of damp plaster, cold ashes, and the bitter dregs of tea. Everything in Staffordshire smelled of things burning down to nothing. Outside, a train whistle shrieked across the dark meadows, a thin, iron cry. William blew out the candle, leaving only the pale emerald light of the comet pooling on the floorboards, and stepped out into the night to hunt them down.

The midnight train was a rattling cage of brass and horsehair, hurtling south through an England that seemed to be holding its breath. William sat in the corner of the third-class carriage, his hat pulled low, listening to the steel wheels hammer out a single word against the rails: Verrall, Verrall, Verrall.

Across from him, two men in tweed overcoats leaned over a map, their voices tight and dry as rustling leaves. They spoke of dreadnoughts steaming out of harbor, of ultimatums ticking like pocket watches, of boys in clean uniforms waiting at the borders. Every few minutes, one of them would glance nervously at the carriage window, where the comet’s pale green banner now stretched a third of the way across the stars.

"A bad omen," the older man muttered, tapping his pipe against his boot. "Mark me, there’ll be blood before the week is out."

William stared at his own reflection in the dark glass. His face looked hollow, carved from tallow and shadow. He wondered if the men on the borders felt this same tight band of iron around their skulls, this same sour heat in the throat. All his life he had been told to wait his turn, to tip his hat, to eat the crust while others took the loaf. And when he had finally found something bright and warm to hold—Nettie’s hand in the summer lanes—someone richer had simply reached down and plucked her away.

His fingers closed around the walnut grip in his pocket. Let the armies march, he thought. Let the whole rotten world burn. He was only going to set one small account straight before the fire reached him.

By the time William stepped onto the platform at Shaphambury, the night had turned strange and luminous. The seaside town smelled of salt, wet shingle, and expensive cigars, a world away from the sulfur and coal-smoke of the Potteries. Yet above the slate roofs and the grand white facades of the promenade, the comet loomed monstrously large, a silent waterfall of green fire spilling across the heavens.

The streetlamps flickered weakly against it, their yellow gaslight drowned in the unearthly emerald glow. People stood in little knots along the sea wall, their faces upturned and ghostly, whispering about the telegraph wires humming with rumors of war. No one looked at William. In his dusty boots and frayed collar, he moved among the holidaymakers like a shadow from another century.

He found the hotel first, a towering wedding-cake of brick and glass, and waited in the dark laurel bushes until a porter’s careless word gave him their direction. They had walked down toward the quiet stretch of sands beyond the pier, away from the anxious crowds.

William followed the shoreline. The tide was out, leaving great shining flats of wet sand that mirrored the comet’s blazing tail, so that he seemed to be walking between two green skies. The surf whispered against the pebbles—hush, hush, hush—trying to quiet the frantic hammering of his heart. Ahead, near a cluster of chalk rocks and sea-grass, he saw a flicker of white linen and heard a soft, familiar burst of laughter.

He drew the revolver from his pocket. The metal clicked softly in the salt air, a tiny, deadly sound lost in the roar of the sea.

They were sitting on a spread steamer rug near the dunes, looking out at the phosphorescent water. Verrall had his arm draped lightly across Nettie’s shoulders, his profile sharp and handsome in the starlight, his white shirtfront gleaming. Nettie leaned against him, her hair loose, looking young and foolish and unbearably lovely.

William stepped out from behind the chalk boulder. His shadow fell long and black across the sand between them.

"Nettie," he said. His voice sounded rusted, like a gate that had not been opened in years.

She gasped, scrambling to her knees. Verrall was up in an instant, placing himself in front of her, though he blinked in confusion as his eyes adjusted to the gloom. "Leadford? What in God’s name are you doing here?"

"I’ve come for what’s mine," William said. He raised the revolver. The barrel trembled slightly, catching the green shimmer from the sky.

"William, no!" Nettie cried, her hands flying to her mouth. "Put it down! You’re mad!"

"Mad?" William laughed, a dry, barking sound that hurt his chest. "Look up there, Verrall. Look at the sky. The whole world’s gone mad. They’re loading the cannons in Berlin and Paris tonight, and you’re down here on the sand stealing another man’s life like it’s a ripe plum."

Verrall stood very still, his jaw tight, though William saw the sudden sweat shine on his forehead. "Don't be a fool, Leadford. Shooting me won't make her love you."

"It will make things even," William whispered. He thumbed back the hammer. The cold iron felt righteous, the only honest thing left in a lying world. He aimed at the white square of Verrall’s chest, took a breath to steady his hand, and began to squeeze the trigger.

Then the wind died.

It did not merely drop; it vanished, as if the Earth had sailed suddenly into a vast, still harbor. The surf fell silent against the shingle. Even the gulls wheeling in the high dark ceased their crying.

Above them, the great green broom of the comet seemed to dip low, brushing the very tops of the cliffs. A mist began to fall—not cold like sea-fog, nor choking like the smoke of Staffordshire, but a soft, luminous vapor that smelled faintly of crushed mint and summer rain after a long drought. It drifted down through the air like green snow, settling on William’s eyelashes, on Nettie’s upturned face, on the dark barrel of the revolver.

William tried to pull the trigger, but his finger had turned to lead. A sudden, overwhelming drowsiness washed over him, warm as a hearth-fire, sweet as the first sleep of childhood. He blinked. The hatred that had burned in his belly for weeks seemed to uncoil like a spring, leaving behind only a strange, hollow ache.

"Look," Nettie whispered, her voice no longer frightened, only filled with a sleepy wonder.

Verrall swayed, his hand dropping to his side. He looked up at the swirling emerald sky, his face slackening, all his pride and cleverness melting away in the quiet light.

William took one stumbling step backward. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and sank halfway into the soft sand. His knees buckled. He did not feel the chill of the earth as he lay down; he felt only the green air filling his lungs, washing out the soot, the bitterness, the long, dark fever of his life. Around him, Nettie and Verrall sank down too, until all three lay still beneath the falling stars.

When William opened his eyes, the world was new.

Sunlight—clear, golden, and sharp as spring water—poured over the chalk cliffs. A lark was singing high in the blue, a thread of silver sound stitched across the morning. William sat up slowly, brushing dry sand from his coat. His head felt remarkably light, as though an iron band had been pried off and tossed into the sea.

Beside him lay the revolver. He picked it up and stared at it with gentle bewilderment. How strange that only hours ago he had believed this lump of metal could mend a broken heart. How small his mind had been, locked inside its cage of jealousy and grime. He dropped the gun into a tide pool, where it sank among the bright pebbles.

Nearby, Verrall was sitting up, looking at his fine evening clothes as if he could not remember why he had worn them. Nettie stirred, lifting her face to the sun. When she saw William, she did not flinch. She only looked at him with clear, sorrowful eyes, seeing him—really seeing him—for the first time.

"William," she said softly.

He walked over to them, feeling no anger, no triumph, not even the old desire to possess her. He saw Nettie as a frightened girl reaching for brightness, and Verrall as a lonely man who had never been taught to share. He knelt and offered Verrall his hand.

Beyond the glittering channel, the great armies of Europe were waking in their trenches, blinking in the same clean light, letting their rifles fall into the clover. In Staffordshire, the furnaces were cooling. There would be no war. There was only the wide, sunlit shore, and three strangers woken from the same long nightmare, ready at last to learn how to live.
