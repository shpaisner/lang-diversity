In Staffordshire, the night did not fall; it settled like soot from a thousand chimneys. William Leadford walked the cinder paths with coal dust wedged deep under his fingernails, where no soap could ever reach. He was twenty-two, thin as a winter reed, and his chest burned with two kinds of fire: the sour smoke of the Potteries and the sharper, sweeter agony of Nettie. She had a laugh like bright pennies dropped into a well, and eyes that held no reflection of the blast furnaces. Every evening he waited by the canal bridge, watching the dark water carry away the orange glare of the kilns, hoping to catch the hem of her skirt, the scent of lavender she wore to drown out the sulfur.

But tonight, the bridge was empty, and something else watched him from the east.

High above the jagged roofs of the clay-ovens hung a new star. It was not the honest, golden spark of the old constellations. It was green—a pale, cold emerald, trailing a faint broom of mist across the bruised sky. Men stood in their doorways in shirtsleeves, pipes dead in their mouths, staring upward. They muttered that it was an omen, a green lantern hung out by God before the storm. William only spat into the black canal. Let the sky burn green or red; what did a clerk with eighteen shillings a week care for the geometry of heaven? His world was narrow, walled in by red brick and damp plaster, and somewhere inside those walls, Nettie was slipping away from him like water through broken fingers.

By Tuesday, the whisper had a name, and the name was Verrall.

William heard it in the clatter of the tramcar and saw it chalked in the cruel, silent grins of the boys at the foundry gate. Verrall, the young gentleman from the big house on the hill, with his motorcar that smelled of leather and expensive oil, his cuffs always white as bleached bone, his pockets jingling with silver he had never bled for. Nettie had gone with him—down to the southern coast, they said, where the air was soft and the sea washed away the memory of Staffordshire grime.

William walked into the pawnshop on High Street, where old brass trumpets and tarnished watches slept behind dusty glass. He laid down his week’s wages and took a small, blunt revolver. It was ugly, heavy as a stone, and smelled of stale grease. He slipped it into his coat pocket, and at once his walk changed; he leaned slightly to the right, anchored by iron.

Outside, the newsboys were running like startled crows, waving damp sheets of ink. Mobilization! they cried. Cruisers in the North Sea! War by Friday! Men gathered around the gaslamps, their faces suddenly tight and hungry, talking of borders and flags and blood. William listened, and a dark, cold joy bloomed in his ribs. The whole world was reaching for its gun. Why shouldn't he? Europe was a powder keg waiting for a match, and in his pocket, his fingers curled around the cold trigger, waiting for the flesh of a man who had stolen his only light.

The train south was a rattling iron cage filled with men singing songs they didn't quite believe. They passed around bottles of warm beer and shouted about the Kaiser, their eyes bright with a strange, borrowed fever. William sat in the corner by the soot-streaked window, his cap pulled low, watching England unroll in the twilight.

Here, there were no slag heaps. The fields were wide and pale with ripening wheat, the orchards heavy with green apples. Yet over it all hung the comet. Each night it had grown, swelling like a luminous dandelion seed blown across the dark velvet of space. Now its tail stretched halfway to the zenith, a vast, silent fan of phosphor-green light that turned the whitewashed cottages the color of sea-glass and made the faces of the sleeping soldiers look like drowned men.

William did not sleep. He pressed his forehead against the vibrating glass, and the rhythm of the iron wheels hammered two words into his skull: Nettie. Verrall. He had followed the trail of a telegram, a gossiping maid, a porter’s memory of a laughing girl in a blue hat. They were at Shaphambury, on the chalk cliffs where the rich went to play at being simple.

At midnight, the train stopped at a junction, and through the open window came the distant, unmistakable mutter of thunder—except the sky was clear as crystal. Somewhere out beyond the dark rim of the Channel, the great gray ships were speaking to one another in iron. William touched his pocket. The world was ending, or beginning, in fire, and he meant to have his small, bitter share of the wreckage before the night was out.

He found them at dusk, on the high turf above the sea, where the wind smelled of salt and wild thyme.

The comet filled the sky now. It was no longer a star; it was a great, ghostly wing of green fire brooding over the curve of the Earth, casting faint, twin shadows from every gorse bush and pebble. Far out on the black water, a sudden orange bloom flowered on the horizon, followed seconds later by a dull, heavy thump that shook the soles of William’s boots. Another flash, then another. The warships were at it in the dark, tearing the night apart.

On the chalk path ahead, Nettie stopped and turned, her white dress glowing faintly emerald in the strange light. Beside her stood Verrall, tall and easy, one hand resting lightly on her elbow. They looked like figures cut from porcelain.

"William," Nettie breathed. There was no anger in her voice, only a small, startled pity that made his stomach twist into a knot of acid.

He stepped out from behind the gorse, his coat flapping in the sea wind, the revolver leveled at Verrall’s chest. His arm shook, not from fear, but from the sheer, crushing weight of twenty-two years of cold bedrooms and coal dust.

"You couldn't leave me one thing," William said, his voice thin and ragged as torn paper. "Not one thing in the whole dirty world."

Verrall didn't flinch; he stepped in front of Nettie, his jaw set. "Put it down, Leadford. Look at the sky. Look at the sea. We're all mad tonight."

"I'm not mad," William whispered, his thumb drawing back the hammer with a sharp, metallic click. "I'm awake."

His finger tightened on the iron. Just then, the green light above them seemed to sigh.

It was not a sound, not quite, though the grass bent flat as if beneath a sudden, warm breath. From the heart of the comet’s vast tail, a mist was falling—a luminous, emerald vapor that drifted down over the cliffs, over the burning ships far out at sea, over the sleeping villages and the marching armies of Europe.

It touched William’s face first, cool as spring water, smelling faintly of crushed mint and summer rain after a long drought. He blinked. The tightness in his chest, that knot of iron and ash he had carried since boyhood, seemed to loosen and untie itself.

Verrall staggered back a step, raising a hand to his forehead, his eyes wide with sudden, childlike wonder. Nettie let out a soft sigh, her knees buckling gently onto the thick turf. She did not look afraid; she looked like a child slipping under cool sheets at the end of a long, hot July day.

William tried to hold onto his rage. He gripped the revolver harder, trying to remember the coal dust, the eighteen shillings, the sting of her laughter. But the green fog was in his lungs now, washing through his veins like quiet music. His arm grew heavy, impossibly heavy. The pistol slipped from his numb fingers and vanished into the clover without a sound.

He sank to his knees beside them, watching the emerald mist swallow the distant flashes of the naval guns, wrapping the whole fevered, quarreling world in a deep and dreamless sleep.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world smelled as if it had been washed in rain and dried in an orchard.

He lay on the chalk cliff for a long moment, listening. There were no guns on the horizon. Only the hush of the tide dragging pebbles down the shore, and a lark spiraling up into a sky so blue it hurt to look at. The green fog was gone, leaving behind only a million diamond beads of dew clinging to the grass.

Slowly, William sat up. He felt light, hollow in a clean, good way, like a house whose windows had all been thrown open to the morning wind. Beside him in the clover lay the revolver, its dull iron already beaded with moisture. He picked it up, looked at it with a faint, puzzled curiosity—how strange that men had ever made such heavy, ugly little toys—and tossed it gently over the cliff edge into the deep sea below.

A few yards away, Verrall was stirring, sitting up and rubbing his eyes, blinking at the bright water. Nettie woke next, pushing the damp hair from her forehead. She looked at Verrall, and then she looked at William.

William stood up and walked over to them. There was no heat left in his heart, no desire to own her, no need to punish the man beside her. He saw them both clearly for the first time: just two frightened, foolish creatures, no different from himself, waking up in a vast and quiet garden. He held out his hand to help Verrall to his feet, and when Nettie stood beside them, the three of them turned together toward the sleeping town, walking down the bright path into a world that had forgotten how to hate.
