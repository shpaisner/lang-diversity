In Staffordshire, the sky was never truly blue; it was the color of an old iron kettle left out in the rain, bruised with coal smoke and the red sigh of the blast furnaces. William Leadford walked the cinder paths with soot in the creases of his knuckles and a sour copper taste on his tongue. He was twenty-two, thin as a winter fence post, and his pockets held nothing heavier than a bent sixpence and a heart full of broken glass.

Above the jagged teeth of the pottery kilns, the comet hung.

Night after night it had crept closer, a great green sickle swinging out of the dark meadow of space. The old women in the rows pulled their shawls tight and whispered of plagues and judgment, while the men at the alehouse tilted their mugs and laughed, though their eyes stayed nervous and bright. William did not laugh. He stood in the ash-yard behind his mother’s cottage and stared at the pale emerald flame washing over the slate roofs. It made the slag heaps look like sleeping dragons. It made him think of Nettie, who had smelled of crushed clover and warm bread, and who was gone.

She had left no letter, only the empty space where her shadow used to fall across the garden gate. Every evening at six, William had waited there, his heart beating like a trapped sparrow against his ribs, until the dusk turned purple and the gas lamps hissed awake. Now the gate swung on one hinge, creaking in the sulfur wind, and the green star burned coldly overhead, indifferent as a rich man’s diamond.

Her name was a fever, and Verrall was the chill that followed it.

William knew Verrall’s kind—the sons of men who owned the brickworks and the mines, who walked the high street in boots polished like chestnuts and smelled of lavender water instead of sweat. Verrall had a motorcar that coughed and roared like a brass beast, scattering chickens and coal-carts alike. He had come down to the lane with his easy smile and his silver watch chain, and Nettie had looked at him the way a moth looks at a parlor lamp.

On Tuesday, William stood outside the pawnshop on High Street, watching his own pale reflection in the dusty glass. Inside, among the tarnished trombones and dead grandfather clocks, lay a small black revolver.

"Thirty shillings," the pawnbroker said, wiping his spectacles on a grease-stained apron. "Belgian make. Good for stray dogs or burglars."

William paid with the savings he had kept in a tin biscuit box beneath his floorboards—money meant for a white tablecloth, a brass bedstead, a life with Nettie in a cottage where the smoke did not settle on the sills. When his fingers closed around the grip, the iron was cold as a winter frog. He slipped it into his coat pocket. It pulled the wool down on his right side, heavy and sure, a secret pendulum swinging against his thigh as he walked toward the railway station. If he could not have her hand, he thought, watching the green comet rise above the telegraph wires, then no man in fine boots would hold it either.

The train south rattled like a box of old bones, carrying William toward the sea where the gossip said they had fled.

Every station they passed was choked with boys in stiff new khaki, their mothers weeping into crumpled handkerchiefs while brass bands blared tinny, frantic marches into the humid twilight. War was coming. You could smell it in the railway carriages, sharper than the coal smoke—a dry, electric scent like thunderheads piling up over the channel. Men in bowler hats snapped open their newspapers, their voices rising in sharp, ugly barks over the clatter of the wheels.

"Ultimatum!" one shouted, jabbing a thick finger at the ink. "The Germans are mobilizing at the border. We’ll have our cruisers in the North Sea by dawn!"

"Let them come," another muttered, his face flushed dark as a plum. "We’ll teach them manners with cold steel."

William pressed his forehead against the vibrating glass. Outside, the English countryside rushed past, drowned in the eerie, phosphorescent wash of the comet. The hawthorn hedges glowed like reefs under green water; the sheep in the meadows looked like pale stones. The whole world, it seemed to William, had caught the same madness that burned behind his eyes. Empires were clenching their fists, cannons were being greased and rolled toward the cliffs, and in his pocket, the little Belgian pistol grew warm from the heat of his palm. He felt no fear of the coming slaughter, only a dark, whistling emptiness that demanded to be filled with noise and smoke.

He found them at Shaphambury, where the chalk cliffs dropped down to a restless shingle beach.

The seaside town was half-deserted, its colored bathing machines locked up tight, its pier stretching out into the dark water like a skeleton’s ribs. Most of the townsfolk were huddled indoors behind drawn curtains, waiting for either the end of the world or the first flash of naval gunfire on the horizon. Only the sea was awake, rolling in great, oiled swells that caught the emerald glare of the sky and shattered it into a million green sparks along the pebbles.

William walked along the edge of the surf, the salt spray stinging his chapped lips. Then, near the shadow of the breakwater, he saw them.

Nettie was wearing a white summer dress that fluttered like a moth’s wing in the rising wind. Verrall stood beside her, his arm draped lightly across her shoulders, his face tilted up toward the monstrous green veil that now covered half the heavens. They looked small and fragile against the vast, glowing dark.

William’s boots crunched on the shingle. They turned together, startled as deer.

"Willie?" Nettie whispered. Her voice was thin, carried away by the tide.

He drew the revolver from his coat. In the comet’s light, the barrel gleamed like wet slate. Verrall stepped half a pace in front of her, his jaw tightening, though his hands trembled at his sides.

"Stay back, Leadford," Verrall said, trying to sound like his father at the mill gates. "Put that away. You’re out of your mind."

"No," William said, his throat dry as chalk. "I’ve never seen clearer in my life."

He raised the iron weight, sighting down the barrel at the pale triangle of Verrall’s shirtfront. His thumb drew back the hammer with a sharp, metallic click that sounded louder than the breaking waves. Nettie let out a small, choked cry and buried her face against Verrall’s sleeve.

William’s finger began to tighten on the trigger.

Then, the sky sighed.

It was not an explosion, nor the crack of thunder, but a sound like ten thousand silk curtains tearing softly in the high, cold dark. Above them, the green tail of the comet seemed to bend, blooming outward like a dandelion clock struck by a sudden gust of wind. A faint, luminous mist began to drift down over the cliffs, over the silent pier, over the dark warships steaming blindly out in the channel.

It touched William’s face first—cool and sweet, smelling impossibly of crushed mint, summer rain, and the ozone after a lightning strike.

He blinked. The terrible, tight knot beneath his ribs suddenly loosened, as if a rusted iron band had snapped in two. His arm grew heavy, weighted not with anger, but with an immense, tidal weariness. The little pistol slipped from his numb fingers and clattered harmlessly onto the wet pebbles.

Across from him, Verrall’s knees buckled. He sank down onto the shingle, pulling Nettie gently with him, their heads drooping like flowers at dusk. William felt the cool stones rise up to meet his cheek. The emerald fog wrapped around them all, soft as eiderdown, and in the great, hushed cathedral of the night, the angry world closed its eyes and fell asleep.

Morning arrived not with a trumpet, but with the quiet, silver ringing of a thrush in the cliff grass.

William opened his eyes. The sky above him was a blue so pure and deep it made his chest ache with a sudden, nameless joy—not the bruised iron kettle of Staffordshire, not the feverish emerald of the night before, but the washed, bright blue of a child’s marble.

He sat up slowly, brushing sand from his coat. Beside him, lying in a hollow of the pebbles, was the Belgian revolver. He looked at it for a long moment. It seemed absurd now, an ugly, rusted beetle from some forgotten nightmare, a clumsy toy made by frightened children who did not know how to use their hands for anything better. He did not pick it up.

Ten feet away, Nettie stirred. She pushed her hair back from her forehead and blinked at the dazzling sun. Verrall sat up beside her, rubbing his eyes, looking bewildered and strangely young without his pride.

William stood and walked over to them. Neither flinched. When Nettie looked up at him, her eyes were clear as spring water, stripped of fear, stripped of the old, tangled guilt. William reached out his hand—the soot-stained hand of a Staffordshire collier—and helped her to her feet, then offered the same hand to Verrall.

"Are you cold?" William asked, and his voice sounded new in his own ears, gentle and unhurried.

"No," Verrall said softly, looking out toward the horizon where three great grey warships sat motionless upon the glittering sea, their smokestacks cold, their guns silent under the clean morning sun. "No, I think the winter is over."
