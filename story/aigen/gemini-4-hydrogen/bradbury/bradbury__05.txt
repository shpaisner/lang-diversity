In Staffordshire, even the rain tasted of coal. It fell grey upon the red-brick rows, hissed against the hot bellies of the pottery kilns, and left black crescents beneath William Leadford’s fingernails. He walked home from the clerk’s desk with his collar turned up and his heart turned down, counting the pennies in his pocket like cold flat stones. Above the belching chimneys, where the smoke usually murdered the stars, something new was burning. It hung in the northern sky—a pale, emerald ember that had no business being there. The comet had grown each night since July, a luminous thistle-seed drifting closer to the earth’s dark orchard. Men stood in the doorways of the public houses, pints forgotten in their hands, staring up at the green wanderer. They spoke in low, dry voices of Serbia, of dreadnoughts sliding into the North Sea, of young men soon to be fed to the iron mouths of cannons. Old women crossed themselves, muttering of plagues and kings’ deaths, while the newspapers screamed in thick black type of ultimatums and marching boots. Europe was a dry hayrick waiting for a match. William scarcely heard them. He walked through the clamor as if through tall grass, his eyes fixed on the little cottage window where Nettie’s lamp would soon be lit. Nettie, whose laugh was a bright silver bell ringing through the soot of Clayton; Nettie, whose skin smelled of sweet-briar soap and summer evenings. Tonight, he told himself, he would ask her to walk out with him past the slag heaps, where the grass still grew stubborn and green. Tonight, he would put his small, ink-stained life into her hands and pray she would not drop it.

The lamp was not lit. Only a cold draft waited in the parlor, and Nettie’s mother weeping into a coarse apron, her shoulders shaking like autumn leaves. Nettie had gone. Gone with Edward Verrall—Verrall of the big house on the hill, whose father owned half the clay pits and whose motorcar coughed blue smoke into the faces of walking men. Gone to the coast, they said, with her Sunday hat and a leather portmanteau, leaving behind only a scrap of paper folded small as a moth. William did not weep. The grief in him turned instantly to slag, heavy and molten in his ribs. He walked back down the crooked street where the green starlight now painted the puddles the color of sour apples. In a cramped shop smelling of camphor and old brass, he laid down three weeks' wages for a revolver. It was small, blued steel, cold as a winter frog in his palm, its six chambers silent and patient. He walked out into the sulphurous dark, the iron weight pulling at his coat pocket, balancing the madness in his head. Why should Verrall have the cream and the roses, while William ate the cinders? Why should the rich steal the only bright thing in a poor man’s sky? Above him, the comet spread a faint, ghostly fan across the zenith, watching him pack his carpetbag, watching him lock his door, watching him step out toward the railway station with murder ticking quietly against his thigh.

The train south was full of boys in khaki. They leaned from the windows, red-faced and shouting, singing music-hall songs that rattled against the glass like hailstones. War had been declared—or would be by morning—and the whole island was shaking like a fevered dog. Newspapers rustled in every carriage, black headlines shouting of mobilizing fleets and burning borders, passed from rough hand to rough hand. William sat wedged in the corner of a third-class compartment, his knees pressed against a soldier’s kitbag, his hand buried deep in his coat pocket. His fingers traced the checkered walnut grip, over and over, a blind man reading a dark gospel. Through the soot-streaked window, the night had changed. The comet was no longer a star; it was a vast, luminous peacock’s tail sweeping across half the heavens, bathing the flying hedgerows and sleeping villages in a weird, underwater glow. The soldiers laughed at it, raising their flasks to the green fire, calling it a lucky sign for the killing to come. William did not laugh. He stared at the pale reflections in the glass and saw only Nettie’s white throat, Verrall’s easy, gentleman’s smile, and the small black hole the bullet would make in the world’s silence. The wheels clacked beneath him: Find them, kill them, find them, kill them. The iron rhythm carried him down toward the sea, while above the rushing steam, the green sky leaned closer, listening.

Shaphambury smelled of salt and wet shingle, a clean, sharp wind that fought the Staffordshire coal dust clinging to William’s woolens. He found them at dusk, walking along the low stone wall above the tide. Nettie wore white muslin that caught the eerie emerald light from the sky; Verrall walked beside her, one hand resting lightly on her elbow, leaning down to catch her words as if they were pearls. Something snapped behind William’s eyes, hot and bright as a snapped wire. He followed them down the chalk steps to the empty beach, where the surf hissed like cooling iron. "Nettie!" His voice tore out of him, raw and ugly. They turned together, two pale shapes against the dark sea. Verrall stepped half in front of her, his face hardening in the green twilight. William drew the revolver. The steel felt immense, a thunderbolt in his fist. He raised it, his thumb drawing back the hammer with a sharp, clean click. Nettie’s hands flew to her mouth. Then the sky broke open. There was no sound, no thunder—only a vast, silent sighing in the upper air, as if a million miles of silk had been torn. A green mist, luminous and cool, poured down from the stars and rolled across the water, swallowing the cliffs, swallowing the town, swallowing the three of them. It smelled of crushed mint, of wet clover, of a summer morning before the factories were built. William tried to pull the trigger, but his fingers forgot the shape of hate. His knees buckled softly into the shingle. The pistol slipped from his hand, and the world went sweetly, deeply dark.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world had been washed clean. It was not the ordinary light of August; it was clearer, sharper, as if an unseen hand had wiped a thousand years of soot from the great glass dome of the sky. He lay upon the damp pebbles, his cheek pressed against a tuft of sea-thrift. Beside his hand lay the revolver, looking absurd and small—a dead black beetle, a silly scrap of clockwork from a forgotten nightmare. He picked it up, felt its cold, stupid weight, and tossed it into the gentle surf. It sank without a ripple of regret. William stood up and brushed the sand from his trousers. His head felt wonderfully light, emptied of the old buzzing flies that had lived there all his life: the sour envy, the bruised pride, the bitter arithmetic of rich and poor, master and man. He breathed in, deep and slow, and it was as if he had never truly breathed before, as if all his twenty-odd years in Staffordshire had been spent holding his breath underwater. Far up on the cliff, a church bell began to ring—not an alarm, not a tolling for war, but a clear, wandering music. He knew, with a sudden, quiet certainty, that across the narrow sea the cannons were silent, that boys in khaki and boys in grey were blinking at one another across the trenches like children waking from a bad dream. The green mist had burned away the fever.

He walked slowly up the shingle toward the stone sea-wall, where two figures sat side by side in the morning sun. Nettie and Verrall looked up as his shadow fell across the warm granite. There was no terror in Nettie’s wide eyes, and no arrogance left in Verrall’s pale face; they looked at William as if he were a brother who had been long on a journey. William sat down on the wall a little way from them, letting his boots dangle over the stones. For a long time, none of them spoke. They simply listened to the gulls crying cleanly over the blue water and watched the white sails of a fishing smack leaning into the wind. When Nettie finally spoke his name, it sounded new, stripped of all its old weight of guilt and desperation. William looked at her—at the way the sunlight caught the loose strands of her brown hair—and found that he loved her still. Yet the love had changed its shape. It was no longer a cage he wished to build around her, nor a prize to be wrested from another man’s hands. It was open as the sky, gentle as the tide returning to the shore. Verrall reached out, hesitated, and then laid a quiet hand upon William’s shoulder, a simple touch between equals in a brand-new hour. Together, the three of them turned their faces toward the east, where the air shimmered like pure glass, waiting to see what kind of world men would build when they no longer hated in the dark.
