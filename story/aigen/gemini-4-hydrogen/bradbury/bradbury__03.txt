Staffordshire was a black lung coughing sparks into the summer night. William Leadford stood in the cinder-yard behind his mother’s cottage, his knuckles stained with ink and coal-dust, staring up at the sky. There, suspended above the smoking chimneys of the pottery kilns, hung a lantern of pale, unearthly green. It had been a mere pinprick in May, a cool splinter of ice in the velvet dark. Now, in August, it was a vast, luminous bruise spreading across the heavens, casting a faint emerald sheen over the slate roofs and the slag heaps.

William’s heart was a small, hot fist beating against his ribs. Nettie was gone. She had slipped away like water through his rough, workingman’s fingers, leaving behind only the ghost of her lavender scent and a crumpled note on the kitchen table. She had gone with Verrall. Edward Verrall, with his soft hands that had never known a blister, his cream-colored flannels, his motorcar that smelled of polished leather and money. 

Around William, the Four Towns muttered in their sleep. Men in the pubs spoke of nothing but the German fleet and the ultimatums flying like poisoned arrows across the Channel. The newspapers were black with headlines, screaming of mobilization, of dreadnoughts steaming into the North Sea, of a world poised on the edge of a furnace. William did not care about the Kaiser or the Empire. His war was smaller, sharper, and smelled of lavender. He looked at the green star, and the green star looked back, cold and bright as a blade.

The ironmonger’s shop smelled of machine oil, wet hemp, and ancient rust. William laid his last silver shillings upon the scarred wooden counter, his fingers trembling slightly, not from fear, but from a terrible, crystalline clarity. The revolver was small and dull, like a dead beetle. He slipped it into the pocket of his frayed tweed coat, where it weighed him down like a stone dropped into a deep well.

All that afternoon, he walked the dusty lanes toward the station, his boots white with chalk. The heat was unnatural, thick and coppery, as though the air itself were waiting for a match to be struck. In the hedgerows, the birds had stopped singing; they sat hunched among the hawthorn leaves, watching the sky with bead-black eyes. Even at noon, the comet was visible now, a ghostly crescent of verdigris hanging beside the pale moon.

He thought of Nettie’s laugh, bright as a struck bell. He thought of Verrall’s voice, smooth as poured cream, speaking of books William could never afford to buy and places he would never see. In William’s mind, the two of them were always walking through some sunlit meadow, while he himself remained trapped in the soot-stained alleys of Clayton, breathing ash. Why should the rich have the light, and the poor have only the dark? The revolver knocked gently against his hip with every stride, keeping time with the pulse in his throat. If he could not have Nettie, then neither should Verrall, nor the world, nor the green star watching from above.

The train rattled south like a tin kettle on a boil. At every station, boys ran along the platforms like startled crows, waving damp newspapers and shouting in shrill, breaking voices. War! War declared! The fleet has sailed! Men leaned from the carriage windows, their faces flushed with ale and sudden, terrible pride, snatching the papers from the boys’ hands. They sang old marching songs, their voices rough and out of tune, while the countryside slid past in a blur of golden wheat and dark elms.

William sat in the corner, his hat pulled low, his hand resting inside his coat pocket. He watched the strangers around him, marveling at how eager they were to die, how hungry for the thunder of guns. They were all mad, he thought, infected by the same fever that burned behind his own eyes. The air in the compartment was stifling, thick with tobacco smoke and the sweat of excited men.

By dusk, he had reached the coast. He walked the final miles along the cliff path toward Shaphambury, where the sea lay flat and metallic beneath a sky that had turned the color of bruised plums. Ahead of him, the comet had grown monstrous, its great feathered tail sweeping halfway across the zenith, bathing the white chalk cliffs in a strange, phosphorescent twilight. Far out on the dark horizon, silent flashes of orange light bloomed and died, followed seconds later by a low, trembling rumble that shook the soles of his boots. The war had begun. Somewhere out there in the dark, iron ships were tearing each other apart, and men were drowning in the cold salt water. William did not pause. He saw the lights of the little seaside bungalow glowing yellow through the tamarisk trees.

He found them in the garden above the beach, where the tall grasses whispered against the wooden fence. They were standing close together, looking out toward the sea and the distant, flickering thunder of the naval guns. Nettie wore a white dress that caught the green radiance of the sky, making her look like a phantom risen from the foam. Verrall stood beside her, one arm draped lightly over her shoulders, his face pale and upturned.

William stepped through the gate. The latch clicked, sharp as a cocked hammer. Nettie turned, and her hand flew to her mouth.

"Will," she whispered.

Verrall stepped forward, instinctively placing himself between the girl and the dark figure in the tweed coat. "Leadford? What are you doing here?" His voice was steady, though his eyes darted to William’s right hand, which was emerging slowly from his pocket.

"She’s mine, Verrall," William said. His voice sounded hollow, as if it were coming from the bottom of a dry well. He raised the revolver. The dull metal caught a spark of green fire from the heavens. "You took everything else. The clean air, the quiet, the money. You weren't content with that. You had to take her, too."

Nettie cried out, a small, broken sound, and clutched at Verrall’s sleeve. Out at sea, a great red flare rose into the night, illuminating the undersides of the clouds like blood on cotton. William’s finger tightened on the trigger. He felt a vast, cold emptiness inside him, a pit that only the roar of gunpowder could fill. He aimed at the center of Verrall’s white shirt.

Then the sky broke open, not with fire, but with a sound like a million silk curtains tearing in a gentle wind.

A vast, luminous wave of green mist came sweeping in from the sea, rolling over the chalk cliffs, swallowing the dark water, racing across the tamarisk trees. It was not smoke; it carried no scent of sulfur or burning coal. As it washed over William, he gasped, dropping the revolver into the tall grass. The air tasted of crushed mint, of summer rain on hot pavement, of the clean, sharp scent of pine needles in a forgotten wood.

It was cold, wonderfully cold, like diving into a clear mountain stream after a lifetime in a furnace. William’s knees buckled. Beside him, he heard Nettie sigh—not a cry of terror, but a soft, long exhalation, like a child sinking into a feather bed. Verrall swayed, reached out blindly for the fence, and slid gently down into the clover.

William fell to his hands and knees. The green vapor swirled around him, thick and soft as river fog, filling his lungs, washing through the dark, soot-clogged chambers of his heart. Above him, the comet’s tail seemed to drape the entire world in a veil of emerald light. Far away, out on the black sea, the orange flashes sputtered and went out. The booming of the guns ceased, swallowed by a silence so deep and sweet that William could hear the tiny rustle of a beetle moving through the grass beside his cheek. His eyelids grew heavy. The rage, the poverty, the bitter ache of his own smallness—all of it dissolved like salt in warm water. He laid his head upon his arm and slept.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was a golden apricot rolling up the eastern sky. The green mist was gone, and with it, the heavy, coppery heat of the old world. The air was as clear and bright as spring water.

He sat up slowly, blinking in the pure morning light. His coat felt strange, shabby and absurd, like a costume worn in a dream he could no longer quite remember. A few feet away, lying in the tall grass, was the small iron revolver. William looked at it for a long time. It seemed to him the ugliest, most pathetic thing he had ever seen—a little lump of cold metal shaped by frightened men to solve problems that no longer existed. He picked it up, walked to the edge of the cliff, and tossed it out over the water. It made a tiny, distant splash, and was gone.

Behind him, Nettie stirred. She sat up, brushing bits of dry grass from her white dress, and looked at him with wide, clear eyes. There was no fear in her face now, only a quiet, wondering tenderness. Beside her, Verrall was waking too, rubbing his forehead, looking out at the empty, peaceful sea where no ships burned.

William walked back to them, his boots crushing the sweet-smelling clover. He knelt beside Nettie and took her hand, and when Verrall reached out to steady her, William took his hand as well. They sat together in the morning sun, three small, fragile creatures beneath a vast and washed-clean sky, listening to the gulls wheeling overhead and the gentle, rhythmic breathing of the sea.
