In Staffordshire, even the rain tasted of coal. William Leadford stood at his narrow window, watching the bottle-kilns of the Potteries breathe their orange soot into a bruised autumn sky. Behind him, his mother’s kettle hissed like an angry goose, and on the oilcloth table lay the Sentinel, its black headlines screaming of dreadnoughts steaming into the North Sea, of Europe sliding like a greased coffin toward the pit.

William did not care about the Kaiser. His war was smaller, sharper, and lived behind his ribs.

Two weeks ago, Nettie had gone. She had taken her Sunday hat, her quiet smile, and the warmth from the kitchen, and stepped into Edward Verrall’s sleek motor-car—all brass lamps and calfskin upholstery—leaving William with ink-stained fingers and twelve shillings a week.

He pressed his forehead to the cold glass. Above the belching chimneys, past the grime that clung to every brick, the sky had changed. Hanging just above the horizon was the comet. It had grown since Tuesday into a pale, luminous smear, the color of pond-ice or a cat’s eye in a cellar. A green star, trailing ghostly dust across the constellations.

Down in the street, drunken boys marched past, singing about hanging the Germans from a sour apple tree. Their boots rang on the cobbles like hammers on an anvil. William watched them go, his breath fogging the pane. Let the world burn, he thought. Let the iron ships sink in the dark. Tonight, he had a train to catch, and a small, cold piece of iron waiting in his pocket.

The revolver had cost him his silver watch and three weeks of dinners, a heavy, snub-nosed thing that smelled of gun-oil and pawnshop velvet. All the way down from the Midlands, through the clattering dark of the third-class carriage, William kept his hand curled around it. The train rattled a furious rhythm: Verrall, Verrall, kill them both.

He closed his eyes and saw Nettie as she had been in July, walking through the dusty lanes beyond Burslem, her cotton dress brushing the cow-parsley. She had smelled of lavender soap and warm bread. How easily Verrall had plucked her away, with his soft voice from the big house on the hill, his effortless way of tossing half-crowns to beggars, his clean fingernails. What did a rich man know of counting pennies for tallow candles while the damp crept up the wallpaper?

Across the compartment, two territorial soldiers slept against the wooden slats, rifles cradled between their knees like awkward brides. The oil-lamp swung overhead, casting long shadows across their smooth faces. They were going to France to be fed into cannons; William was going to the Sussex coast to settle an account in blood. Both journeys seemed entirely natural, inevitable as winter.

When he stepped onto the platform at Shaphambury, the salt wind hit his face, sharp and clean after the sulfur of Staffordshire. Above the tiled roofs, the comet now filled a quarter of the heavens, a vast plume of emerald fire. The gaslights along the esplanade looked yellow and sickly beneath its cold, unearthly glow.

He walked the chalk road out of town, past shuttered boarding houses and gardens where late roses hung heavy with dew. Even here, at the edge of the sea, the madness of Europe beat like a pulse in the dark. Down by the railway siding, searchlights swept the clouds, white fingers groping for airships. Far out across the black Channel, a dull, subterranean thudding shook the soles of William’s boots—great naval guns testing their throats in the night.

William did not flinch. The green light of the comet lay over the downs like spilled paint, turning the white cliffs the color of verdigris, turning his own knuckles pale and spectral as he walked.

He found the bungalow just as the letter he had stolen from Nettie’s cousin described it: a low, timbered place perched above the shingle, with a wide veranda facing the sea. Warm amber light spilled from the French windows, pushing back the eerie emerald of the sky.

William crouched behind a wind-bent tamarisk hedge, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. Through the glass, he saw them. Verrall sat in a wicker chair, a glass of something golden in his hand, laughing at some small jest. And there was Nettie, kneeling by the hearth, poking at a driftwood fire. The flames caught the copper in her hair, the soft curve of her cheek. She looked up at Verrall with the exact smile she had once given William beneath the summer elms.

A red mist rose behind William’s eyes, hotter than the Staffordshire kilns. He drew the revolver, stepped onto the gravel path, and heard the stones crunch loud as pistol shots beneath his heels.

The French windows swung open. Verrall stepped onto the veranda first, peering into the luminous dark, his white shirtfront glowing faintly green. Nettie followed, a shawl drawn tight around her narrow shoulders.

"Who's there?" Verrall called, his voice carrying that effortless authority William hated more than anything on earth.

"William!" Nettie gasped, her hand flying to her throat.

He walked up the wooden steps, the gun held low, his chest heaving. Up close, Verrall looked younger than he had in the Potteries, almost fragile in the comet’s wash, though his jaw was set.

"Put that away, Leadford," Verrall said quietly, stepping half in front of Nettie. "Don't be a fool."

"A fool?" William laughed, a dry sound like ripping canvas. "You take everything, you people. You take the air, the coal, the bread from our mouths, and then you come down to the lanes and take the only thing a poor man has left to keep him warm."

"Will, please," Nettie whispered. Tears shone on her lashes, green as sea-glass. "I couldn't breathe there, Will. The smoke—the endless dark—"

"So you came to his house to breathe?" William raised the heavy barrel. It trembled, then steadied, pointing straight at Verrall’s white shirt. "Look at the sky. The whole world is going to hell tonight. Why should you two stay behind in your warm little room?"

He curled his finger around the trigger. The hammer clicked back—a tiny, sharp mechanical tooth snapping in the quiet. Verrall did not move; he only stared down the barrel with grave eyes. Nettie cried out, burying her face against Verrall’s sleeve.

And then, before William could squeeze the iron, the wind died.

It was as if the entire earth had suddenly held its breath. The distant booming of the Channel guns ceased. The surf stopped hissing on the shingle, hanging frozen in a long, glassy curl.

Above them, the green comet seemed to swell, rushing downward until its trailing veil brushed the stars out of existence. A sound began—not thunder, but a vast, velvety sigh, like a million pages turning in a dark library, or summer wind passing through high wheat.

William tried to pull the trigger, but his arm felt heavy as lead pipe. A strange scent drifted across the veranda. It smelled of crushed mint, of ozone after a July storm, of apples ripening in a forgotten orchard. A pale green mist, luminous and cool as spring water, began to settle over the tamarisk bushes, over the shingle, over the three of them standing frozen on the porch.

"What is it?" Nettie murmured, her voice dreamy, the terror draining from it like water from a cracked cup.

William looked up. The air itself was turning into a gentle, glowing emerald snow, drifting into his lungs, washing away the old soot of Staffordshire, cooling the furnace behind his eyes. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and clattered onto the floorboards.

He saw Verrall sway, reaching out not to strike, but to steady Nettie as she sank gracefully to her knees. William’s own legs folded beneath him. He fell onto the rough timber, watching the green vapor swirl around them like a kindly tide. The last thing he heard before the great, sweet sleep took him was the distant chime of a town clock striking four, and then nothing—only light, and silence, and the turning of the world.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world had been washed to the bone.

He lay on the veranda, yet the wood beneath his cheek felt warm and smooth as polished cedar. Above him, the sky was an astonishing, cloudless blue, deeper and clearer than any sky he had ever seen. The air he drew into his lungs tasted like sweet well-water.

He sat up slowly. A few feet away, the revolver lay on the planks, looking absurd and small, a meaningless lump of rusted iron, like a child’s discarded toy. William stared at it, waiting for the familiar knot of rage to tighten in his stomach, waiting for the cold poison of pride and jealousy to rush back into his veins.

Nothing came. Only a vast, quiet wonder, as if a heavy woollen blindfold had been peeled from his eyes.

Nearby, Nettie stirred, pushing back her hair. Across from her, Edward Verrall sat up, blinking in the golden light. When Verrall saw William, he did not flinch. He looked at him with a sudden, profound recognition, as if they were two brothers who had woken from the same long, feverish nightmare.

"Leadford," Verrall said softly. His voice had lost its brittle edge; it sounded merely human, warm and bewildered. "Are you hurt?"

"No," William said, his own voice clear as a bell. He looked at Nettie, watching them both with shining eyes, free of fear. He still loved her, yet the hunger to own her, to lock her in a cage of his own misery, had vanished with the mist.

"Look at the sea," Nettie whispered.

They stood together at the railing. Below them, the Channel lay flat and silver, empty of ironclads and gunsmoke, while along the bright shingle a lone fisherman paused in marvel at the pebbles beneath his feet, as though seeing the earth on the first morning of creation.
