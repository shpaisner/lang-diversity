In Staffordshire, the sky was always the color of an old iron skillet, greased with coal-smoke and scarred by the orange fires of the bottle kilns. William Leadford walked the cinder paths with his hands shoved deep into his threadbare coat, his knuckles raw, his heart a tight, blackened fist. In his right pocket lay a five-shilling revolver, cold as a river stone.

Everywhere, the brick chimneys breathed soot into the twilight, as if the earth itself were burning slowly from the inside out. William hated the grime in his fingernails, the thinness of his mother’s soup, the endless, grinding clatter of the potteries. Most of all, he hated the quiet parlor where Nettie used to sit, before she vanished like summer rain down a dry well.

She had gone with Verrall. Verrall, with his soft hands, his motorcar that smelled of polished leather and petrol, his laugh that belonged to sunlit lawns and tennis courts.

William stopped by the canal, where the water lay thick and oily as ink. Above the jagged silhouettes of the slag heaps, the evening was changing. A strange star had come to visit the world. Night after night it had grown, until now it hung in the east like a pale green dandelion clock, shedding a faint, spectral dust across the heavens. People whispered in the public houses that it was an omen. William didn’t care for omens. He only knew that the air tasted of sulfur, and that somewhere to the south, Nettie was laughing in another man’s arms.

By the time William boarded the third-class carriage for the coast, the whole country had caught a fever. At every station, boys in peaked caps ran along the platforms waving damp newspapers, their voices cracking like dry twigs. War! Germany! The Fleet sails at dawn!

Men leaned from the carriage windows, their faces flushed with ale and sudden, terrible importance, shouting about battleships and ironclads, about teaching the Kaiser a lesson before the leaves fell. The train wheels hammered the rails—kill-them, kill-them, kill-them—a metallic pulse that matched the drumming in William’s temples.

He stared out at the passing hedgerows, blurred by speed and twilight. Europe was drifting toward a waterfall, and no one wanted to drop the oars. He felt a grim, dark kinship with the angry crowds. Let the world burn, he thought. Let the great guns roar in the North Sea. His own private war was tucked against his ribs, heavy and small and made of blued steel.

He closed his eyes and tried to summon Nettie’s face, not as she looked when she lied to him at the garden gate, but as she had been in July, walking through the high meadow grass near Clayton. Her straw hat had swung from her wrist by a blue ribbon, and the sun had turned the fine hairs on her neck to spun brass. She had smelled of sweet clover then, before the petrol fumes and the fine silk stockings came to steal her away. When William opened his eyes, the green comet was watching him through the soot-streaked glass, brighter than the moon.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, wet kelp, and pine needles—a sharp, clean shock after the choked lungs of the Midlands. William walked down from the station through streets of whitewashed cottages and shuttered tea-rooms. The seaside town seemed to be holding its breath. Even the gulls had stopped their wheeling, perched silently along the ridgepoles like carved ivory birds.

Night was pooling in the hollows of the sand dunes. Above the English Channel, the comet had swollen into a vast, luminous fan of emerald fire, brushing the zenith. It cast no warmth, only a weird, underwater glow that turned the chalk cliffs to jade and made the rolling surf look like liquid glass. Out on the dark horizon, the searchlights of the battleships swept back and forth, nervous white fingers poking at the sky.

William followed the boardwalk until he reached the lonely bungalows tucked behind the marram grass. He had spent his last shillings tracking the motorcar here, asking questions of ostlers and innkeepers with a tight, polite smile that hurt his jaw.

There, in the third bungalow from the sea, a yellow oil lamp burned behind lace curtains. Shadows moved across the linen—a tall shadow with broad shoulders, and a smaller one that tilted its head in a way William knew better than the lines on his own palm. His breath came in shallow, ragged gasps. He stepped off the boards into the soft, shifting sand, crouching behind a clump of sea-holly, and waited for the door to open.

The door clicked open near midnight, spilling a wedge of warm amber light onto the porch. Nettie stepped out first, a dark shawl pulled tight around her shoulders, followed by Verrall in his pale flannel suit. They walked down toward the water’s edge, their voices low and murmuring against the hush of the tide.

"Look at it, Edward," Nettie said softly, tilting her face toward the green sky. "It’s like the whole world is inside a bottle."

William rose from the dunes like a ghost stepping out of a grave. His boots crunched on the dry shells. "Nettie," he called.

She spun around with a sharp little cry. Verrall stepped instantly in front of her, his jaw setting hard in the emerald light. "Leadford? What the devil are you doing here?"

"I came for what’s mine," William said, though his voice sounded thin and far away, swallowed by the vastness of the ocean. All the rehearsed speeches—about the poor man’s sweat, the rich man’s theft, the soot in his throat—turned to dry ash in his mouth. He only knew the terrible, hollow ache in his chest. He pulled the revolver from his coat.

"William, no!" Nettie’s hand flew to her mouth.

"Stand aside, Verrall," William whispered.

Verrall didn’t move. Instead, he lunged forward, his arm raised. William’s finger jerked on the cold trigger. A flat, ugly crack shattered the seaside quiet, sending a flock of unseen sandpipers whirring into the night. The bullet whined harmlessly into the dark surf. William raised the barrel again, sobbing with rage and shame—and then the wind died.

It did not fall like rain; it rolled in from the sea like a silent, emerald tide. A luminous mist, thick as spun silk, brushed across William’s cheek. He waited for the choke of sulfur, for the sting of poison, but when he drew a breath, it tasted of crushed mint, of cold mountain springs, of summer lightning after a long drought.

The revolver suddenly felt absurdly heavy, like a lump of lead tied to a drowning man. William’s arm dropped to his side.

Around him, the world was slowing down, like an old brass clock running out of tension. The searchlights out in the Channel flickered and went out. The booming of the surf softened to a gentle sigh. He looked at Verrall, who stood frozen, his hand still half-raised, his eyes wide and vacant as he stared into the swirling green fog. Nettie had sunk to her knees on the damp sand, her shawl slipping from her shoulders, watching the mist curl around her fingers like friendly kittens.

Inside William’s chest, the hot coal of his anger hissed once and went entirely cold. A strange, overwhelming tenderness washed through his veins, loosening the tight knots in his shoulders, smoothing the creases from his brow. He didn’t hate Verrall. He didn’t even ache for Nettie. He only felt an immense, sleepy pity for all of them—for the boys shouting on the train platforms, for the men stoking the Staffordshire kilns, for himself, running through the dark with a piece of iron in his hand.

His knees buckled softly. The gun slipped from his open palm into the sand, and William Leadford lay down beneath the green stars and slept.

When William woke, the sun was up, and the sky was a blue so pure and deep it felt as though someone had washed the world with rainwater and hung it out to dry.

He sat up, brushing fine white sand from his coat. His head was astonishingly clear. For the first time in twenty-four years, there was no dull weight behind his eyes, no sour taste of coal-smoke in his throat. He looked down at the five-shilling revolver half-buried beside his boot. It looked small and foolish, a rusted relic from a dream he could barely remember. He picked it up by the barrel and tossed it into the retreating tide, where a silver wave swallowed it without a sound.

Ten yards away, Verrall was sitting up, blinking in the bright morning. Beside him, Nettie stirred, pushing her dark hair back from her forehead. She looked at the ocean, at the cloudless sky, and then at William. There was no fear in her eyes, and no triumph in Verrall’s.

William stood and walked across the wet sand toward them. The breeze carried the scent of pine and salt, clean and sharp. He held out his hand to help Nettie to her feet, as naturally as if they were children waking after a long afternoon in the clover.

"William," she said, and her voice held a quiet wonder. "What happened to the sky?"

"It changed," William said, looking out toward the empty horizon where the warships had been. Somewhere far inland, the bottle kilns were cold, and the war had been forgotten like an old coat left on a chair. "And I think we’ve changed with it."

Verrall stood beside them, nodding slowly, squinting into the brilliant, boundless day. Together, they turned their faces toward the sun, waiting to see what the new world would ask of them.
