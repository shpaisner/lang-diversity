In Clayton, where the potteries smoke from dawn to dark and the sky above the marl-pits is the colour of a dirty dishcloth, William Leadford sat at his high desk and hated the world. He was nineteen, thin-chested, and poorly fed on tea and dripping; his cuffs were frayed to the thread, and his boots let in the Staffordshire mire. Outside the grimy counting-house window, the blast-furnaces of the Black Country belched their red-and-amber glare against a heaven that had gone strangely green. For three weeks the Great Comet had hung above the pit-heads, growing nightly from a pale pinprick to a broad, emerald-tinted besom that swept half the stars aside.

The men in the yard spoke of nothing else, save the war. Newsboys ran hoarse down the cobbled lanes, crying the German ultimatum and the calling-up of the Reserves. The Empire was buckling on its belt, and the beer-shops rang with loud, foolish songs. Leadford cared nothing for the Kaiser or the Fleet. In his waistcoat pocket lay a crumpled sheet of cheap notepaper, smelling faintly of lavender and treachery. Nettie had written it from the junction at Crewe: she was sorry, she said, and she could not bear the soot and the waiting any longer, and she had gone south with Mr. Edward Verrall.

Verrall was the ironmaster’s son. He had broad shoulders, a motor-car that stank of burnt oil, and a way of laughing with his chin tilted up. Leadford knew where they had gone—Nettie’s foolish, vain sister had let slip the name of Shaphambury-on-Sea. That night, while his mother coughed her thin, patient cough in the back kitchen, Leadford counted his savings on the oilcloth table: four pounds, seven shillings, and a handful of coppers. It was enough for a third-class ticket, and it was enough for lead.

He bought the revolver in a dim little pawnshop by the Checkshill canal, where the water lay thick as soup under the gas-lamps. It was a stubby Belgian bulldog, nickelled once, now dull with grease and pocket-lint. The pawnbroker, a deaf old man with a green eye-shade, wrapped it in brown paper and took Leadford’s thirty shillings without a blink. Half the county was buying iron that week; the papers said the North Sea was thick with torpedo-boats, and a man might need to guard his own hearth. Leadford slipped the heavy, cold lump into his coat pocket and walked to the station through a drizzle of warm, soot-laden rain.

The London train was packed to the roof-boards with reservists. They smelled of wet serge, shag tobacco, and cheap bitter; they leaned out of the windows to kiss weeping girls and shout at the platform porters. When the train pulled clear of the Midlands and ran south through the darkened shires, they sang "Soldiers of the Queen" until the lamps rattled in their sockets. Leadford sat wedged in a corner by the door, his knees pressed against a corporal’s kit-bag, his right hand buried deep in his pocket.

He despised them all. He despised their red, beef-fed faces, their noisy loyalty to a King who would never know their names, and the great, blind machine of steel and coal that was grinding them toward the guns. Most of all, he despised himself—the pale clerk with ink on his knuckles, whose girl had been bought away by a pair of flannel trousers and a handful of sovereigns. Above the racing chimney-smoke of the locomotive, the Comet peered through the carriage window, vast and cold and green, like the eye of a cat watching a rat-hole.

Shaphambury was another world, washed clean by the Channel wind. Here were no slag-heaps, no clanging shunting-yards, only white chalk cliffs and yellow gorse tumbling down to a grey-green sea. Leadford walked the cliff-path in his dusty black coat, his boots white with chalk-marl, looking like a stray crow among the gulls. Out beyond the headland, five miles off shore, a line of grey cruisers steamed without lights, dark and purposeful as sharks. Now and then a white searchlight beam leaped from a fighting-top, swept the empty horizon, and snapped shut again. The war had begun in earnest; a coastguard told him, over a wall of tarred flint, that the Germans had shelled a fishing-fleet off the Dogger Bank at noon.

Leadford nodded and walked on toward the red-roofed bungalows scattered along the western dunes. He did not care if the Germans landed at dusk. His eyes were fixed on the shingle path below the cliffs, where the tide hissed among the pebbles.

He saw them an hour before sundown. Verrall was in white flannels and a blue blazer, swinging a cane with the easy, insolent grace of a man who has never added up a ledger in his life. Beside him walked Nettie, in her Sunday serge skirt and a straw hat trimmed with cornflowers. She was laughing—that quick, breathless little laugh Leadford knew better than his own heartbeat—and she had her hand tucked through the crook of Verrall’s arm. They turned up the narrow path toward the dunes, where the gorse grew tall as a man’s shoulder, and the shadow of the great green star began to fall across the grass.

Night dropped, though there was no darkness in it. The Comet filled half the southern sky now, a roaring fan of emerald fire that turned the white chalk the colour of verdigris and made the sea look like molten bottle-glass. Out in the Channel, something flashed—a quick, orange wink on the horizon—followed ten seconds later by a dull, heavy thump that rattled the pebbles under Leadford’s soles. Then another, and another, until the night air trembled with the steady, distant drumming of twelve-inch guns.

Leadford stepped out from behind a clump of wind-bitten tamarisk on the cliff-path. They were twenty paces away, standing by the whitewashed stones of the coastguard’s mark, looking out toward the flashes at sea.

"Nettie," Leadford said. His voice was cracked and dry as a dead reed.

She spun round with a little gasp, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall did not flinch; whatever else he was, the ironmaster’s son had the nerve of his breed. He stepped smoothly between the girl and the clerk, his shoulders squared, his cane held ready.

"What the devil are you doing here, Leadford?" he said, sharp and level. "Go home. You’ve no business here."

"I’ve business with you," Leadford whispered. He pulled the Belgian revolver from his pocket. The nickel gleamed sickly green in the comet-light. "And with her."

Nettie cried out—"Willie, don’t!"—and clutched at Verrall’s sleeve. Verrall struck her hand gently aside and took a single pace forward, his eyes fixed on the muzzle. Leadford’s finger tightened on the heavy trigger. A red mist seemed to rise behind his eyes, hotter than the Clayton furnaces. He fired. The bark of the little gun sounded thin and foolish against the booming of the fleet. The bullet whined off a flintstone at Verrall’s feet. Leadford cocked the hammer for the second shot—and then a strange, sweet wind blew in across the dunes.

It was not the salt breath of the Channel. It was a heavy, luminous green mist that rolled over the cliff-edge like a breaking wave, smelling faintly of crushed mint and clean rain after drought. Where it touched the gorse, the yellow blossoms seemed to glow like little lamps.

Out at sea, the thunder of the twelve-inch guns stopped mid-salvo, as though a giant hand had been laid across the muzzles.

Verrall took half a step, blinked hard, and went down on his knees in the chalk-dust without a sound. Beside him, Nettie sank slowly onto the turf, her straw hat rolling away into the grass, her eyes already closed as if in the deep, dreamless sleep of a tired child.

Leadford tried to hate them. He tried to hold the hot, black knot of his rage together in his chest, tried to lift the iron in his hand and finish what he had come to do. His arm felt as heavy as a pig of Staffordshire iron. He drew in a lungful of the green vapour—cool, sweet, and piercing clean—and all the soot and bitterness in his narrow chest seemed to dissolve like smoke in a gale. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and thudded softly into the turf. He stumbled forward two paces and fell at full length beside the path, his cheek pressed against the cool chalk, watching the great green star dissolve into a sea of merciful, bottomless dark.

When he woke, the sun was up, and the sky was a pale, washed azure such as he had never seen in all his nineteen years. The Comet was gone—shrunk to a faint, harmless ghost of vapour high in the west. A lark was singing somewhere above the downs, its notes dropping clear as water-beads through the still air.

Leadford sat up and looked at his hands. They were thin and ink-stained still, yet he felt no shame in them, nor any anger at the world that had made them so. Beside his knee lay the Belgian revolver, wet with morning dew—a silly, ugly little toy of iron and brass. He kicked it gently aside into the grass, wondering dimly what madness had made him carry it three hundred miles.

A few yards off, Verrall stirred, propped himself on an elbow, and looked about him with wide, bewildered eyes. His white flannels were stained with chalk and grass. Near him, Nettie sat up slowly, pushing the hair back from her forehead. She looked at Verrall, and then at Leadford, and began to weep—not in terror, but in a quiet, wondering relief, like someone waking from a long fever.

Leadford stood up. His legs were stiff, yet his head was clear as a bell. He walked over to Verrall and held out his hand—not as a servant to a master, nor as a rival to a thief, but as one man to another on a bare hillside. Verrall looked at the hand for a moment, then took it and pulled himself to his feet. Together, the three of them turned to look out over the Channel, where the great grey warships drifted silent and harmless on a silver tide, the old madness of the world washed clean away.
