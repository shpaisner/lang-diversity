In Staffordshire, where the pit-banks shoulder the sky and the pot-ovens belch their greasy soot into the Midlands rain, a man learns early that the world is divided into those who stoke the fires and those who warm their shins by them. William Leadford was a stoker by birth and a clerk by the grace of three shillings a week, and his coat was shiny at the elbows. He stood in the High Street of Clayton with a newly bought revolver knocking against his hip-bone—a cheap, snub-nosed thing from a pawnbroker’s tray, oiled with lard and heavy with bad intentions.

Nettie was gone. She had slipped her cable in the night, leaving a blotted note on the kitchen table that smelled faintly of cheap scent and Verrall’s Turkish cigarettes. Verrall, whose father owned half the clay-pits between here and Burslem, had whistled her down the wind as a gentleman whistles a spaniel. They were gone south, the baker’s boy said, to Shaphambury by the sea, where the air was clean and the gentry took their ease.

Leadford looked up between the chimney-stacks. Above the red glare of the blast-furnaces hung the Comet. Three weeks ago it had been a pin-prick of green fire; tonight it was a great, ragged smudge of verdigris, brighter than the street-lamps, turning the coal-smoke to the colour of a stale bruise. Men in the public-houses were squinting at it over their pewter, talking thick and fast—not of the Comet, though, but of the North Sea, where the grey cruisers were coaling at double-tides and the papers screamed of an ultimatum from Berlin. Let Europe burn, Leadford thought, and spat into the gutter. He had a private war of his own to settle first.

The South-Western train rattled down through the fat shires like a tin kettle tied to a dog’s tail, and Leadford sat in the corner of a third-class smoker with his knuckles white on his knees. The carriage was thick with shag tobacco and the sour wool of reservists’ greatcoats. Four of them sat opposite him, lads from the Potteries with their kit-bags between their boots, laughing too loud to hide the twitch in their jaws. They were bound for Portsmouth, to the barracks and the troopships, while the newsboys at every junction bellowed of the Channel Fleet clearing for action.

"Green’s for danger, mate," said a corporal, jerking his thumb toward the window as twilight fell.

Out there, beyond the flying telegraph-poles and the neat, hedge-bound pastures that never knew a slag-heap, the sky was curdling. The Comet had risen clear of the horizon, a vast, pale fan of emerald light that drowned the evening star. It threw no warmth, only a cold, uncanny sheen on the slate roofs of the villages and the brass buttons of the soldiers.

Leadford only grunted and shifted the revolver in his pocket. His head ached with a dry, drumming fury. All his twenty-two years he had eaten the leavings—short commons, damp sheets, the sneer of the foreman, and the patronising nod of young Verrall riding by on his bay mare. Nettie had been the one clean, bright thing in the grime, and Verrall had pocketed her as he might pocket a trinket at a fair. When the train slowed for the coast, Leadford stepped out before the wheels had fairly stopped, his boots ringing on the platform flags with the single, dull rhythm of a man going to his own hanging.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, tar, and lavender—a soft, moneyed town built to catch the southern sun. By nine o’clock, however, there was nothing soft about the sea. Under the sickly glare of the Comet, the Channel looked like hammered lead, and three miles out, the low black hulls of torpedo-destroyers were nosing westward in line ahead, their searchlights twitching like nervous fingers across the dark water.

Leadford did not look at the fleet. He walked the esplanade with his collar turned up, past the shuttered bathing-machines and the white-painted hotels where the blinds were drawn tight against the fevered sky. He had fourpence left in his pocket and a map of the town etched in his hot brain. A porter at the station, bribed with sixpence he could ill spare, had remembered a gentleman in a tweed Norfolk jacket and a girl with a green ribbon in her hat taking a fly toward the cliff bungalows beyond the headland.

He found the track where the turf began, climbing steeply above the chalk. The wind off the water was dead calm—not a breath stirred the sea-thrift, yet the air felt heavy, pressing down on the eardrums like the silence before a thunderstorm in the pits. Up ahead, set back among stunted tamarisks, a square bungalow showed a patch of yellow lamp-light through its French windows. A shadow crossed the blind—broad-shouldered, easy, lifting a glass. Then came the lighter shadow, the turn of a head Leadford knew better than his own face in the glass.

He drew the revolver from his pocket and thumbed back the hammer. The click sounded very small and sharp against the vast, breathless hush of the sea.

They came out onto the gravel path before he reached the gate—Nettie with a shawl over her shoulders, and Verrall behind her, bareheaded, smoking with the slow, unhurried ease of a man who had never been told to make haste in his life. They stopped dead when Leadford stepped out from the tamarisks.

"Will!" Nettie’s hand flew to her throat, her eyes wide and black in the green starlight. "Will, what are you doing here?"

"I’ve come to settle the account, Nettie," Leadford said, though his voice cracked in his dry throat. He levelled the snub-nosed iron at Verrall’s waistcoat. "Stand clear of him."

Verrall did not flinch, though his jaw tightened and his cigarette dropped to the gravel. "Put that thing down, Leadford," he said, crisp as a subaltern on parade. "You’re making a fool of yourself. She came of her own free will."

"She was bought!" Leadford shouted, his finger tightening on the trigger, his whole body shaking with the accumulated poison of the slums. "Bought with your father’s dirt-money!"

He meant to fire. He saw the white patch of Verrall’s shirt-front jump in his sights. Then, out across the leaden Channel, the horizon flashed twice—a dull, orange wink—and three seconds later the heavy, flat thump of twelve-inch naval guns rolled up the cliffs and rattled the glass in the bungalow windows. The war had found its voice.

Nettie screamed, a small sound swallowed by the dark. At the same instant, the wind woke—not from the sea, but from above—a strange, warm sigh that smelled strongly of bruised mint and wet chalk. A thick, pale-green vapour slid down over the headland, blotting out the bungalows, the destroyers, and the shouting world. Leadford’s knees buckled under him like wet paper; he heard the revolver clatter on the stones as the sweet, heavy darkness took him by the throat.

It was not sleep, and it was not death, though it had the quiet of both. Leadford lay with his cheek pressed into the damp turf, and the green mist rolled over him in slow, silent tides, like the Thames fogs he had heard the old soldiers tell of, only clean—achingly, piercingly clean.

Through the thick of his stupor, he seemed to feel the great machine of England grinding to a halt. Far to the north, in Clayton and Hanley and Wolverhampton, the blast-furnaces were roaring unattended while the puddlers slept beside their moulds; the express trains stood dead between the signals, their boilers sighing steam into the emerald dark; and out upon the North Sea, where the grey ironclads had just begun to spit iron at each other across the swells, the gunners were sinking down behind their shields, their fingers loosening on the lanyards.

For an hour—or perhaps it was a year—the vapour washed through the chambers of Leadford’s brain. It was as if someone had thrown open the high, dusty windows of a locked attic. All the old, sour furniture he had carried since boyhood—the envy, the rankling pride, the hot, narrow little hatreds that kept a poor man warm in winter—shrank and curdled in that cool, mint-scented air until they looked no bigger than the cinders in a grate. He saw himself as he had been an hour ago, a stunted, snarling creature with a six-shilling pistol in his fist, and a great, helpless pity for himself, and for Verrall, and for the four frightened lads in the Portsmouth train, washed over him like warm rain.

When he opened his eyes, the sun was up—not the smoky, copper penny of Staffordshire, but a clear, washed gold that made the chalk cliffs shine like snow. The green vapour was gone, leaving only a bright, dewy sharpness in the air that made every breath feel like a draught of cold spring water.

Leadford sat up slowly. Ten paces away, the revolver lay on the gravel path, its cheap nickel plating dulled by the night’s dew. It looked like a child’s silly, rusted toy, and he had no more wish to touch it than he had to eat cinders.

A few yards off, Verrall was rising on one elbow, blinking at the sky, his tweed coat streaked with chalk-dust. Beside him, Nettie stirred, sat up, and smoothed her hair with both hands. When she saw Leadford, she did not scream or shrink back. She looked at him with a quiet, wondering steadiness, as if they were all three passengers who had just survived the same shipwreck.

"Will," she said softly, and there was no fear in it, only a vast bewilderment.

Verrall stood up, brushing the dirt from his knees. The easy, patronising stiffness was gone from his shoulders; he looked younger, and strangely bare, like a man stripped of his uniform. He walked over to the path, picked up the revolver by the barrel as if it were a dead beetle, and tossed it over the cliff-edge into the gorse.

"We were mad," Verrall said, looking out toward the sea, his voice thick with wonder. "God in heaven, what were we doing?"

Leadford got to his feet and stood beside him, and Nettie came to stand between them. Out on the Channel, where the guns had thundered in the dark, three grey destroyers lay motionless on the sparkling water, their engines stopped, their decks crowded with tiny figures looking up at the clean, empty morning sky.
