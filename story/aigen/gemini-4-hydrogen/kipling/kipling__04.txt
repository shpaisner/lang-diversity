The Five Towns sat under their own grease, as they have sat since the first pit-head gear was rigged, and the smoke of the bottle-kilns lay flat on the cabbage-patches like a wet blanket. William Leadford walked home from the timber-yard with his chin tucked into his collar and a penny newspaper crushed in his fist. The paper screamed of Ultimatums—big black type that smelled of printer’s ink and cheap blood—for the Emperors across the water were rattling their sabres in the scabbards, and the Reservists were already packing their kits in the back streets of Hanley.

William cared nothing for the Emperors. He had his own war in his pocket: a crumpled half-sheet of ruled notepaper, smelling faintly of violet-water, in which Nettie told him she was gone to the sea with young Verrall. Verrall, whose father owned the Deep Pit and half the terrace houses in Clayton!

At the corner by the canal bridge, William stopped and spat into the black water. Above the jagged teeth of the pottery chimneys, the sky was clearing to a bruised, smoky purple, and there hung the Green Star. It had been a speck in May; by August it was a lantern, bright as a railway signal set at All Clear—though God knew nothing was clear below it. Men in the pubs called it a judgement; the schoolmaster called it a wandering gas-bag of cyanogen. William looked up at its cold, emerald eye and felt the skin tighten across his knuckles. He had four pounds ten in the Post Office savings, a second-hand Belgian revolver in his tin trunk, and a heart like a clinker fresh from the furnace.

The Belgian revolver rode heavy against William’s ribs all the way from Crewe to the South Coast. It was a cheap, snub-nosed thing, bought off a discharged sailor in Liverpool, and he had oiled it with sewing-machine grease until the cylinder clicked like a well-kept lock.

The night express was packed to the corridors with men in khaki. They leaned out of the windows at every junction, roaring music-hall choruses and waving bottles of lukewarm beer, while the railway-clerks ran up and down the platforms with yellow telegraph flimsies fluttering in the gaslight. Somewhere in the dark beyond Salisbury, a battery of field-guns was being loaded onto flat-cars; the chains clanked and the horses screamed as the slings lifted them. Europe was sliding down the ways like a launched battleship, and nobody could knock the shores away fast enough to stop her.

William sat in the corner of a third-class smoker, his knees pressed against a corporal’s kit-bag, and said never a word. When the soldiers offered him a pull at their flasks, he shook his head. They thought him a sour, chapel-bred clerk—and so he was, in his threadbare black serge—but inside his skull a small, white-hot engine was hammering out one name over and over: Verrall. Verrall. Verrall.

Through the soot-streaked window, the Comet kept pace with the train. It had grown a tail now, a great fan of pale sea-green fire that swept half-way to the zenith, dimming the honest stars. The corporal pointed at it with his pipe-stem and swore softly. "Rum sort of limelight for the show," he said. William only touched the cold iron in his pocket and watched the dark hedges fly past.

Bungay-on-Sea was built for the people who draw dividends, not for the men who dig the coal. There were no slag-heaps here, only red-tiled villas tucked behind tamarisk hedges, and gravel drives swept as clean as a quarter-deck. The air smelled of pine-needles and salt water, instead of sulphur and fried fat.

William found the bungalow at dusk, perched on the lip of the chalk cliff where the downs dropped away to the Channel. He knew it by the big Daimler motor-car parked under the lean-to—Verrall’s car, all brass lamps and red leather, mud-splashed from the London road. Inside, the French windows stood open to the warm evening, and a gramophone was winding through a waltz.

William slipped through a gap in the privet and crouched among the gorse-bushes. His boots were grey with chalk dust; his collar was wilted with sweat. Out on the black water, three miles from shore, the long white fingers of the Channel Fleet’s searchlights were sweeping the horizon, crossing and recrossing like duellists’ blades. Somewhere out there, behind the dark rim of the sea, the other fleets were steaming with their lights doused and their magazines open.

Then Nettie stepped out onto the veranda. She was wearing a white muslin frock William had never seen, and her hair was piled high in the fashion of the illustrated papers. Verrall followed her, a glass in his hand, his white shirt-front gleaming in the lamplight. He laughed at something she said—a short, easy, well-fed laugh that crossed the lawn like the crack of a whip. William drew the Belgian revolver from his pocket, thumbed back the hammer, and waited for his breath to steady.

He did not mean to creep up like a poacher, so he walked straight across the gravel, his boots crunching loud in the quiet. Nettie saw him first. Her hand flew to her throat, and the glass slipped from Verrall’s fingers, shattering on the red tiles.

"Willie!" she cried, and there was more terror than pity in her voice.

Verrall did not flinch. To give the devil his due, the breed has nerve; he stood between Nettie and the steps, his hands half-raised, looking down his nose at the shabby Midlands clerk. "Put that toy away, Leadford," he said evenly. "You're making a fool of yourself."

"I've walked from the station to settle an account," William said. His voice sounded strange in his own ears, thin and dry as a rattle. "Stand aside, Mr. Verrall. I've no quarrel with her—only with you, and the likes of you."

"You're mad," Verrall snapped, taking a step forward. "There's a war on, man—half the country's under arms, and you come whining here over a girl!"

William raised the pistol. The foresight trembled against Verrall’s waistcoat. At that instant, the night went dead. The gramophone inside the house gave a long, dying groan and stopped; the wind off the Channel dropped as if a door had been slammed. Above them, the Green Comet flared—not a light, but a silent, blinding wash of emerald that turned the chalk cliffs to jade. Then came the smell: sweet as crushed fern, sharp as lightning, rolling in from the sea in a low, luminous fog. William pulled the trigger—or thought he did—but his finger turned to lead, his knees buckled, and he went down onto the gravel beside Verrall’s broken glass.

It was a sleep that had no dreams in it, and no measure of time. Across the length of Europe, the green vapour crept through keyholes and gun-ports alike. In the engine-rooms of the great battleships, stokers dropped their shovels and lay down beside the roaring furnaces; on the Belgian frontier, cavalrymen slid from their saddles into the clover, while their horses stood cropping the grass above them. In Staffordshire, the fires in the bottle-kilns burned down to grey ash for the first time in a hundred years, and nobody woke to stoke them.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was high and the sky was a blue he had never seen—clear as washed glass, without a smudge of coal-smoke from horizon to horizon. He lay on the gravel drive. His cheek was bruised from the stones, and the Belgian revolver lay three inches from his nose, dull and ugly in the clean morning light.

He sat up slowly, expecting the old, familiar ache behind his eyes—the sour knot of poverty and spite that had been his daily bread since he was twelve years old. It was gone. His head felt as light and hollow as a swept barn. He looked at his hands, stained with timber-yard dirt and cheap ink, and wondered for a long moment why he had brought them so far to do murder.

A gull wheeled overhead, crying cleanly against the empty blue. Out in the Channel, the grey hulls of the Fleet lay motionless on the tide, their funnels dead, not a wisp of smoke rising from any of them. The great machine of the war had stopped in the middle of a stroke, and the silence was sweeter than music.

Verrall was sitting on the veranda steps with his head in his hands, his dress-shirt damp with dew, looking remarkably young and foolish without his collar-stud. Beside him, Nettie knelt on the tiles, staring out over the bright sea as though she had never seen water before.

William stood up, brushed the gravel from his knees, and walked to the steps. Neither of them shrank from him. When he bent and lifted the revolver, it felt absurd—a clumsy lump of ironmongery, fit only for a scrap-heap. He tossed it over the cliff-edge; they heard it clatter faintly on the shingle below.

"I was going to shoot you," William said, with no heat, only a quiet, wondering shame.

Verrall looked up, blinking in the sunlight. "I know," he said. "And I was going to have you locked up for twenty years." He rubbed his unshaven jaw. "It seems a very small thing to bother about, now."

Nettie turned toward them both, her eyes washed free of the old vanity and the old fear. "Look," she whispered, pointing down the white cliff-road.

A coastguard was walking up from the village, his uniform unbuttoned, a loaf of bread under his arm. He leaned on the gate, smiling like a man woken from a long fever. "Morning, all," he called. "The telegraph's dead, the Fleet's asleep, and the world's turned over in its bed. Care to walk down and see what's left of us?"

William looked at Verrall, and Verrall looked back—not as master and man, nor as rival and thief, but as two strangers shipwrecked on the same bright shore. With Nettie between them, they walked out into the morning.
