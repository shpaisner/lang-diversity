The Potteries lay under their usual blanket of greasy coal-smoke, though the great kilns of Burslem and Hanley were slacking down for want of orders, and men stood at the street-corners with their hands in their breeches-pockets, spitting into the gutter and watching the sky. It was not a proper sky for Staffordshire. Above the ragged black teeth of the pit-head gear hung the Comet, swelling night by night until it threw a sickly, copperas-green shadow across the cinder-paths.

William Leadford kept his eyes on the cobbles. He was twenty-two, lean as a rake-handle, with ink-stains on his middle finger from the clerk’s stool at Rawdon’s works and a bitter, sour taste at the back of his throat that no small beer could wash away. In his right-hand coat pocket lay a Belgian bulldog revolver, bought for eleven-and-sixpence off a pawnbroker in Newcastle-under-Lyme, together with six brass-jacketed cartridges that felt heavier than lead.

Nettie was gone. She had slipped out of her father’s cottage by the canal-bank three days back, leaving only a twisted scrap of paper to say she would not be a poor man’s drudge while the world was wide. And Verrall—young Verrall of the Manor, who wore cream flannels and smelled of cedar-wood and cavalry-twill—had taken his motor-car east toward the sea that very afternoon.

At the corner by the Red Lion, a newsboy was bawling himself hoarse. "War! Ultimatum! Fleet sails at dawn!" Men snatched the damp pink sheets, swearing and jostling. Leadford bought no paper. Let the Germans and the Frenchmen blow each other to scrap-iron; he had his own account to settle at Shaphambury-on-Sea.

The Great Eastern slow train smelled of wet corduroy, shag tobacco, and the sour breath of too many men packed into a third-class smoker. Half the compartment were Reservists called up from the Midlands—red-necked lads in ill-fitting serge who passed a stone bottle of gin from hand to hand and sang music-hall choruses about the Kaiser until the carriage windows rattled in their sashes.

Leadford sat in the far corner by the door, his knees drawn up, his hand never leaving the bulge in his pocket. Every mile across the flat fenland seemed to grind his teeth tighter together. He thought of the long evenings he had walked Nettie beside the foul black water of the Trent and Mersey Canal, talking of Socialism and the New Age and the books he had read by candle-end, while she watched the swallows and said nothing at all. And all the while Verrall had been waiting with his money and his smooth-tongued lies.

"Look at yon," said a corporal, jerking a thumb at the smeary glass.

The train had slowed for a signal-box outside Ely. Over the dark, reed-choked ditches, the Comet was rising—no longer a star, but a vast, feathery fan of pale emerald fire that swept half-way to the zenith. It turned the steam from the locomotive into ghostly verdigris and made the faces of the singing soldiers look like the faces of drowned men.

"Rum sort o’ lantern," muttered the corporal, crossing himself before he remembered he was a Chapel man. "If the German ships have that to steer by, they’ll see us plain enough."

Leadford said nothing. He watched the green glare crawl across the carriage floor and wished the engine would run faster.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, tar, and rotting bladderwrack—a clean, fierce smell that stung Leadford’s soot-lined nostrils like ammonia. He had walked from the station with his boots grey with chalk-dust, asking no questions, guided by the instinct of a terrier that knows which hedge the rabbit took.

He found them toward dusk on the high, windy path above the shingle, where the sea-thrift grew thick between the flints and the tamarisk bushes bent double under the Channel gale. The big white hotel down on the Esplanade was ablaze with electric light, and a band was thumping out The Girl I Left Behind Me for the benefit of the gentry on the terrace; but up here on the cliff there was only the grumble of the tide and the crying of the gulls.

Verrall was walking a pace ahead, his hands behind his back, wearing a white sweater with a club stripe at the collar. Nettie walked beside him in a blue serge skirt and a straw hat tied down with a veil. She was laughing—a clear, careless little laugh that hit Leadford harder than a fist between the eyes.

He slipped behind a clump of gorse, his heart hammering against his ribs like a faulty donkey-engine. So this was what a man got for keeping straight and reading his Ruskin and saving sixpences out of eighteen shillings a week! He drew the Belgian revolver from his pocket. The barrel was cold as a frog, and the cheap nickel plating gleamed dull green in the light of the Comet, which now hung directly over the French coast like a drawn scimitar.

"Wait," he whispered to himself, his thumb on the hammer. "Wait till they turn the bend."

They turned the bend by the old coastguard’s shelter, where the chalk path narrowed to a yard’s width above a hundred-foot drop. Leadford stepped out of the gorse-shadow and stood square in their way.

Nettie stopped dead, her hand flying to her throat. "Willie!" she gasped.

Verrall did not flinch—to give the devil his due, the breed had nerve—but he thrust Nettie behind his shoulder and eyed the revolver with the cool, measuring look of a man who knew guns. "Put that down, Leadford," he said evenly. "You’re making a fool of yourself."

"A fool?" Leadford’s voice cracked like a whip-lash. "You’ve robbed me of everything I had in the world, you—you gentleman! You’ve come down here to play your games while the whole country’s burning up for war!"

He raised the pistol. His finger took the slack of the trigger.

Then the wind died. It did not slacken; it simply ceased, as if someone had shut a great door across the Channel. The surf on the shingle below dropped to a whisper. Out of the east, rolling swift and low over the black water, came a bank of luminous green fog. It had a strange, sweet, sharp smell—like crushed laurel leaves and cold rain after lightning—and as it washed over the cliff-edge, the very air seemed to thicken into velvet.

Leadford tried to pull the trigger, but his finger would not obey. A vast, dizzying languor crept up his shins and into his chest. He saw Nettie sink slowly to her knees in the sea-thrift, her eyes wide and untroubled; he saw Verrall sway like a felled pine and catch at the turf. Then the green dark folded over William Leadford’s eyes, and he dropped beside his revolver without a sound.

When he woke, the sun was an hour high, and the sea was the colour of a thrush’s egg.

Leadford sat up slowly, brushing the dry chalk from his sleeves. There was no ache in his head, no sourness in his mouth, none of the old, grinding knot behind his breastbone. He felt as a man feels after a long fever has broken in the night—light, clean, and astonishingly clear-sighted, as though someone had taken a rag and wiped twenty years of Staffordshire grime off the windowpane of his mind.

Beside his left boot lay the Belgian revolver. He picked it up and turned it over in his palm. It looked absurd—a mean, greasy little lump of bad iron, fit only to frighten sparrows or ruin a man’s life for eleven-and-sixpence. Without haste, he stood up, walked to the lip of the cliff, and tossed it out over the gulls. It turned twice in the bright air and vanished into the green water below with scarcely a splash.

He looked back at the town of Shaphambury, lying neat and white in the curve of the bay. Smoke was rising from the bakers’ chimneys as usual, yet something in the stillness of the morning told him the world had turned a corner while they slept. All the hot, blind fury that had driven him across three counties—the envy of Verrall’s boots, the hunger to own Nettie like a piece of freehold property, the whole ugly scramble of rich and poor—seemed now like a quarrel between drunken children in a dark room. Now someone had opened the shutters, and the daylight was pouring in.

A few yards off, Nettie stirred in the grass, sat up, and pushed the tangled veil back from her forehead. Verrall was already on his elbow, blinking at the sun like a man who had forgotten his own name. For a long minute none of the three spoke.

When Verrall looked at Leadford, the old insolent stiffness was gone from his jaw. "I treated you badly, Leadford," he said, and his voice had no more pride in it than a working man’s. "I took what wasn’t mine to take, and I’d have fought you for it last night if I could. I’m sorry."

"There’s nothing to forgive," Leadford answered simply. "She wasn’t mine to lose, either. We were all of us mad in the dark."

Nettie looked from one to the other, her eyes clear and unafraid. She reached out and laid her hand for an instant on Leadford’s sleeve—not in pity, nor in the old coquetry, but as one neighbour greets another after a storm.

Down on the lower path, a coastguard in his blue jersey was running toward the town, waving his cap and shouting to a fisherman on the beach. The words carried clear up the cliff in the still air: "They’ve struck! The ships have struck! Neither side’ll fire a gun—the admirals are shaking hands off Dover!"

Verrall stood up, brushing the grass from his flannels, and held out a hand to Nettie. Then he looked at Leadford. "Will you walk down with us, Leadford? There’ll be work to do in the town today—real work, I think, for once."

Leadford nodded, and the three of them turned down the white chalk path together, into the clean and quiet morning.
