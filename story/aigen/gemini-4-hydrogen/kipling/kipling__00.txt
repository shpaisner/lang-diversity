In the Black Country, where the pit-banks sit like scabs on the green hide of England and the bottle-ovens belch their yellow grease into the rain, a man learns early what his skin is worth. William Leadford knew it to the halfpenny. Eighteen shillings a week at the foundry desk, a mother with a cough like a cracked bell, and lungs that tasted of sulphur before he was twenty-two. And over it all, night after night, the Thing in the sky.

They called it a comet, the professors in London, though to Willie, leaning over the canal bridge with his collar turned up against the drizzle, it looked like a drop of verdigris squeezed from a dirty thumb. Every evening it hung lower above the slag-heaps, shining with a cold, greasy green light that turned the puddles to poison and made the colliers’ faces look like tallow in a mortuary.

Willie did not care for astronomy. He cared for Nettie Stuart, who had hair the colour of polished copper and a laugh that made a man forget the four-o’clock whistle. He had walked her out by the towpath when the hawthorn was white, and he had believed—God help the fool!—that a clerk with ink on his cuffs could hold a girl against the gentry.

Then came the Tuesday when the cottage in Clayton was empty, and Mrs. Stuart sat by the cold grate with a folded note in her apron. Nettie was gone. Not with a collier, nor a pot-maker, which a man might have hammered out behind the beer-house, but with young Edward Verrall of the Hall—him with the motor-car and the voice like buttered toast.

A man who has been kicked by his betters all his days does not weep when the last kick lands; he goes to the pawnshop. Willie had four pounds saved in a mustard-tin beneath the floorboards—his mother’s burial money, though she was not dead yet—and he spent thirty shillings of it on a second-hand, five-chambered Brummagem revolver and a box of greasy cartridges. The pawnbroker did not ask his business. In those weeks, half England was buying ironmongery, and the other half was shouting for it.

For Europe had caught the fever that was in the green star. At the station, newsboys were howling like terrier-pups over the evening editions: the German fleet had slipped its moorings at Kiel; the Channel Squadron was steaming under forced draught, black smoke flatting out behind the funnels; the Reservists were kissing their wives goodbye under the gas-lamps. Men talked of Krupp guns and dreadnoughts as though they were cricket scores, their eyes bright and dry with the madness.

Willie took the train south with the revolver banging against his thigh-bone. He had found the address in Nettie’s drawer—a seaside hotel at Shaphambury, where the chalk cliffs drop white into the grey tide. All night the carriages rattled through the dark, packed with shouting clerks and red-faced farmers singing music-hall songs about the Kaiser. Willie sat in the corner, his cap pulled over his eyes, his fingers locked round the butt of the little pistol. Let the nations cut each other’s throats if they pleased. He had a smaller, cleaner piece of butchery to do first, and the green star watched him through the smuts on the window-pane.

Shaphambury was full of the sea and the rumour of war. Out beyond the breakwater, where the fishing-smacks usually bobbed like corks, three lean grey cruisers lay at anchor, their searchlights sweeping the black water with long, nervous fingers of white fire. Now and again a signal-lamp blinked from the headland—dot-dash-dot—talking in the cold dark to the forts along the coast.

Willie walked the Esplanade with the salt wind biting his Staffordshire throat. The green light of the comet was everywhere now, broad as a harvest moon and twice as wicked, washing the chalk cliffs in the hue of an old copper kettle. The hotel windows were shuttered, for fear of German shells, they said, though no German had yet set foot on the shingle.

He found them at the cliff’s edge, away from the lamps, where the gorse smelled of coconut and the sea sucked at the stones below. There was no mistaking Verrall’s Norfolk jacket, nor the pale oval of Nettie’s face turned up to him. They were talking low—not like lovers in a penny novelette, Willie noticed with a sudden, ugly twist of his gut, but like two people who had bought something dear and were wondering if the price was too high.

Willie slipped behind a clump of tamarisk and drew the Brummagem pistol. The hammer clicked twice—sharp as a beetle’s wing—in the pause between the breakers. His hand was steady enough. He had spent six years in the foundry watching molten iron poured into moulds; a man learns not to flinch when the hot metal runs.

"You’ve come a long way, Willie," said Nettie. She had turned her head, and the green glare caught the whites of her eyes. She did not scream. Perhaps the Staffordshire blood was thicker in her than he thought.

Verrall stepped in front of her—to give the devil his due, the puppy had the pluck of his breed. "Put that thing down, Leadford," he said, his voice tight as a fiddle-string. "There’s enough murder loose in the world tonight without your adding to it."

"You’ve had everything else," Willie said, and his voice sounded thin and rusty, like a hinge that had never seen oil. "The land, the money, the soft beds, and now her. Leave me my bit of lead, Mr. Verrall. It’s all I paid for."

He raised the barrel. Out on the water, a mile from shore, a sudden dull boom rolled across the bay—a gun-flash, orange against the sickly green—and the cruisers’ searchlights snapped together like crossed swords. The war had begun, somewhere out in the dark where the destroyers were hunting.

Willie’s finger tightened on the trigger. Then the wind died.

It did not slacken; it simply ceased to be, as though a great hand had clapped a lid over the world. A smell came down from the sky—sweetish, heavy, like bruised almonds and warm rain—and a green mist rolled over the cliff-edge. Willie tried to pull the trigger, but his arm felt stuffed with wet clay. Verrall swayed, caught at Nettie’s shoulder, and went down in the gorse. Willie’s knees followed. The last thing he saw was the Brummagem pistol dropping harmlessly into the turf.

They woke when the sun was high, though the world did not look like the world they had left behind.

Willie sat up first, spitting the taste of old iron out of his mouth. His head was as clear as a scrubbed pewter pot. No ache, no bile, no hot little knot of hatred sitting behind his breastbone like a coal. He looked at the pistol lying beside his boot, picked it up by the barrel, and stared at it as a man stares at a dead toad he has somehow carried in his pocket. With a flick of his wrist, he pitched it over the cliff-edge into the surf.

Down in the bay, the three grey cruisers were still there, swinging idly on their cables, but no smoke rose from their funnels. A ship’s boat was pulling slowly toward the shingle, and the bluejackets at the oars were laughing—not the tight, drunken laughter of the night before, but the easy chuckling of men who have dropped a heavy pack.

Across Europe, though Willie did not know it yet, the same clean wind was blowing through the barracks and the chancelleries. Men who had lain down with bayonets fixed and murder in their teeth were sitting up in the clover, blinking at the strange, bright air, wondering why on earth they had dressed themselves in red and grey to shoot strangers over a ditch. The great green vapour had washed the soot out of the human engine, and left the brasswork shining.

Verrall stirred in the gorse, pushed himself up on one elbow, and looked at Willie without fear or pride. Nettie sat beside him, her copper hair tangled with dry grass, her hands clasped round her knees. There was no shame in her face—only a quiet wonder, like a child waking in a strange house.

"I meant to kill you both," Willie said, as plainly as he might have said he meant to catch the eight-fifteen train.

"I know," said Verrall. He rubbed the back of his neck, looking down at his fine tweed coat, now stained with chalk and dew. "And I meant to keep her locked away like a prize heifer. Lord, Leadford, what fools we were!"

"Aye," Willie said. "Big fools."

Nettie looked from one to the other, no longer weighing what either man could give her. "Look at the sea," she said softly.

They stood up together, the foundry clerk, the squire’s son, and the girl from the Potteries, and walked to the cliff’s lip. The Channel was blue—not the greasy lead of yesterday, but a clean, washed blue under a sky stripped of smoke. Far off, a German torpedo-boat steamed slowly alongside a British cruiser, her crew lining the rail to wave their caps like collier-boys on a bank holiday.

Willie drew a deep breath. For the first time since he was a child, his lungs did not rattle. He knew there would be work to do—kilns to rebuild, laws to mend, a whole dirty world to scrub from cellar to garret—but as he turned toward the road with Nettie and Verrall beside him, he knew he would never go back to his eighteen-shilling cage.
