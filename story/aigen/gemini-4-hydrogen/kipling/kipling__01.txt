In the Five Towns, where the bottle-kilns squat like blackened beehives and the air tastes of sulfur and wet ash, a man learns early what his place is worth. William Leadford had learned it behind the ink-stained counter of Rawdon’s Pottery—twelve shillings a week, a cough that woke him at four, and a mother who scrubbed the front step till her knuckles bled for pride’s sake. He was twenty-two, thin as a lathe-strip, with a head full of sour pamphlets and a heart burning like a slack-heap.

Then Nettie Stuart went away with Edward Verrall.

Verrall’s father owned the pits at Swadlincote and a red-brick park with iron gates; young Verrall had a motor-car, white flannels, and the easy, half-laughing way of men who never count pence for tallow. Nettie had been Leadford’s—promised by the canal towpath, kissed behind the slag-mounds—until those flannels came down the lane. She left a note on the dresser, blotted with tears and poor spelling, and vanished south.

Overhead, meanwhile, the sky had gone mad. Night after night, a great green star hung above the chimney-stacks, trailing a fan of cold, emerald fire that turned the coal-smoke the color of verdigris. In the public-houses, men scarcely looked at it; they were too busy shouting over the evening papers. The German Emperor had rattled his saber, French reserves were entraining at Lyon, and the British Fleet was coaling at Portland with the dockyard gates locked. War was coming down the wind, hot and noisy, while William Leadford sat in his cold parlor, staring at a dry inkwell and nursing a hate sharper than any bayonet.

He bought the revolver on a Tuesday in Hanley, from a little dealer whose window held accordions and tarnished silver watches. It was a stubby, nickel-plated bulldog, six-chambered, with a gutta-percha grip that smelled of old pockets. Leadford paid nine shillings for it and three more for rim-fire cartridges, sliding the heavy iron into his serge coat till the lining sagged.

“Going rabbiting, young man?” the dealer asked, squinting over his spectacles.

“Ay,” said Leadford, and went out into the drizzle.

The train south to the coast was packed to the footboards with reservists and commercial travelers, talking through a blue fog of shag tobacco. A corporal in the corner had a map of the North Sea spread across his knees, stabbing at the Dogger Bank with a black-nailed thumb.

“They’ll try a rush before declarations are signed,” the corporal said, nodding wisely. “Torpedo-boats out of Wilhelmshaven. Mark my words, we’ll hear big guns off Dover before Thursday.”

Leadford said nothing. Wedged by the window, his knuckles pressed hard against the pistol, he watched the green comet climb above the racing telegraph poles. Fat, quiet pastures slid past—land that knew nothing of Rawdon’s Pottery or Nettie’s lies—while the green light grew brighter, washing the hedges in an unearthly glare. He had found their address in an old envelope behind her mother’s clock: a coast village called Shaphambury, where gentry went to smell the salt. Let the Germans come, he thought; let the rotten world blow up in cordite and smoke. He had his own reckoning to settle first.

Shaphambury lay white and clean under a chalk headland, smelling of tarred rope, bladderwrack, and sweetbriar. To Leadford, whose lungs were seasoned with Staffordshire soot, the sea-wind tasted almost thin, like cold water after beer. He walked the shingle road above the bay with his hat pulled low, boots grinding the flint.

Out beyond the point, three grey cruisers were steaming without lights, long black funnels trailing a smudge against the green horizon. Every few minutes a searchlight’s white finger leaped across the swell, sweeping empty water as if looking for ghosts, then snapped shut. Villagers stood in knots by the capstans, watching in tight-lipped silence. Even the gulls had stopped wheeling; they huddled on the groynes with heads tucked low, troubled by the oily stillness of the air.

He found the bungalow half a mile past the coastguard station—a neat box of whitewashed timber with green shutters and a veranda facing the sea. A bicycle leaned against the palings, and through parted curtains he saw the warm yellow glow of an oil-lamp.

Leadford stopped behind a clump of tamarisk, his breath coming short and dry. A shadow crossed the blind—tall, broad-shouldered, moving with that cursed, unhurried grace of the Verralls. Then came a lighter shadow, and the faint, clear sound of Nettie’s laugh.

That laugh struck him across the face like a wet strap. He drew the bulldog from his pocket, thumbed back the hammer with a sharp click, and stepped through the wooden gate. The air felt thick enough to chew, heavy with a sweetish scent neither sea-salt nor garden-flowers.

They came onto the veranda before he reached the steps—Verrall in his white sweater, carrying binoculars, and Nettie with a shawl over her shoulders, looking at the sky. The comet had swollen till it covered half the zenith, a vast, pulsing curtain of green fire that turned the chalk cliffs the color of moss.

“Look at the sea, Teddy,” Nettie whispered. “It’s like oil.”

“Here’s something nearer than the sea,” Leadford said.

He stood at the foot of the stairs, the nickel barrel gleaming pale green in his fist. Nettie gave a choked cry and caught Verrall’s arm. Verrall did not flinch—the gentry are trained not to jump at noises—but his jaw set hard as he pushed the girl behind him.

“Leadford,” he said, keeping his voice level. “Put that down. You’re a fool if you think this mends anything.”

“I’m not here to mend!” Leadford shouted, his voice cracking like a boy’s in the dead air. “I’m here to break! You took what was mine because you’d the brass to buy it, and left me to rot in the smoke. Well, the smoke’s followed you!”

Far out in the Channel, a dull thump shook the shingle under their feet—then another, like giant doors slamming in a cellar. The cruisers had opened fire.

“God!” Verrall muttered, glancing toward the black horizon. “It’s begun.”

“Ay,” said Leadford, raising the pistol till the foresight rested on the white wool of Verrall’s chest.

Then the sky fell down. It came without wind, as a rolling, luminous green mist that swept off the water and over the headland, swallowing cruisers, cliffs, and bungalow in one silent, emerald wave.

Leadford’s finger tightened on the trigger, but the iron seemed to turn to warm wax against his skin. A great, cool sweetness rushed into his nostrils—not the choking damp of a coal-pit, nor the sting of acid, but a breath like crushed mint and clean rain after drought.

“Nettie—” he heard Verrall say, very far off, as though through thick glass.

Then Verrall’s knees gave way, and he slid down against the veranda post without a sound. Nettie sank beside him, her shawl pooling on the boards like spilled milk. Leadford tried to step forward, to hold onto his fine, hot rage, to remember the twelve shillings a week and the ink-stains; but the rage was slipping out of him like water from a cracked pitcher. His boots felt anchored to the earth. He dropped to one knee on the gravel, the revolver clattering softly from his open palm.

Out at sea, the distant thumping of naval guns hesitated, fired one ragged, lonely shot, and ceased. Over the whole breadth of England—over the roaring blast-furnaces of the Black Country, over the shouting crowds in Whitehall, over dark transports steaming toward the Belgian coast—the green vapor settled like a quiet hand laid upon a fevered forehead.

Leadford lay on his side in the gravel, his cheek resting against his arm. He watched the green stars swim and blur through the mist, and for the first time since childhood, the tight, sour knot beneath his ribs untied itself. There was no darkness in that sleep, only a deep, cool silence, as if the world had stopped its machinery to let the bearings cool.

He woke to a sun that had nothing in common with yesterday. The sky above Shaphambury was a pale, washed blue, clean as a scrubbed plate, and the green terror of the night had vanished without leaving a stain on the horizon.

Leadford sat up slowly, brushing gravel from his cheek. His head was astonishingly light—not dizzy, but clear, as if someone had opened a window in a stuffy room and let the cobwebs blow out. Beside him lay the nickel-plated revolver, beaded with morning dew. He picked it up, turned it over once in his fingers, and tossed it into the tamarisk bush; it looked as silly and shameful as a child’s wooden sword.

On the veranda, Verrall was stirring, sitting up with one hand to his brow, blinking at the bright water. Nettie sat beside him, her hair down around her shoulders, looking quietly at the sea. Neither seemed startled when Leadford climbed the steps.

“The guns stopped,” Verrall said. His voice had lost its smooth, patronizing ring; he spoke simply, like a man waking in a strange inn.

“Ay,” said Leadford, leaning against the rail. “They stopped.”

Out in the bay, two grey cruisers lay motionless on the glassy swell, signal-halyards hanging slack, their decks dotted with tiny blue-clad figures who stood looking shoreward in complete stillness. No smoke rose from their funnels.

Nettie looked up at Leadford, and for the first time he saw her clearly—not as a prize stolen from him, nor a dream hoarded in the dark, but as a frightened girl in a torn shawl, very small under the wide morning sky. There was no hatred left in him to spend on her, and none for the man beside her.

“You’ve come a long way, Will,” she said gently.

“I have,” Leadford answered, looking out across the peaceful, shining Channel. “And I think we’ve all of us got a deal of work to do before night.”
