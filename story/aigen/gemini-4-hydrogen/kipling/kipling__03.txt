The smoke of the Five Towns never wholly leaves a man’s throat, even when he has washed the clay from his knuckles and put on his Sunday serge. William Leadford knew the taste of it—sulphur and wet coal and the sour steam of the drying-sheds—better than he knew the taste of butcher’s meat. He was twenty-two, thin as a lath, with a clerk’s ink-stain on his middle finger and a radical’s fire behind his eyes. Now the fire had turned to ash, for Nettie was gone. She had left no more than a folded scrap of paper on her mother’s dresser, smelling faintly of lavender-water and the grander sort of soap, to say she had gone south with Edward Verrall. Verrall, whose father owned half the pits and all the timber-yards between Clayton and the hill.

Leadford stood in the cinder-yard behind his mother’s cottage, watching the sky above the bottle-kilns. The smoke-pall was torn by a cold east wind, and there, hanging low above the ridge-tiles, burned the Green Star. Men in the taprooms called it the Comet, and spat into the sawdust when they named it, for the papers said it would brush the world’s flank before the month was out. Leadford did not care if it cracked the earth like a bad plate. In his coat pocket lay a bull-dog revolver, bought for twelve shillings from a pawnbroker in Burslem, oiled with mutton-fat and loaded in five chambers. The sixth he kept empty for decency’s sake, or fear of his own pocket.

All England was mad that week, though not for Nettie. At Crewe Junction, where Leadford changed for the London train, the platforms were choked with Militia-men in stiff new khaki, singing songs about the Kaiser and the North Sea, their kitbags smelling of dubbin and damp canvas. A newsboy thrust a pink sheet under Leadford’s nose: Ultimatum Expires at Midnight! Grand Fleet Clears for Action! The clerk beside him in the third-class carriage—a fat man in a celluloid collar—tapped the headlines with a trembling thumb and swore the Germans would be in Lowestoft by Tuesday.

Leadford stared out at the flying hedgerows and said nothing. What were battleships to a man whose heart had been stolen by a gentleman’s son? Yet the madness of the carriage got into his blood, as war-fever will, mixing with his private venom until he scarcely knew whether he hated Verrall or the German Emperor more. By dusk, when he changed again for the Sussex coast, the Green Star had climbed higher. It was no longer a star, but a misty, emerald thumb-print smudged across the violet dark, casting no warmth, only a sickly, copper-green sheen on the carriage brasswork and the white faces of the passengers. At Shaphambury, where the sea-wind tasted of kelp instead of coal-smoke, Leadford stepped down alone. The station-master was arguing with a telegraph-boy about Mobilization Orders; neither looked twice at the pale young man in the cheap serge suit walking swiftly toward the sandhills.

He found the bungalow easily enough, for a man with twelve shillings’ worth of lead in his pocket has a hound’s nose for his quarry. It stood apart from the town, tucked behind a low dike of shingle and tamarisk where the downs sloped down to the Channel. It was a neat, timbered box of a place, painted white, with a broad veranda looking toward France. Through the uncurtained French windows, Leadford saw the lamp-glow—warm, yellow, and insolently peaceful. There was a silver tea-service on the table, and Nettie’s laugh floated out over the crisp rattle of the surf, light as a shuttlecock.

Leadford crouched in the spiky marram-grass twenty yards off, his boots filling with cold sand. His fingers were stiff around the butt of the bull-dog. He had rehearsed the speeches a hundred times on the train—bitter, ringing things about the working man’s honour and the theft of the poor man’s ewe lamb—but here, with the salt wind blowing through his thin coat, the words turned to dry chaff in his mouth. Out beyond the black horizon, a searchlight swept the clouds: one of the Channel cruisers, sweeping for torpedo-boats before the midnight hour struck. Below it, the sea lay like beaten pewter under the Comet’s growing glare. Then the glass door opened. Verrall stepped out in white flannels, a briar pipe between his teeth, and Nettie followed him with a shawl about her shoulders. They walked down the gravel path toward the shingle, arm in arm, talking low. Leadford rose from the grass like a heron from a reed-bed and followed them.

“Nettie!”

The name tore out of his throat, harsh as a file on iron. She spun round on the crunching pebbles, her hand flying to her mouth, while Verrall stepped half a pace before her—not afraid, merely annoyed, as a gentleman is annoyed by a stray dog on a private lawn.

“Leadford?” Verrall said, peering into the green dusk. “What the devil are you doing here?”

Leadford did not answer him. He looked only at Nettie, whose face under the Comet’s light was the colour of sea-foam. “Come away, Nettie,” he said, and his voice shook so that he hated himself for it. “Come home. You don’t belong to his sort.”

Before she could speak, Verrall laid a hand on her arm and told Leadford to clear off before he called the coastguard. That did it. The Staffordshire soot, the long shifts, the fat clerk on the train, and the mocking ease of the rich man’s voice all snapped together in Leadford’s skull. He hauled the bull-dog from his pocket and fired twice. The flat crack-crack was swallowed instantly by the roar of the shingle. He saw Verrall flinch and stagger back a step—whether hit or tripped by the loose stones, Leadford could not tell—and Nettie shrieked. Leadford ran forward, thumbing back the hammer for a third shot, when a sudden, sweet heaviness struck him across the knees. The air had changed. It smelled no longer of kelp and cordite, but of crushed almonds and warm summer rain after thunder. Overhead, the Green Star had burst into a vast, silent fan of emerald mist that swallowed the searchlights and the stars alike. Leadford stumbled, his lungs filling with a strange, cool fire, and pitched forward onto the wet stones.

He thought he was dead, or dying of the gas that sometimes leaked from the old workings at home, yet there was no pain in his chest. Instead, a great quietness came down upon the shingle-bank, heavier than sleep and clearer than water. Through the closing shutters of his eyes, he saw the emerald fog roll in from the Channel—not drifting like sea-fret, but pouring down from the upper sky in silent, luminous curtains. Far out, the searchlights of the Grand Fleet winked once, twice, and went out, as if a giant hand had snuffed the candles before bed. On the cliffs above Shaphambury, the bugles that had been blowing the assembly died in the middle of a bar.

All along the coast, from the Foreland to the Wight, men were dropping where they stood—sentries beside their stacked rifles, stokers at the furnace-doors of the destroyers, admirals over their charts, and clerks in the crowded London streets—breathing in the sweet breath of the Comet. Leadford lay with his cheek against a smooth flint, the useless pistol still hooked in his numb fingers. In his dream, if dream it was, he saw the Five Towns beneath a roof of green glass: the kilns were cold, the strikes were forgotten, and the bitter, crooked little pride that had driven him south with murder in his pocket melted away like tallow on a hot hob. He heard Nettie’s voice, very far off, calling his name—not in terror now, but gently, as she had called it when they were children gathering blackberries in the lane behind Clayton. Then the green dark took him altogether, and he slept without a single dream.

When he woke, the sun was up, and the world was washed clean. There was no green mist left in the sky, only a blue so deep and clear it made his eyes ache to look at it, like the glaze on the finest bone-china before it goes to the kiln. A lark was singing somewhere high above the downs, and the Channel lay flat as a mill-pond, sparkling with little white caps of gold.

Leadford sat up stiffly, brushing the dried kelp from his serge trousers. Beside him lay the cheap revolver. He picked it up, looked at the dull brass of the cartridges, and felt a sudden, quiet shame—not the hot, prickly shame of poverty, but the clean wonder of a man who looks at a toy he broke in a childish temper. He walked down to the water’s edge and flung the thing as far as he could into the tide.

Then he turned toward the white bungalow. Verrall was sitting on the low brick wall by the gate, his left arm bound in a clean linen handkerchief where the bullet had grazed him; Nettie stood beside him, her shawl drawn close against the morning air. Neither of them ran, nor did they shout. When Leadford reached the gate, Verrall looked up and nodded, his eyes as clear and untroubled as the sea behind him.

“You’re awake, then,” Verrall said quietly. “We were coming down to look for you.”

Leadford leaned his elbows on the timber gate and looked at Nettie—really looked at her, without the fever of ownership or the sting of his own pride. She was only a girl, tired and pale in the morning sun, and he saw now that he had never loved her so much as he had hated Verrall.

“There’s no war,” Leadford said, though he did not know how he knew it.

“No,” Verrall answered, gazing out toward the empty horizon where the cruisers had vanished. “None at all. Come inside, Leadford. The kettle’s on, and we’ve a deal to talk over.”
