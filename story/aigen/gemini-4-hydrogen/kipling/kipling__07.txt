The Five Towns lie under a blanket of their own making—fat, greasy coal-smoke from the pot-banks, grey steam from the winding-engines, and the sour reek of the canal where the barges nose through warm scum. William Leadford knew every brick of it, and hated every brick. He was twenty-two, a clerk at Rawdon’s pottery at eighteen shillings a week, with ink on his cuffs and a cough that never quite left his ribs. For two years he had walked out with Nettie Stuart along the cinder-paths by the slag-heaps, believing, as young men with empty pockets will, that a girl’s promise is forged like Sheffield steel. Then young Verrall came down from the Hall in his grey tweed motoring-coat, with a silver cigarette-case and a voice like warm butter. Nettie went. She left three lines on cheap ruled paper, and by Tuesday the neighbours were whispering behind their curtains. Leadford did not weep. He sat in his mother’s kitchen, watching the tea-leaves settle, while outside the window a new star hung above the blast-furnaces—a pale, wicked green, no bigger than a sixpence, yet bright enough to drown the Plough. At the street-corner, the newsboys were howling themselves hoarse about the German ultimatum and the Fleet coaling at Portsmouth. Leadford listened to the shouting, and felt the iron in his own chest grow cold. Europe might go to the devil in its own way; he had a private war to settle first.

He bought the revolver in Hanley, from a one-eyed Jew who kept trays of brass compasses and Sheffield blades behind a wire grille. It was a Belgian six-chambered Bulldog, nickelled once, now pitted like a diseased plum, and it cost Leadford his last sovereign and his silver watch. The dealer wiped the cylinder with an oily rag and asked no questions; in Staffordshire that week, men were buying stranger things than pocket-pistols. By noon Leadford was in a third-class smoker bound for the East Coast, where Verrall’s people kept a summer bungalow above the sea. The carriage was packed to the luggage-racks with reservists and sweating clerks, all talking at once. A corporal of the North Staffords sat opposite Leadford, nursing his kit-bag between his knees and explaining to a commercial traveller how the Big Guns would sink the Kaiser’s cruisers before breakfast. They passed round a bottle of lukewarm beer and a crumpled newspaper whose black headlines screamed of mobilisation. Leadford stared out at the flying hedgerows, saying nothing. His right hand stayed deep in his overcoat pocket, his thumb tracing the milled edge of the Bulldog’s hammer. Every mile the train clicked off seemed to tighten a wire inside his skull. He thought of Nettie’s small, white hands, and of Verrall laughing as he shifted gears on the steep hill out of Clayton, and the green star seemed to burn behind his eyelids even in the noon glare.

Shaphambury was a clean little town of red brick and whitewashed boarding-houses, built to catch the salt wind off the North Sea, though tonight the wind had dropped dead. Leadford stepped out of the station into a panic that smelled of hot horse-flesh and luggage-straps. The well-to-do were fleeing inland; dog-carts and motor-cars choked the narrow High Street, piled high with cabin-trunks and hat-boxes, while red-faced gentlemen shouted at porters who no longer cared to listen. Above the slate roofs, the comet had grown monstrous. It was no longer a star, but a blazing emerald sickle that filled half the eastern sky, turning the whitewash of the houses to the colour of verdigris and painting the faces of the crowd like drowned men. Near the esplanade, a boy was ringing a hand-bell and crying a late edition: the French frontier was crossed, and three British destroyers had been sighted off the Dogger Bank, steaming without lights. Leadford bought no paper. He turned his back on the station and walked north along the cliff-path, where the gorse grew thick and black against the sea. His boots were grey with Staffordshire dust, and his throat was dry as lime, yet he walked steadily. Half a mile past the last gas-lamp, the lights of a low timber bungalow shone yellow against the sickly green of the sky.

He crept through the wet gorse until the gravel of the bungalow’s terrace lay ten paces below him. The French windows stood wide to the sultry night, and a Japanese lantern swung unlit from the veranda beam. Inside, Verrall was pouring claret into two glasses. He wore white flannels and a blazer with a college crest, and he looked as easy and unhurried as though the world were not cracking along its seams. Nettie sat on the low wicker chair beside the table, her chin propped in her hand, staring out toward the black sea. When Verrall spoke to her, she turned her head and smiled—a small, frightened smile, yet full of the trust she had once given Leadford on the cinder-paths. The sight of it struck Leadford like a shovel between the shoulders. Far out beyond the headland, the night air shuddered—a dull, heavy thump-thump, twice repeated, like a carpet being beaten three parishes away. It was not thunder. Verrall froze with the decanter in his hand; Nettie’s hand flew to her throat. Somewhere out in the dark, the ironclads were speaking to one another in earnest. Leadford did not wait for the third shot. He stepped out of the gorse, his boots crunching loud on the shingle, and brought the Belgian revolver up into the green glare.

"Stand away from her, Verrall," Leadford said, and his voice sounded thin as tin against the hush of the sea. Nettie gave a short, sharp cry, half-rising from the wicker chair, her eyes wide and black in the comet’s light. Verrall set the decanter down without spilling a drop. He was no coward, whatever else he might be; he stepped squarely in front of the girl, his shoulders blocking the lamplight, and looked down the crooked barrel of the Bulldog. "Put that silly thing down, Leadford," he said quietly. "You’re drunk, or you’re mad. This isn’t the Potteries." Leadford’s finger took up the slack of the trigger. He could see the pulse beating in Verrall’s neck, just above the silk collar, and he knew he could not miss at ten paces. Then the wind came. It was not a gale, nor even a squall, but a long, shuddering sigh that swept in off the water, flattening the marram-grass against the sand. With it rolled a low bank of vapour, heavy as wet wool and glowing with a faint, shifting green, smelling sharply of bruised thyme and warm rain. Leadford tried to draw the trigger back, to finish the business before the fog took them, but his knuckle turned to lead. His knees buckled cleanly beneath him. He saw Verrall stagger sideways into the wicker table, saw Nettie sink down like a folded coat, and then the sweet, cold mist filled his mouth and laid him flat upon the gravel.

When Leadford woke, the sun was an hour above the sea, and the world had been washed with clean water. There was no smoke on the horizon, no green glare in the sky—only a high, pale blue that made his eyes ache with its clarity. He sat up slowly, brushing the gravel from his palms, and looked at the revolver lying beside his dusty boot. It seemed to him the strangest, meanest little lump of iron he had ever seen; he wondered, with a quiet, detached pity, what fevered fool had carried it three hundred miles to do murder. His chest felt light, as though a strap had been unbuckled from around his ribs. Ten feet away, Verrall was sitting up against the veranda post, rubbing his temples and blinking at the bright water. Nettie stirred beside the overturned wicker table, pushed the hair from her forehead, and looked straight at Leadford. There was no scream in her, and no shame, only the slow, wondering look of one waking from a long delirium. Leadford stood up, picked the Bulldog from the stones, and tossed it over the cliff-edge into the tide below. He walked across the terrace—not as a clerk, nor as a beaten man, nor as a lover—and held out his hand to help Verrall to his feet. Far down the coast, a morning train whistled, clear as a blackbird, and out across the empty North Sea the guns were altogether still.
