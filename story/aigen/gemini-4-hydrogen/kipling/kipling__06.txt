Leadford stood at the back gate of the yard in Clayton, where the pot-banks shouldered the grey sky and the sulphur-smoke lay low enough to taste on the tongue. In his coat pocket the Belgian revolver—bought for twelve shillings off a dealer in Hanbridge—knocked heavy against his hip-bone. It was a mean little tool, nickel-plated and loose in the cylinder, fit only for a mean man's errand; yet Leadford’s thumb knew every notch of its chequered grip.

Nettie had been gone three days. She had left no more than four lines on cheap ruled paper, smelling faintly of the lavender-water she bought at the draper’s, to say she was for the coast with young Verrall. Verrall, whose father owned half the pits in the valley and kept two motor-cars in a brick coach-house! That was the way of it: the gentry took the cream, and a clerk on twenty-two shillings a week might lick the empty pan and thank his Maker for the privilege.

Above the black chimney-stacks, the Comet hung like a bruised emerald in the dusk. Even the pit-lads had stopped throwing stones at stray dogs to stare up at it, their faces turned greenish-pale in the queer light. Down the cobbles of the High Street a newsboy went bawling through the fog: "Extra! German Fleet out! Mobilisation!"

Leadford spat into the gutter. Let the Emperors blow each other’s ironclads to scrap; he had his own war to settle before the world went up in smoke. He buttoned his threadbare ulster, felt the cold iron against his thigh, and set off down the hill toward the station.

The South-bound excursion train rattled through the dark like a tin kettle tied to a dog’s tail. In the third-class carriage, six men sat knee to knee on the bare wooden slats, breathing shag tobacco and damp worsted. Two were Reservists bound for Chatham—broad-necked lads from the Potteries who kept tapping their hobnailed boots on the floorboards and talking of the North Sea in the flat, unhurried voices of men who knew how to take orders.

"They say the cruisers are coalin' at Rosyth," said the taller one, nursing his canvas kit-bag between his knees. "My brother-in-law’s stoker on the Formidable. He wrote Tuesday that the stoke-holds are like hell’s kitchen."

"Let 'em coal," snapped a red-faced commercial traveller in the corner, his leather sample-case wedged tight against his shins. "Business is dead as mutton. Who’s going to buy Sheffield plate with that green devil staring down through the skylight?"

He jerked his thumb toward the soot-streaked window. Outside, the Midlands lay drowned under a strange, copperas-coloured twilight. The Comet’s tail had fanned out across half the heavens, shedding a cold, sour radiance over the slag-heaps, the canal locks, and the sleeping hedgerows.

Leadford said nothing. He kept his chin sunk in his collar and his right hand buried in his pocket, nursing his hatred as a fireman nurses a banked furnace. He was not thinking of the Kaiser’s cruisers, nor of the clerks in Whitehall scribbling telegrams; he was thinking of Verrall’s smooth, sunburnt neck and the way Nettie used to laugh when she pinned her Sunday hat on. The iron wheels clacked beneath him: Kill-him, kill-him, pay-him-out, mile after mile down the road toward the sea.

Shaphambury lay silent under the chalk cliff when Leadford stepped out of the station into a wind that tasted of salt and wet shingle. There was no coal-smoke here to blot the sky; the Comet burned overhead like a great green brazier, turning the whitewash of the lodging-houses to the colour of mouldering cheese and throwing shadows as sharp as knife-cuts across the empty parade.

He knew where to look. A porter at the station-inn, bribed with sixpence and a glass of bitter, had pointed him along the cliff-path toward the timbered bungalows above the bay—the sort of places rich folk hired for a month to play at living simple.

Leadford walked softly on the turf beside the gravel. His boots were worn thin at the heels, and the night damp seeped through to his socks, yet he felt neither cold nor fatigue—only a tight, singing dryness in his throat. Presently he saw the low red roof of Sea-View crouched behind a hedge of tamarisk. A warm yellow lamp still burned behind the drawn blinds of the parlour. Once, a man’s laugh drifted out across the little garden, easy and careless, followed by the faint clink of a glass.

Leadford crouched in the gorse above the low flint wall, his breath coming short through his teeth. He drew the Belgian revolver and spun the cylinder once with his thumb—click, click, click—to make sure the cheap brass cartridges sat square. The green glare from the sky lay heavy on the oil-black sea below, and far out beyond the headland a dull thud shook the air—once, twice—like heavy guns firing out in the Channel. Leadford did not turn his head. He settled his elbows on the flint coping and waited for the door to open.

False dawn came not with red or grey, but with a sickly, shimmering emerald that crept across the water like spilled oil. The air had grown dead still; not a gull cried along the shingle, and the tamarisk leaves hung motionless as painted tin.

At five by the church clock in the valley, the bungalow door clicked open. Verrall came out first, bareheaded, wearing a flannel jacket over his shirt, with Nettie close behind him in her blue serge skirt. Both of them were staring up at the eastern sky, where the Comet’s core now hung low and monstrous above the horizon, pulsing like a struck gong.

"Good God," Verrall muttered, "look at the colour of the sea—"

Leadford vaulted the low wall. His boot crunched on the gravel, and he stood ten paces from them, the pistol levelled in both shaking hands.

Nettie gave a little choked cry—"Willie!"—and caught at Verrall’s sleeve. To give the devil his due, Verrall did not flinch. He stepped cleanly in front of the girl, his jaw hardening in the way of his kind when cornered.

"Put that thing down, you fool," said Verrall steadily. "You’ll do no good with that."

"I’ll do what’s owed!" Leadford shouted, though his voice cracked in the dead air. "You’ve—"

He pulled the trigger. The hammer fell with a sharp snap—a misfire on the cheap Belgian cap—and before he could cock it again, the wind came. It was not a gale, but a silent, towering wall of green vapour rolling in off the Channel, smelling sharply of bruised mint and warm iron. Leadford tried to draw breath to curse them, but his knees turned to water. The pistol slipped from his numb fingers, Nettie sank down against the porch, and the green dark folded over the three of them like a heavy blanket.

When Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was an hour high—a clean, white-gold sun such as he had never seen in Staffordshire, shining through an air washed bright as crystal. A lark was singing somewhere high above the cliff-top, and the sea below broke in crisp, silver-edged rollers along the shingle.

He sat up stiffly on the gravel. His ulster was soaked with a sweet-smelling dew, and his head felt curiously light, as though a man had taken a stiff broom and swept a decade of soot and cobwebs out of the rafters of his skull. Beside his boot lay the Belgian revolver, its nickel plating dulled with moisture. Leadford picked it up, looked at the miserable little toy, and wondered, with a sudden, quiet shame, what manner of madman had carried it two hundred miles to spill blood on a clean morning. He broke the breech, shook the six dull cartridges into his palm, and tossed both cartridges and pistol far out into the thickets of gorse.

Then he looked toward the porch. Nettie was sitting on the wooden step, her chin in her hands, gazing out across the bay. A yard away, Verrall leaned against the flint wall, staring down at his own soft, manicured palms as if he had never properly seen them before. Neither of them looked afraid; they had the dazed, grave faces of children woken too early from a bad dream.

Leadford stood up, brushed the gravel from his knees, and walked slowly across the path toward them.

"Are you hurt, Nettie?" Leadford asked. His own voice sounded strange to him—steady and quiet, stripped of the old, whining bitterness.

She looked up, and for the first time in her life she did not drop her eyes before him. "No, Willie," she said simply. "Only—everything seems very small and far away. Even yesterday."

Verrall turned his head. The easy, careless arrogance was gone from his mouth, leaving only a young man who looked suddenly aware that he had never done a stroke of honest work in his twenty-four years. "You meant to shoot me, Leadford," he said, without heat. "And I suppose, by the old reckoning, you had your reasons. We were all half-poisoned by the dark we lived in."

"Aye," said Leadford. "Poisoned is the word. The smoke gets into the blood up North, and a man thinks his own spite is the whole world."

Down in the valley, the church bells of Shaphambury began to ring—not the frantic clamour of an alarm, but a slow, measured peal that carried clear across the cliffs. Along the coast road, two soldiers in khaki had stopped by a broken cart, laughing as they helped an old carter heave a fallen wheel back onto its axle; there was no talk of the North Sea now, nor of Emperors, for the same clean wind had blown across the Channel and left the fleets idle at their anchors.

Leadford looked at Nettie, and then at Verrall, and felt no pang at all—only a great, quiet pity for the blindness they had all shared.

"Keep the girl safe, Verrall," Leadford said, holding out his hand. "And learn a trade, if you can. There’s a deal of rubbish to clear away up in Staffordshire, and I’d best be catching the morning train."

Verrall gripped his hand hard. Leadford tipped his cap to Nettie, turned through the tamarisk gate, and stepped out briskly onto the white road toward the waking town.
