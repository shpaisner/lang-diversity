In the Potteries, where the marl-pits gape like old wounds and the bottle-kilns breathe soot into the wash-tubs, a man learns early to keep his teeth shut and his grievances warm. William Leadford was twenty-two, a clerk in a tile-works at Clayton, with ink on his cuff, coal-dust in his pores, and a mind soured by cheap pamphlets and a narrower bed than he liked. Nettie had been his—promised, as good as, by the reckoning of the cinder-paths behind the allotments. Then came young Verrall from the big house up the hill, with his Norfolk jacket, his leather-smelling motor-car, and that easy, brassy laugh that buys what it has not earned. Nettie was gone by the Tuesday mail, and Willie had six-and-sixpence left of his wages after he bought the Belgian revolver in Hanley. It was a squat, pitted piece of iron, foul-smelling at the muzzle, but it clicked true enough when he thumbed the hammer in the dark of his attic. Outside his narrow sash, the sky was no longer honest Staffordshire black. A new star had climbed above the slag-heaps—a smeared, verdigris blotch with a tail like a sweeping broom of pale fire. The old men at the tap-room said it meant blight on the turnips; the papers from London said it meant nothing at all, being too busy shrieking about the German fleet and the mobilisation of the reserves. Willie cared nothing for the Kaiser’s ironclads. He oiled his six-and-sixpenny pistol with mutton-fat, wrapped it in a red handkerchief, and waited for the morning train.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool, shag tobacco, and the sour breath of men who had read the morning editions too fast. All the way down from Crewe to the southern junction, the talk ran on one rail: ultimatum, cruiser, blockade, and the price of bacon. A corporal of Marines in the corner kept tapping his kit-bag with a knobby stick, telling the world how the North Sea would be swept clean before Michaelmas. Willie sat with his knees jammed against a drummer’s sample-case, his hand inside his coat pocket, his thumb resting on the cold checkering of the Belgian grip. He watched the hedgerows whip past—green enough, though the sunlight had a queer, jaundiced cast to it, as if the world were viewed through a dirty chemist’s bottle. Every station platform was choked with lads in ill-fitting khaki and women with red eyes who did not know whether to cheer or to howl. Europe was slipping her cable, drifting broadside-on toward the reefs, and the fools in the carriage cheered the tide. Willie despised them all. They were drunk on printer’s ink and brass bands; he, at least, had a clean, private business of his own. Nettie had written three lines from a boarding-house near the chalk cliffs at Shaphambury—three lines of thin, frightened ink telling him not to follow, which was the surest way to bring him. He stepped out onto the gravel at the coast terminus as the dusk came down, not purple and salt-clean as it ought to be, but stained with a sickly, copperish glow from the zenith.

Shaphambury sat high on the white chalk, all stucco terraces and plate-glass windows looking out over a sea that looked like melted pewter. By nine o’clock the Comet had half the sky to itself, a monstrous, silent fan of green light that turned the foam on the shingle to stale absinthe. Out beyond the headland, two dark smudges lay on the water—destroyers, by the lean rake of their funnels, winking red and white at each other with their masthead lamps before they blacked out altogether. Somewhere far to the eastward, beyond the horizon’s rim, there came a dull, double thump that rattled the panes in the bathing-machines. The war had begun, or was clearing its throat to begin. Willie did not look twice at the sea. He had found the big hotel on the Esplanade, and he had seen Verrall’s motor-car cooling in the yard, its brass lamps winking green. He waited in the tamarisk bushes by the cliff-walk, his boots caked with Staffordshire clay that had no business on southern turf. His throat was dry as lime-kiln ash, and his heart beat in short, ugly strokes against his ribs. Every time a gravelled footstep crunched on the path, he lifted the pistol an inch clear of his pocket. He was not afraid of the gallows; a man who has breathed kiln-smoke for twenty years does not fear a drop of six feet. He only wanted to see Verrall’s face lose its easy, gentlemanly set—to see Nettie understand that a poor man’s pride is not a thing to be left behind like an old pair of clogs.

They came up from the beach-steps together just as the town clocks struck ten, though the chimes sounded muffled and small under that vast, humming canopy of green. Verrall had a white silk scarf around his throat; he was flicking ash from a cigarette, talking in that level, unhurried drawl that Willie hated worse than a blow. Nettie walked a pace behind, her shawl pulled tight, her face looking small and pinched in the unnatural light. Willie stepped out from the tamarisks onto the white chalk path. He did not shout. He simply cocked the hammer; the clack-clack was sharp as a snapped bone. Verrall stopped dead, the cigarette dropping from his fingers to smoulder in the grass. Nettie gave a little choked cry—not his name, Willie noticed, not Willie, just a sound like a trapped rabbit. "Stand clear of her," Willie said, and his voice sounded thin and cracked in his own ears. Verrall’s hand went up, palm outward, the old trick of the master speaking to the striker. "Put that down, Leadford. Don't be a fool—there's war tonight, man, the world's gone mad enough." "She's mine," Willie spat, and brought the barrel up until the notch caught the green shine of Verrall's shirt-front. Above them, the Comet’s tail seemed to shudder and stoop, pouring a sudden, blinding cataract of emerald fire across the stars. Down in the bay, a coastal battery opened with a single, tearing roar that shook the chalk beneath their boots—and then, before the echo could roll back from the cliffs, the air itself grew thick and heavy, like warm oil.

Willie’s forefinger tightened on the iron, yet the shot never broke. It was as though a great, soft hand had been laid across his mouth and nostrils—a smell of crushed mint, and ozone, and the sharp, clean tang of a thunderstorm that never breaks. The green vapor was falling from the zenith, not like fog from the sea, but in silent, swirling curtains that blotted out the hotel lamps and the winking destroyers alike. Willie tried to curse, tried to hold the foresight steady on the white silk scarf, but his knees turned to wet tow. Verrall was swaying where he stood, his aristocratic chin dropping onto his breast; Nettie sank down onto the turf without a sound, her shawl spreading like a dark wing around her. Willie’s pistol slipped from his numb fingers and clattered against a flint. He fell forward onto his hands, his lungs drinking in the strange, sweet air in great, greedy gulps. There was no pain in it, only a vast, roaring quietude, as if all the engines in Staffordshire and all the turbines in the Fleet had been shut off at the main valve together. Down on the Esplanade, a cab-horse whinnied once and was still; out by the headland, the searchlight beam that had been sweeping the mist wavered, dipped into the pewter sea, and went out. The world—the dirty, greedy, shouting world of tile-works and ironclads and broken promises—turned over on its side in the wet grass and went to sleep.

When Willie Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was an hour above the eastern sea, and the sky was the clean, washed blue of a child’s paint-box. There was no green smear left in the heavens, no smoke on the horizon, and no sickness in his blood. He sat up slowly, feeling his ribs; he felt as a man does after a long fever has broken in the night—light-headed, hollow, and absurdly clear-eyed. Beside his boot lay the Belgian revolver, already speckled with orange rust from the heavy dew. He picked it up, looked at the pitted barrel, and tossed it over the edge of the cliff; he heard it strike the shingle far below with a small, foolish clink. Ten paces off, Verrall was sitting with his back against a tamarisk stump, staring at his own white hands as if he had never seen them before. Between them, Nettie knelt in the grass, her hair unbound, weeping—not the sharp, frightened weeping of last night, but quiet, easy tears that ran down her cheeks while she smiled at the sea. Willie walked over to them. There was no rage left in him, no more than there is heat in yesterday’s clinker; he looked at Verrall and saw only another bewildered man in a crumpled coat, and at Nettie and saw a girl who had been afraid of the dark. "We were fools," Willie said, and his voice was steady and strange to him. Verrall looked up, blinking in the bright salt air, and nodded slowly. Out in the Channel, a grey cruiser lay drifting with the tide, her funnels cold as stone, and all along the white coast the great silence held, waiting for men to learn what to do with the morning.
