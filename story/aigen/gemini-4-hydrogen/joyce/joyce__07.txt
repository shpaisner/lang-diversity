The soot of the Potteries lay thick upon the sill, blacking the thumb where William Leadford pressed it, and down in the hollow the bottle-kilns belched their grey-brown prayers into a sky already bruised with green. Three pounds ten, his ledger had said, and notice given for the clerkship. Let them keep their ink-pots and their bowed backs. In his coat pocket the cold snout of the six-chambered revolver knocked against his hipbone at every stride, a hard iron tongue waiting to speak for him. Nettie. Her name came up like bile and honey together in his throat. Nettie with her little gloved hands and her eyes that looked past his frayed cuffs toward the white dust of a motorcar. Verrall, she had written on the half-sheet of blue paper, he is kind to me, Willie, you must not think hard of us. Not think hard! Beneath the gas-lamp by the canal, where the slag-heaps rose like the ribs of a starved beast, he stopped and spat into the scummy water. Overhead, past the drifting fleece of coal-smoke, the great star hung—no honest white candle of the Lord, but a swollen, sickly emerald, trailing a smudge of phosphor across the dark. Let it fall, he thought, his fingers tightening on the milled grip in his pocket. Let the whole rotten, greasy world crack like a faulty plate in the firing, and Verrall with his silver cigarette-case under the rubble.

Down High Street the newsboys were howling like gulls over offal. WAR! ULTIMATUM! BRITISH SQUADRON SAILS! Yellow placards flapped against the wet brick of the tobacconist’s, and inside the Three Crowns the colliers were banging their pewter pots, red-necked and loud with someone else’s courage. Give the Kaiser a taste of Sheffield steel, one roared, though his own lungs rattled like dry peas in a bladder. William shouldered past them, his collar turned up against the drizzle that smelled faintly, tonight, of bruised grass and copper. Steel, he muttered to the cobbles. Always steel for the likes of us, and silk for the likes of him. A stout gentleman in a fur-lined ulster paused by the post-office pillar to consult a gold hunter, glancing up once at the green glare that turned his plump jowls the colour of mouldy tallow. Damn them, William thought, his heart hammering a dry, feverish march against his ribs. They’ll march the lads off to drown in the North Sea while young Verrall takes his ease by the Hampshire cliffs, kissing the hollow of her throat. He bought a halfpenny Sentinel only to crush it in his fist; the wet ink stained his palm, black as his own bile. Everywhere men’s faces were turned upward now, pale and pinched in the emerald wash, waiting for the guns to speak in the east, waiting for the sky to drop its cold fire.

Clack-a-clack, clack-a-clack, the third-class carriage shook his teeth in his head as the night express tore southward through the sleeping shires. Opposite him, a commercial traveller snored with his mouth wide, his bowler hat tipped over his nose, a cadaverous shadow in the greenish gloom that leaked through the soot-filmed glass. William stared at his own reflection in the pane—hollow-cheeked, wild-eyed, the hair damp on his forehead. Nettie, the wheels sang, Nettie and Verrall, silk and the devil. He remembered the Sunday by the Trent, the cheap muslin she wore, the way she had laughed when a beetle dropped into her teacup, her throat bare and warm in the sun. Had she laughed like that for him in the big house? Had she laid her small ear against his broadcloth coat and forgotten the smell of the clay-pits and the sour cabbage in his mother’s kitchen? She is mine, his blood beat, mine by every cold night I walked to see her light go out, mine by every shilling I saved for a ring she never let me buy. His hand crept again into his pocket, stroking the cold, oiled cylinder. A station lamp flashed by—Bletchley—and a porter’s face, upturned and terrified beneath the great trailing plume in the zenith. Soon the sea. Soon the chalk downs and the white gate she had described in her foolish, cruel letter. Outside, the hedgerows and telegraph poles fled past, drenched in that uncanny green, as though England had sunk ten fathoms deep beneath a silent sea.

The salt wind off the Channel bit through his thin serge coat, carrying the rattle of shingle and the long, hollow sigh of the tide. Up on the cliff-path, where the gorse hunched dark against the luminous sky, the bungalow crouched behind its tamarisk hedge, two yellow squares of lamplight glowing warm against the green night. William stood by the white gate, his breath coming short and ragged. Through the uncurtained sash he saw them. Verrall stood by the mantelpiece, slim in his evening black, pouring something amber into a glass; and there, in the low wicker chair, was Nettie, her dark hair loose about her shoulders, her face turned up to him with a little, hesitant smile. A red mist swam before William’s eyes, hotter than the furnace-mouths of Hanley. He pushed the gate; the latch clicked sharp as a cocked hammer. He stepped onto the damp turf, drawing the revolver free, the barrel gleaming dull and oily in the comet’s ghastly noon. Now, he whispered, now let the rich man pay. As he raised his arm, a strange, cool vapour came rolling up from the sea-wall, thicker than mist, sweet as bruised apples and wet clover, wrapping his ankles, rising to his chest, heavy and soft as a woman’s sleeve. Across the water, far out toward the invisible French coast, a dull boom trembled in the air—or was it only the blood drumming in his own ears? The green cloud swallowed the garden, blinding the yellow windows, cool upon his burning eyelids.

He tried to pull the trigger, to force his stiff forefinger back against the iron curve, to shatter the glass and the smugness and the bitter world together. Hate, he told himself, remember the cold attic, remember the laughter. Yet his arm felt weighted with lead, and the fierce, tight knot behind his breastbone was loosening, unravelling like a wet rag in the current. The sweet green breath filled his nostrils, deep and clean, washing away the coal-dust and the sourness of thirty narrow years. Somewhere in the dark, Verrall’s voice called out—not in anger, not in fear, only a puzzled, drowsy murmur—and then a glass chimed once upon the floor and was still. William’s knees gave way beneath him. He sank into the long, dew-drenched grass beside the tamarisk, the revolver sliding from his slack palm to lie harmless among the daisies. It is the end, he thought, without terror. Nettie’s shadow swayed once at the window, then vanished, as though she too had lain down in a quiet room. Above the mist, the great green star burned serene and vast, watching the little earth grow still. And far away, beyond the chalk cliffs and the rolling grey Channel, the same green tide was pouring through the portholes of great ironclads, silencing the stokers at the furnaces, laying the gunners gently down beside their breeches; it was drifting over the dusty roads of Flanders, over the sleeping parliaments and the black, quiet chimneys of Staffordshire, folding every hot, clenched fist into the open palm of sleep.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the green terror had gone from the sky, and a clean, gold-washed morning stood over the sea. A lark was singing somewhere high in the blue, a silver thread of sound, and the air tasted as air had never tasted in his memory—light, sharp, and wholly free of smoke. He sat up. Beside him in the grass lay the little black revolver, ugly and absurd as a dead beetle; he looked at it with a quiet wonder, unable to recall the heat that had made him carry such a thing across half of England. He rose, brushing the damp earth from his trousers, and walked toward the open door of the bungalow. Verrall was sitting on the low stone step, his black coat rumpled, his face pale and stripped of its old, easy insolence, staring out at the bright water. Nettie stood behind him in the doorway, her hand resting lightly on the lintel. When she saw William, she did not shriek or shrink back; only a deep, lucid sorrow, clear as spring water, passed across her eyes. Willie, she said softly, and there was no tremor of deceit in her voice, nor any triumph in Verrall’s as he looked up. We have all been mad, I think, William said, and his own voice sounded strange and gentle to him, like a stranger’s heard across a quiet field. He sat down upon the warm grass a little way from them, his hands resting open on his knees, while below the cliffs the white-fringed tide whispered against the shingle, and the new world, washed clean of its old fever and its old pride, lay open and silent beneath the sun.
