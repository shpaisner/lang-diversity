Sootfall on the slate roofs of Clayton, grey upon grey, and the kilns belching their sour sulphur into a sky that had forgotten the colour of blue. William Leadford stood by the scullery window, his knuckles raw from the crate-packing, watching the cinder-heaps smoke like cold altars. Inside, his mother’s cough rattled dryly in the grate-ash, a thin, scraping music of poverty. Two shillings short on the rent again, and Mr. Rawdon’s agent coming Friday with his waxed moustache and his little black ledger. Let them choke on their ledgers. He fingered the dog-eared pamphlet in his coat pocket, the ink smudged by his own sweat: The Awakening of Labour. Fine words, yes, fat words for thin bellies. Above the jagged silhouette of the bottle-kilns, something hung in the bruised twilight—not a star, not the pale, indifferent moon, but a smeared thumbprint of green fire. The comet. Old women in the alleyways crossed themselves and muttered of judgment; the colliers spat into the gutter and cursed the price of bacon. What did a wanderer in the void care for Staffordshire muck? Yet it burned there, a cold emerald eye watching the slag-heaps, watching William’s narrow chest heave with a nameless, sour bile. He turned from the glass. The teapot sat cold on the oilcloth. Nowhere to go, nothing to own, save the fierce, cramped furnace of his own skull and the memory of Nettie’s hair smelling of cheap lavender soap in the lane behind the gasworks.

She was gone. Three words scrawled on a torn scrap of grocer’s paper, smelling faintly of butter: Forgive me, Will. Forgive? A word for priests and well-fed magistrates. He walked the greasy cobbles down toward the High Street, his boots letting in the mire, his mind a wheel turning endlessly over the same sharp stone. Verrall. Young Master Verrall with his grey flannel trousers and his father’s ironworks, his motorcar spitting blue reek down the lanes, scattering the hens and the barefoot children. How easy it was to buy a girl’s wonder with a silk scarf and a box of sugared almonds! Nettie, who had sworn by the canal bridge that she’d wait till he’d saved enough for two rooms and a dresser. Liar, soft-eyed little liar, leaning now against Verrall’s tweed shoulder on some plush train bound for the southern coast, her laugh ringing out like silver coins on a counter. At Barker’s pawnshop, behind the brass balls and the tray of tarnished watches, the old man slid the Belgian revolver across the baize. Seven shillings and sixpence. Cold, squat, ugly as a toad, its cylinder oiled and waiting. William dropped it into his pocket; the weight dragged his coat sideways, comforting as a clenched fist. A man with nothing had at least the power to end a thing. Let Verrall keep his motorcar and his fine manners; lead knew no distinction between broadcloth and fustian. The green star pulsed above the telegraph wires, brighter now, casting a sickly, phosphor sheen over the wet slates.

Special Edition! Ultimatum Expired! Fleet Sails! The newsboy’s shriek tore through the steam of the Junction platform, raw as a gull’s cry over offal. Men in bowler hats snatched the damp sheets, their faces tightening into masks of solemn, stupid pride. War. The word buzzed in the carriage like a trapped blowfly as the train clanked southward through the darkening shires. Across from William, a commercial traveller with a ginger beard tapped his pipe against his boot-heel and declared the Germans needed a bloody good thrashing, aye, before harvest. William said nothing, staring out at the flying hedgerows, the black silhouettes of elms against a sky turned strangely luminous, a vast, pale sea of verdigris. Europe was slipping its leash, millions of dull, obedient cattle marching toward the slaughterhouse while the bands played and the bishops blessed the bayonets. Madness—the same hot, blind madness that hammered behind his own temples, urging his fingers toward the cold iron in his pocket. Why shouldn’t the world burn? Let the cities crack like pots in an overheated kiln; let the kings and the Verralls and the smug shopkeepers choke in the smoke. The train’s wheels sang a fierce, rhythmic litany over the iron joints: kill them, kill them, kill them all. Through the grime of the carriage window, the comet’s tail unfurled across half the heavens, a ghostly broom sweeping the stars aside, bathing the sleeping pastures in an eerie, breathless light that made the grass look like tarnished silver.

Shaphambury smelled of brine, horse-dung, and expensive cigars. Along the esplanade, the gas-lamps struggled weakly against the pervasive, emerald twilight that poured down from the zenith. William’s heels ground the fine gravel of the cliff-walk, his Staffordshire coat grey with railway dust among the white flannels and rustling muslins of the seaside gentry. Every laugh from the veranda of the Grand Hotel was a needle in his ear. There—by the stone balustrade overlooking the dark, oily heave of the Channel—a flash of pale blue silk. Nettie. Her small hand rested on the sleeve of a cream-coloured jacket, her face tilted up toward the monstrous, glowing fan of the comet. Beside her, Verrall struck a match, the brief orange flare illuminating his smooth, untroubled jaw, his careless smile. How clean they looked, how effortlessly whole, as though the soot of the Midlands did not exist, as though men were not dying of hunger in the black alleys behind the potteries. William’s hand closed around the butt of the Belgian pistol. His heart beat in his throat, thick and salt. Not anger merely, no, something deeper, a scalding humiliation that demanded blood to wash it clean. He stepped into the shadow of the tamarisk bushes, watching them descend the zigzag path toward the lonely shingle below the cliffs. The sea whispered hushed, treacherous syllables on the stones. No one near. Only the vast green eye of heaven watching, cold and indifferent as a rich man’s stare.

He caught them by the breakwater where the tide hissed through the weed-hung timbers. Nettie gasped, her hand flying to her throat, her eyes wide and dark in the uncanny, emerald dusk. Will! Verrall stepped forward instinctively, broad-shouldered, blocking her, his voice sharp with the habit of command: See here, Leadford, what madness is this? Madness. Yes, the word tasted sweet as copper on William’s tongue. He drew the revolver. The barrel trembled, pointing at Verrall’s white waistcoat, at the smug, unearned ease of him. You stole her, William croaked, his voice cracked from the train-smoke and the long, bitter silence of his life. You have everything, and you stole the one thing— A flash, a flat, puny crack that the sea swallowed instantly. Sand kicked up by Verrall’s boot. William pulled the trigger again, cursing the stiff spring, his eyes stinging—not with tears, no, with a sudden, dizzying mist that seemed to rise from the very shingle. A sweet, heavy scent, like crushed almonds and summer clover after rain, rolled across the water. Above them, the green veil of the comet’s tail touched the upper air, dissolving in a silent, violet-green cascade of light. The revolver slipped from William’s numb fingers, clattering on the stones. Verrall swayed, clutching at the air, his face slackening into sudden, childlike wonder. Nettie sank gently to her knees upon the pebbles, her lids fluttering shut. William’s legs folded beneath him; the dark tide of sleep rose warm and fragrant over his rage, washing away the world.

Morning broke without a cloud, yet the light was not the old, harsh glare of yesterday. It was a washed, crystalline gold, tender as the first hour of creation. William sat up on the shingle, his palm pressed against the cool, wet stones, and drew a breath so deep it seemed to rinse the very marrow of his bones. Where was the sour knot in his chest? Where the cramped, venomous heat that had driven him across half of England with murder in his pocket? Gone—shed like a filthy, outgrown coat. Beside him, the little pistol lay rusted with salt-dew, an absurd, pathetic toy. A few paces off, Verrall stirred, pushing himself up on one elbow, blinking at the dazzling sea. Their eyes met. There was no hatred in Verrall’s gaze, only a quiet, puzzled clarity, as though he were seeing a brother for the first time after a long, fevered dream. Nettie woke with a soft sigh, smoothing her crumpled skirt, looking from one man to the other without fear, without coquetry, her face luminous in the pure air. Far out on the horizon, a grey dreadnought lay motionless on the glassy water, its guns silent, its crew perhaps standing on the decks in the same stunned, merciful hush. William rose and offered Verrall his hand. The rich man took it, his grip firm and warm. They stood together on the clean sand, three small figures under an immense, forgiving sky, knowing without words that the old world of cages and masters and bitter, grasping love had passed away in the night.
