Grey slag and the sour reek of the kilns. William Leadford stood by the cinder-path where the marl-pits yawned black under a smeared sky, his knuckles raw from the ledger-desk, his boots weighted with the greasy clay of Staffordshire. Behind him, the bottle-ovens belched their eternal soot over the slate roofs of Clayton, choking the stunted hawthorns, settling like a shroud on his mother's sill. She was indoors now, coughing into her apron, boiling tea-leaves a third time in the chipped brown pot. And Nettie was gone. Gone with Verrall, that sleek-haired pup of the gentry, with his soft-palmed hands and his pockets full of unearned silver. William spat into the nettles. The thought of her—her quick dark eyes, the warm curve of her nape beneath the cheap straw hat, the faint scent of lavender soap—turned in his belly like a jagged shard of potsherd. To think she had let him kiss her by the canal towpath, had whispered his name in the damp dusk while the barges slid by like shadows, only to trot after a silk waistcoat and a motor-car's brass horn! Upward, above the jagged chimney-pots, the sky was bruised a sickly, luminous green. The comet. Men in the taprooms muttered of it over their flat ale, calling it a portent, a blister on the firmament, a sign of the wrath to come. Let it fall, he thought, clenching his fist in his coat pocket where the cold iron lay snug. Let it burn the whole rotten hive to clinker.

The highroad south was a ribbon of white dust and horse-dung, bordered by hedges grey with summer blight. William walked with a stiff, deliberate stride, the cheap Belgian revolver knocking against his thigh with every step—thump, thump—like a second, colder heart. At the crossroads by the tavern, a boy in a ragged jersey was bawling the noon edition, waving a damp pink sheet in the hot air. ULTIMATUM! FLEET SAILS AT DAWN! The men leaning against the brick wall did not look up from their pipes; they had the dull, bullock-eyed patience of the Potteries, bred to be fed to the furnaces or the trenches, whichever the masters chose first. Europe was piling up its powder-kegs, Kaiser and King rattling their tin sabres while the clerks sweated and the women wept. Well, let them slaughter one another in the mud of Flanders or the North Sea. William had his own war to wage, small and sharp and personal. He saw them in his mind's eye, laughing over cold chicken and claret in some seaside boarding-house, Verrall's white teeth flashing, Nettie's hand resting lightly on his sleeve. Whore, his mind hissed, and then immediately recoiled, sickened by its own venom. Not a whore—only a foolish, starved bird dazzled by a bright cage. Yet the iron in his pocket demanded its reckoning. Justice, the pamphlets called it, or vengeance; in the green-tinted glare of the afternoon, the two words tasted alike, metallic and bitter as copper on the tongue.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, tarred rope, and fried plaice, a clean, brisk world that mocked the grime beneath his fingernails. Down the long esplanade, where the painted bathing-machines stood in neat rows like wooden coffins, the holiday folk strolled in their flannels and muslin, chattering of the German cruisers and the strange greenness creeping over the sun. William kept to the shadows of the tamarisk hedges along the cliff-walk, his eyes narrowed, scanning every parasol and straw boater. His throat was parched; he had eaten nothing since dawn save a crust of dry bread. Then, near the gorse bushes where the path dipped toward the shingle, he saw them. Verrall was pointing out to sea with his malacca cane, his linen suit immaculate against the dun-coloured grass, and Nettie stood beside him, her face tilted up, the wind catching a loose strand of her dark hair. A sudden, blinding rage, hot as kiln-fire, surged behind William's eyes. He stepped out onto the gravel, his boots crunching loud in the salt stillness. "Nettie!" His voice cracked, harsh and provincial, a crow's caw among songbirds. They turned together. Nettie's hand flew to her mouth, her eyes widening with a terror that stabbed him deeper than her scorn; Verrall merely stiffened, his smooth brow furrowing in mild, aristocratic annoyance, stepping half a pace before her as though to shield a lapdog from a stray cur.

"Stand back, Verrall," William breathed, and the revolver was in his hand, ugly and blunt in the queer, emerald twilight. The sea below had turned the colour of stagnant pond-water; the gulls hung motionless on the updraught, silent as painted birds. "I've come for what's mine, or to settle the score between us." Verrall did not flinch, though his cheek paled beneath its tan. "Put that down, Leadford, you fool. You're half-mad." "William, don't!" Nettie cried, her voice trembling like a plucked wire. He raised the barrel, sighting along the notched metal at Verrall's waistcoat, right where the gold watch-chain gleamed. His finger tightened on the trigger. Then the sky exhaled. It was not a sound, nor a wind, but a vast, velvety sigh that seemed to rise from the very ribs of the earth. A scent washed over the cliffs—sweet, penetrating, like crushed clover and warm rain after a long drought—and with it fell a sudden, fathomless languor. William's arm grew heavy as lead. The hatred in his chest, that tight, knotted fist of iron, seemed to uncurl, finger by finger, dissolving into a strange, luminous mist. The revolver slipped from his numb grasp and thudded softly into the thrift. Across the grass, Nettie swayed and sank gently to her knees, her eyelids fluttering shut; beside her, Verrall crumpled without a word, his cane rolling down the slope. William dropped beside them, his cheek against the cool turf, watching the great green veil of the comet sweep silently across the stars.

He woke to a morning of impossible clarity. The air tasted like cold spring-water, sharp and pure, as though the lungs of the world had been rinsed of centuries of soot and fever. William sat up slowly, rubbing his eyes, and looked at his hands. The coal-grime was still wedged beneath the nails, the cuffs of his coat were still frayed, yet he felt as if he had stepped out of a cramped, airless cellar into an open meadow. Beside him in the grass lay the Belgian revolver, its blued steel dull in the sunlight. He picked it up, turned it over once, and tossed it carelessly over the cliff-edge; he heard no splash, only the faintest clink against the shingle far below. How absurd it seemed now, that little tube of iron, that frantic, strutting ego that had carried it three hundred miles to spill blood! He thought of the boys shouting of war at the crossroads, of the grey battleships steaming through the Channel, of the ministers in their frock-coats signing away the lives of Staffordshire lads—and a vast, quiet pity welled up in him, clear as tears. They had all been walking in their sleep, drugged by pride and hunger and fear, striking out in the dark at shadows they took for enemies. Now the sun was up. The great green broom had swept the cobwebs from the rafters of the mind, leaving only the plain, unadorned truth of things: that men were brothers, and the earth was wide enough for all.

A low murmur stirred the grass beside him. Nettie was sitting up, her dark hair tangled with dry bent, blinking in the bright salt glare. A few paces off, Verrall rose onto one elbow, looking about him with the bewildered, innocent gaze of a child waking in a strange bed. Neither of them shrank when William stood and walked toward them; the old wall of shame and class and fury had crumbled in the night like unmortared brick. "William," Nettie said softly, and there was no terror in her voice, no coquetry, only a quiet, wondering recognition. He knelt beside her, taking her hand—not to claim it, not to bruise it with possession, but simply to feel the warm pulse of another living soul. Verrall came over and sat upon the turf opposite them, his immaculate linen creased and stained with grass, his face stripped of its old, careless insolence. "We were mad," Verrall said quietly, looking out over the glittering water where the white sails of fishing-smacks were beginning to dot the bay. "All of us. Yesterday I thought of nothing but myself, and my own pleasure. And the papers—the guns—" He shook his head, marveling. "It is over," William said. He looked northward, in his mind's eye seeing the smoke-stacks of Clayton, his mother's small kitchen, the grey rows of cottages where the men were waking now, rubbing their eyes in the clear light, wondering why they had ever hated one another. There would be work to do, clay to mould and slums to tear down, a whole world to build anew from the ashes of the old. But for this hour, sitting together on the cliff-edge above the singing sea, the morning was enough.
