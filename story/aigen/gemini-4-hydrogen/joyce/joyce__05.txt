The kilns of Swadlincote and Hanley breathed their soot into the low Staffordshire sky, a grey woollen shroud that tasted of sulphur and damp ash. William Leadford walked with his shoulders hunched against the drizzle, his boots clacking over the greasy cobbles of the alley. Three shillings and sixpence in his pocket, a frayed collar chafing his neck, and in his breast a cold, heavy stone where his heart ought to be. Nettie. Her name repeated itself against the iron rhythm of the tram-bells, a mocking chime. She had walked these same lanes with him once, her shawl pulled tight, her laugh bright as a struck match in the fog, before the young master from Checkley had come idling by in his brass-lamped motorcar. Verrall, with his soft, uncalloused palms and his easy, Oxford drawl, smelling of lavender water and leather. Up above the chimney-pots, tearing through the rags of cloud, hung the green star. Folk at the corner-shops craned their necks to gape at it, muttering of portents and the end of days, their faces washed in its pale, sickly phosphorescence. William spat into the gutter. Let it fall, he thought. Let the whole rotten edifice of masters and clerks, of slag-heaps and starved bellies, crack open like an overfired pot. His mother would be waiting by the grate with weak tea and a crust, her knuckles swollen from the wash-tub, sighing over the rent-book. He did not want her pity. He wanted the barrel of the little pistol tucked beneath his mattress, cold and certain as winter.

In the cramped parlour, the gas-mantle hissed like a cornered snake. William spread the evening paper across the oilcloth table, his thumb leaving a smudge of coal-dust beside the black headlines. Ultimatum. Cruisers in the North Sea. The German Fleet Mobilizes. Europe was a powder-keg, the leader-writer declared, and every chancery from Berlin to Whitehall was striking matches in the dark. Good, William thought, his jaw clenching until his teeth ached. Let the great ironclads ram each other into the silt; let the young gentlemen in their braided tunics bleed into the mud alongside the colliers' sons. It was only the world's fever made visible, the same sour poison that boiled in his own veins whenever he pictured Nettie's pale throat against Verrall's velvet lapel. He climbed the narrow stair to his attic box, the boards groaning beneath his weight. From beneath the lumpy flock mattress he drew the cheap Belgian revolver he had bought off a dock-hand in Liverpool, wrapped in an oily rag. Six chambers. Small, dull-blue, unlovely, yet heavy with a terrible democracy. A clerk's finger could pull a trigger as cleanly as a lord's. Outside the dormer window, the comet had swelled into a vast, luminous fan of emerald mist, drowning out the Plough and the Pole Star. Its light fell across the slate roofs like spilled absinthe, turning the grime of the Potteries into something spectral and strange. Tomorrow he would take the morning train east, out of this smoke-choked hollow, down to the sea where they were hiding.

The third-class carriage smelled of stale tobacco, wet wool, and cheap peppermint. By the time William stepped onto the platform at Shaphambury, the afternoon was already dying into a bruised, unnatural twilight. A salt wind whipped across the marshland, carrying the cries of gulls and the distant, muffled boom of the tide against the shingle. He walked past the boarding-houses with their shut blinds and peeling stucco, his hand plunged deep into his coat pocket, his fingers curled tight around the checkered grip of the pistol. Everywhere he looked, people stood in little knots on the promenade, silent and upturned, watching the heavens. The comet was no longer a star; it was a great, silent broom of green fire sweeping from zenith to horizon, its edges frayed into delicate, feathery plumes. The sea beneath it looked like dark bottle-glass, streaked with oily emerald foam. He knew where they were—Nettie’s cousin had let it slip over a pint of bitter: the little red-brick bungalow beyond the headland, where the cliffs dropped away to the sands. His boots crunched on the gravel path, loud and rhythmic in the hush. She is mine, the gravel whispered. You are nothing, the sea answered, dragging the pebbles back into its cold throat. He felt no hunger, though he had not eaten since dawn, only a thin, singing clarity in his skull, as if he were a wire drawn too tight over a peg. Above the bungalow’s dark roof, a single yellow lamp glowed behind drawn curtains, warm and insolent.

He did not knock. He vaulted the low tamarisk hedge, his coat snagging on a dry branch, and came upon them on the terrace overlooking the black water. They were standing by the stone balustrade, looking up at the sky, Verrall’s arm draped lightly around Nettie’s waist. At the sound of William’s boots on the flagstones, Nettie turned, and the green light caught the sudden, startled white of her eyes. "Willie," she breathed, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall stepped half a pace forward, his chin tilted in that lazy, infuriating way of his, half-amused, half-annoyed, as though a tradesman had come to the wrong door. "Leadford, isn't it?" he said, his voice smooth as cream. "You're a long way from Staffordshire, my good fellow." The words snapped the last thread of William's patience. He dragged the revolver from his pocket, the metal gleaming dully in the emerald dusk. Nettie cried out—a sharp, bird-like sound—and Verrall stiffened, his mask of ease cracking into grey terror. "Stay back," William shouted, though his voice sounded thin and ridiculous against the roar of the surf. "You stole her! You with your money and your—" He pulled the trigger. The crack of the shot tore the quiet air, a brief, orange stab in the green gloom, and the bullet splintered the stone coping an inch from Verrall’s elbow. Before William could cock the hammer again, a strange, heavy sweetness washed over the terrace. The air thickened, turning the colour of young willow-leaves, and the sea-wind died instantly into a vast, breathless hush.

The pistol slipped from William’s fingers and clattered onto the flagstones, forgotten. A dense, luminous fog—not cold like the Staffordshire mist, but warm and fragrant as crushed mint and summer rain—was rolling in off the Channel, swallowing the cliffs, the bungalow, the dark horizon. He tried to draw breath to curse them, to roar his hatred into Verrall’s pale face, but the green vapour filled his lungs with a profound, dizzying languor. His knees buckled. Down he went onto the cool stone, watching through heavy-lidded eyes as Nettie sank gracefully beside the balustrade, her hair spilling over her shoulders, and Verrall folded slowly to the earth like a dropped cloak. There was no pain in it, only a vast, unravelling silence. All the sharp edges of his life—the coal-grime, the rent-book, the jeers of the clerks, the black headlines screaming of dreadnoughts and slaughter—began to dissolve like salt in warm water. He heard, as if from a great distance, the muffled thud of a foghorn out on the shoals, and then even that faded into a gentle, humming stillness. Across the sleeping island, across the narrow seas where the grey warships lay idling in the dark, the green tide of the comet’s breath was rising, sealing every angry mouth, uncoiling every clenched fist. William’s cheek pressed against the damp stone. In the last flicker of his waking thought, there was no Nettie, no Verrall, no William Leadford—only a quiet, boundless dark, soft as velvet, waiting for the morning.

He woke to birdsong—a thrush, clear and insistent, somewhere in the tamarisk hedge—and a sky of such washed, crystalline blue that it made his eyes ache to look upon it. The green mist was gone. William sat up slowly, rubbing his palms over his face. His head was astonishingly light, as though a fever that had burned in his bones since childhood had broken in the night and left him clean. Beside the stone coping lay the little Belgian revolver, a dull, absurd lump of scrap iron; he wondered, with a faint, detached pity, at the frantic ghost who had carried it all the way from Staffordshire. A few yards away, Verrall was stirring, blinking up at the morning sun, his fine tweed suit rumpled with dew. Nettie sat up between them, pushing her tangled hair back from her forehead, her gaze travelling from Verrall to William without fear, without coquetry, only a wide, wondering stillness. "Willie," she said again, and this time there was no terror in the name, only the simple recognition of one waking soul to another. William rose to his feet and walked to the balustrade. Below, the sea lay flat and silver, gently lapping the shingle. Far out, on the horizon, a smudge of smoke hung motionless above a distant cruiser, as if the men aboard had forgotten why they were steaming toward the guns. He turned back to Nettie and Verrall, feeling neither envy nor claim, only a quiet, luminous ache of brotherhood for the pair of them, and for his mother waking in her smoky kitchen far away. The old world, with its soot and its jealousies, had burned away in the night; and as William stepped down toward the shore, he knew that whatever came next, he would walk into it with open hands.
