Soot on the sill, soot in the crease of his collar, soot drifting down like black snow from the bottle-kilns of Clayton. William Leadford walked the cinder-path with his hands deep in his pockets, his knuckles brushing the cold, oiled snout of the revolver. Five shillings and sixpence at the pawnshop in Burslem, a neat little toy of blued steel to mend a broken world. Above the jagged teeth of the pit-heads, the evening sky had turned the colour of a bruised plum, and there, hanging low over the slag-heaps, burned the comet. A pale green smudge, a sickly, watching eye. Let it watch, he thought. Let the whole rotten firmament stare down at Staffordshire stewing in its own sulphur. Nettie was gone. Nettie of the quick, birdlike laugh, Nettie whose hair had smelled of rain and cheap lavender soap along the towpath by the canal. Gone with Edward Verrall. The name tasted of copper and bile in his mouth. Verrall of the soft hands, Verrall of the motor-car and the crisp pound notes, lifting her out of the grime as a man lifts a bright pebble from a ditch. He spat into the black canal water. A barge slid past, lantern swinging, the bargeman's shadow long and crooked on the brick wall. She had written him a little note on thick cream paper—Dear Willie, you must see it is for the best—as if love were a matter of bookkeeping, a ledger balanced in his disfavour. His boots crunched on the clinkers. Every step jarred the heavy iron against his thigh, steady as a pulse, promising an end to the long, grey humiliation of his days.

Outside the newsagent’s in the High Street, a knot of men in flat caps stood under the flaring gas-jet, their faces yellow and pinched. The placards screamed in black ink: ULTIMATUM. FLEETS MOBILISED IN THE NORTH SEA. War, then. The old men in London and Berlin rattling their sabres, sending the lads from the potteries and the pits to drown in grey water or rot in Belgian mud so the masters might keep their markets. A fellow with a cough was shouting about the Kaiser, pounding a dirty fist into his palm, his eyes bright with borrowed fury. Leadford shouldered past them, his lip curling. Fools, all of them, drunk on printer’s ink and brass bands. What was the Empire to a man who earned eighteen shillings a week and lived in two damp rooms with a coughing mother? At home, the kettle was singing on the hob, a thin, fretful whine. His mother sat by the grate, her shawl pulled tight over her narrow shoulders, darning a sock by the tallow dip. She did not look up when he came in, only sighed—that small, patient sound that had hollowed out his childhood. He went to the tin box beneath his straw mattress, took the three sovereigns he had hoarded since Easter, and slipped them into his waistcoat. No goodbye. A kiss on her dry, papery cheek would only wake her suspicions. From the narrow window, the green star seemed larger, casting a faint, uncanny sheen over the slate roofs. Europe was marching to the slaughterhouse, and he, William Leadford, had a private frontier to cross before the night was out.

Click-clack, click-clack, the third-class carriage jolted eastward through the dark midlands, every beat of the iron wheels hammering out the rhythm of his fever: Verrall and Nettie, Verrall and Nettie. The oil-lamp in the ceiling swung in a lazy arc, throwing jumping shadows across the knees of his fellow passengers. Opposite him, a stout commercial traveller in a checked waistcoat was folding his evening paper with plump, ringed fingers, discoursing to a nervous clerk on the trajectory of the comet. Green vapour, the traveller said, tapping the newsprint. Nitrogen, or some such foreign gas. The professors at Greenwich say we’ll pass right through the tail before dawn. Like as not it’ll poison the whole boiling of us, war or no war. The clerk gave a weak, fluttering laugh and glanced out at the blackness. Leadford stared at his own reflection in the rattling pane—a thin, sallow face, hollow-cheeked, eyes burning like two coals under a ragged brow. He pressed his elbow against his ribs to feel the hard lump of the pistol. In his mind’s eye he saw Nettie again, not as she was in the dim parlour on Sunday evenings, shy and warm against his sleeve, but laughing in some bright, sunlit garden, her white dress brushing the grass while Verrall leaned down to whisper in her ear. A hot wave of nausea rose in his throat. Let the comet’s tail choke the earth, he thought, let the seas boil and the dreadnoughts sink like lead—so long as he reached Shaphambury before the darkness lifted, so long as he saw the terror in Verrall’s well-bred eyes.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, tar, and expensive leisure. Walking up from the station, Leadford felt the grime of the Black Country clinging to his coat like a curse among the whitewashed boarding-houses and trim privet hedges. Yet the seaside town was strangely hushed. Doors stood open; people lingered on the esplanade in their dressing-gowns and evening coats, faces tilted upward in silent, uneasy wonder. The comet was no longer a star. It had unfurled across half the heavens, a vast, luminous fan of emerald fire, bathing the shingle beach and the curling whitecaps in a cold, unearthly twilight. Even the gas-lamps looked pale and yellow against that monstrous green glare. He followed the cliff path northward, his boots silent on the turf. Below, the tide hissed and dragged at the pebbles. Then, rounding a clump of wind-bent gorse, he stopped. Ahead of him, near the low stone wall overlooking the sea, stood two figures. A man in a light tweed suit, easy-shouldered, his hand resting lightly on the girl’s elbow; and Nettie, her dark hair blown across her cheek, her face turned up toward the glowing zenith. Even in the eerie green light, he knew the curve of her throat, the small, quick gesture of her chin. Verrall said something, and she laughed—a clear, silver sound that carried over the wash of the waves and pierced Leadford like a needle of ice. The sea air was suddenly too thin to breathe. He drew the revolver from his pocket, the metal slick with the sweat of his palm, and stepped out from the shadow of the gorse.

Nettie! His voice tore out of him, raw and ugly, cracking against the quiet of the headland. They spun round together. Verrall’s face went stiff, an Englishman’s mask of outrage and alarm, stepping half in front of her. Nettie gave a sharp little cry, her hand flying to her mouth. Willie—what are you doing here? Leadford did not answer her; words were useless things, worn thin by months of brooding. He raised the pistol, his arm shaking violently, aiming at the pale oval of Verrall’s chest. You stole her, he choked out, the bile rising hot and bitter. You bought her with your filthy money, you— Somewhere out beyond the dark horizon, a dull, heavy boom rolled across the water—the sound of great guns, perhaps, the fleets colliding in the fog, or the sky itself splitting open. A sudden wind swept over the cliff, bringing with it a strange, intoxicating scent, sweet and sharp as crushed mint and summer rain. Leadford gasped. His lungs felt strangely light, then heavy, as if he were breathing cool green water. He pulled the trigger. A flat, foolish crack echoed off the stones, the bullet whistling harmlessly into the dark air above the sea. His knees buckled beneath him. Verrall was stumbling forward, not to strike him, but clutching at his own throat, his eyes wide and dreamy. Nettie sank slowly onto the turf, her skirts billowing softly around her like a fallen flower. The emerald mist was pouring down from the stars now, thick and luminous, wrapping the cliffs, the warring ships, the darkened cities in a vast, gentle silence. Leadford’s cheek struck the cool grass, his fingers uncurling from the iron, and sleep fell over him like a soft green wave.

Dawn came without a cloud, washing the eastern sky in tender, translucent gold. Leadford opened his eyes slowly, expecting the familiar ache of his narrow room, the taste of soot and yesterday’s anger. Instead, he breathed air so pure, so crystalline, it seemed to rinse the very marrow of his bones. The comet was gone, dissolved into the vast, serene blue of morning. Beside him on the dew-drenched grass lay the revolver, its blued barrel gleaming faintly in the early sun. He stared at it for a long moment, marveling at the small, absurd contraption of iron and wood, and at the poor, tormented ghost who had carried it across England in the dark. A few paces away, Edward Verrall was sitting up, rubbing his forehead with a bewildered, boyish gesture, his tweed coat rumpled and damp with dew. Nettie stirred beside him, lifting her head to look out over the sparkling, quiet sea. Leadford rose to his knees, then stood, feeling neither triumph nor shame, only a vast, aching tenderness for all three of them. How small their little tangle of pride and longing seemed beneath this boundless sky. When Nettie turned and saw him, there was no terror in her eyes, only a quiet, wondering recognition, as if they had all woken together from the same long, feverish dream. Willie, she said softly, her voice clear as water. He walked over, leaving the pistol behind him in the clover, and offered his hand to help Verrall to his feet. Together, the three of them stood on the cliff’s edge, watching the fishing boats put out from the harbour below, while across the quiet world, the guns fell silent and the new day began.
