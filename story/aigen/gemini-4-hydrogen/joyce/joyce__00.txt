Soot on the sill, soot in the lung, the eternal grey breath of the Five Towns rising to smudge a sky already sick with green. William Leadford sat by the cold grate, his knuckles raw from the ledger-ink and the iron-dust, listening to the distant, sullen cough of the colliery engines. War, the broadsheet cried in blackest type, folded greasy beside his crust. Ultimatum. Cruisers in the North Sea. Let them burn, he thought, the lords of the kilns and the fat-bellied factors in London, let them choke on their own cordite. Above the slag-heaps of Clayton, where the night-shift flares bled orange into the mist, a new thing hung: a pale, verdigris smear, a star with a trailing beard of sickly light. The astronomers called it a comet, a wandering vagabond of gas and stone, but to William, staring through the blistered glass of his mother’s parlour, it looked like a wound festering in the heavens. Everything rots, his mind muttered, turning the phrase like a sour penny under his tongue. Wages rot, iron rusts, and promises are paper for the fire. His mother shuffled in the scullery, the clatter of a tin basin, a sigh heavy as wet clay. He felt the small, cold lump in his coat pocket—the pawnshop revolver, six shillings’ worth of greasy certainty—and his jaw tightened. Let the Germans come. Let the green star fall and smash the potteries to shards.

Nettie. The name came not with the softness of the lane behind Checkley, where the hawthorn had smelled of honey and damp earth, but with a sharp, metallic tang, like biting on a brass pin. Nettie. She had taken her laugh—that quick, bright bird-chirp of a laugh—and given it to Verrall. Edward Verrall, with his motor-goggles pushed up onto his forehead, his tweed smelling of lavender water and petrol, his smooth, uncalloused palms that had never known the bite of a shovel or the cramp of a clerk’s stool. William saw them again in the theatre of his skull: the gleam of her white blouse in the back of the touring car, the way she had looked back—or had she? No, she hadn’t looked back. She had leaned toward the steering wheel, toward his easy, moneyed murmur, leaving William in the dust of the turnpike with a crumpled ribbon in his fist. Whore of the rich, the bitter demon in his ribs hissed, though another voice, smaller and older, wept for the girl who used to share his peppermint drops under the canal bridge. He stood up, his chair scraping the flagstones. If I cannot have her, no gilded pup from the Hall shall. The green light from the window fell across his sleeve, turning the worn serge the colour of stagnant pond-water. He kissed his mother’s dry, papery cheek without a word, and stepped out into the sulfur-thick dark, his hand closed hard around the butt of the pistol.

The third-class carriage rattled southward, a wooden box of stale tobacco and wet wool, carrying him away from the kilns toward the sea. Every clack of the iron wheels beat out a dull litany: Verrall, Nettie, Verrall, Nettie, kill, kill, kill. Opposite him, two commercial travellers argued over the evening papers, their voices thick with porter and panic. They’ve shelled the coast, one said, tapping a stained fingernail on the map. The fleet’s engaged. William stared out at the rushing blackness of the Midlands. There were no stars tonight, only that vast, luminous smudge, larger now, spreading across the zenith like spilled absinthe on a velvet cloth. It cast a phantom twilight over the hedgerows and the sleeping spires, a ghostly, underwater sheen. At the junction, soldiers were boarding—boys in khaki with stiff new boots and frightened, laughing eyes, singing some music-hall catch about the King or the Empire. Fools, William thought, watching their flushed faces under the station lamps. Marching to die for the Verralls. When he stepped out at the coastal terminus, the salt wind hit his face, sharp and cold, carrying the faint, distant thudding of—was it thunder, or the big guns out beyond the Dogger Bank? He walked along the shingle road toward the bungalow at Shapham, his boots crunching on the pebbles, his heart a hot, hammered anvil in his chest. The comet’s light was blinding now, turning the foaming crests of the breakers to liquid jade.

A yellow lamp burned behind the curtains of the bungalow, warm and insolent against the eerie green of the dunes. William crept through the marram grass, his trousers soaked with dew, the salt-spray stinging his lips. Voices drifted out through the half-open French window—a low, musical chuckle from Verrall, and then Nettie’s voice, soft and breathless, asking about the light in the sky. Look, Teddy, it’s like a great green wing. William’s fingers tightened on the trigger-guard. He stepped onto the wooden veranda, his shadow stretching long and monstrous across the floorboards. He shoved the glass door wide. They turned—Verrall in his silk smoking-jacket, a glass of claret half-raised, and Nettie, her dark hair loose over her shoulders, her eyes widening in the sudden draught. William, she breathed, and the old sweetness in her tone only sharpened his fury. He drew the revolver, his arm trembling with the weight of all his starved, humiliated years. You thought you’d hide here, he snarled, his voice cracking like a boy’s. While the world burns and I rot in the dirt? Verrall stepped forward, calm, infuriatingly composed, placing himself before the girl. Put that down, Leadford. You’re mad. I’m awake, William shouted, aiming at the white triangle of Verrall’s shirt-front. Outside, the sea roared, and a strange, sweet, suffocating smell—like crushed almonds and summer rain—began to drift through the open door, thick as a creeping fog.

The hammer clicked—or did it? A sudden, overwhelming languor unstrung William’s wrist, as if his bones had turned to warm wax. The green mist was pouring into the room now, visible, curling around the ankles of the chairs like luminous smoke. Verrall coughed, his hand flying to his throat, his knees buckling gently onto the rug. Nettie gave a little cry, not of terror, but of wonder, sinking down beside him as her eyelids fluttered shut. William tried to shout, to pull the trigger, to hate them one last time, but the air he drew into his lungs was impossibly sweet, heavy with an alien, narcotic peace. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and thudded softly on the carpet. His knees gave way. Falling, he saw the ceiling dissolve into the vast, emerald radiance of the comet’s tail, wrapping the earth in a silent, breathless shroud. Somewhere far out in the North Sea, he knew—with a sudden, dreamlike certainty—the gunners were slumping over their hot brass breeches; in the blackened streets of Staffordshire, the strikers and the masters were sliding gently down onto the cobbles, side by side. The great, fevered machine of the world was stopping, its gears greased with celestial dew. His anger evaporated like a drop of vinegar on a hot stone. Darkness took him, soft as a mother’s shawl, and in that green oblivion, all names—Leadford, Verrall, Nettie, England—melted into a single, quiet sigh.

When he opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the air tasted of nothing at all—or rather, of everything clean: sea-salt, wet thyme, and morning. William sat up slowly on the rug. Beside him, the revolver lay like a foolish, rusted toy, an absurd relic of a dead dream. He looked at his own hands; the ink-stains were still there, the broken nails, yet they seemed to belong to a creature he had pitied long ago. Across the room, Verrall was stirring, blinking at the bright, washed blue of the sky beyond the veranda. There was no arrogance left in the man’s face, only a stunned, childlike clarity, as if he were seeing light for the very first time. And Nettie—she sat up, brushing the hair from her brow, and looked at both of them. William felt no stab in his breast, no bitter, possessive clawing at his throat. He saw her as she truly was: neither an angel nor a prize, just a young woman, frail and luminous, breathing the same astonishing air. William, she said softly, and this time he heard no mockery, no guilt, only a quiet recognition. Verrall rose, steadied himself against the table, and extended a hand—not to command, not to condescend, but simply to help William to his feet. Out beyond the dunes, the sea lay glassy and unscarred, no smoke of warships on the horizon, only the gulls wheeling in the vast, merciful silence of a world made new.
