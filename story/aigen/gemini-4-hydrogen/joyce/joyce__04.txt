Soot on the sill, soot in the lungs, the eternal grey porridge of the Potteries steaming under a bruised sky. William Leadford stood by the window of his mother’s kitchen, turning the crumpled half-sheet of notepaper between thumb and blackened forefinger. I am going with him, Will. Forgive me if you can. Forgive. A word for curates and fat aldermen, not for a clerk in a tile-works with eighteen shillings a week and holes in his boots. Outside, the bottle-ovens belched their yellow-grey bile into the twilight, and far above the chimney-stacks, pale as a drowned girl’s eye, hung the green star.

Verrall. Even the name had the slick, oiled purr of a motor-car. Young Mr. Verrall with his flannel trousers and his Cambridge drawl, smelling of lavender water and unearned leather. He had only to crook a finger by the canal path, where the water ran warm and oily from the engine-houses, and Nettie had fluttered to him like a moth to an arc-lamp. William’s jaw clenched till the molars ached. In the dresser drawer, beneath his folded copies of The Clarion and a pamphlet on the Midland coal strikes, lay the little bulldog revolver he had bought off a drunken foundryman in Hanley. Six chambers. Cold, honest iron. Let Verrall quote Latin to an ounce of lead. His mother’s kettle hissed on the hob, a thin, querulous whistle that seemed to mock the beating in his temples. Nettie, Nettie. The soft curve of her throat under the hawthorn hedge last May, lost now to silk and idle laughter.

He walked out into the gaslit murk of Burslem, his collar turned up against the drizzle that tasted of sulphur and wet ash. At the corner by the Red Lion, a knot of colliers stood under the sputtering lamp, their faces smeared like minstrels, reading the evening Sentinel held between them.

—Ultimatum, one spat into the gutter. —They’ve sunk two cruisers off Heligoland. My brother-in-law’s in the reserves.

—Let ’em fight, said another, hitching his belt. —Bloody Kaiser wants a lesson. Good for trade, war is.

William pushed past them, his shoulder striking the wet wool of a coat. Good for trade. Good for the masters in their stone villas up the hill, while the lads from the pits went out to rot in Belgian mud for a shilling a day. The whole rotten hive of Europe was humming for blood, emperors and chancelleries rattling their tin sabres, and the fools in the streets cheered them on. He looked up. Through a rent in the coal-smoke, the comet had grown—no longer a mere star, but a smeared, luminous thumbprint of verdigris across the zenith, trailing a faint, ghostly fan of light. A barber’s apprentice stared upward, mouth ajar, his apron stained with lather. The end of the world, someone muttered, half-laughing, half-afraid. William’s hand closed in his pocket around the knurled grip of the revolver. Let it end, he thought fiercely. Let the whole filthy, unjust, mocking world crack like a fired pot, so long as he found them first.

Clack-clack, clack-clack, clack-clack. The third-class carriage smelled of damp tweed, stale shag, and cold mutton fat. William sat pressed against the wooden partition, watching the Midlands slip backward into the dark—hedgerows, signal-boxes winking red and green, the black ribs of viaducts. All night he had ridden south, toward the sea, toward Shaphambury, where an aunt of Nettie’s kept a lodging-house and where a clerk at the railway had seen a gentleman in a tweed ulster booking two tickets.

Verrall’s hands on her waist. The thought came unbidden, a hot needle behind his eyes. He pressed his forehead against the cold pane. Outside, the English fields lay washed in an eerie, phosphor-green twilight, though dawn was hours away. The comet filled half the heavens now, a vast, silent cataract of emerald fire pouring down toward the horizon. Across from him, a commercial traveller snored, his bowler hat tipped over his nose, oblivious to the sky, oblivious to the headlines on the seat beside him: WAR DECLARED. FLEETS ENGAGED IN THE NORTH SEA.

William’s fingers traced the trigger-guard inside his coat. Eighteen shillings a week. The damp room in Clayton. The cough that never left his mother’s chest. And Nettie, laughing in a seaside parlour, pouring tea from a silver pot while Verrall’s boots dried by the fender. I will kill him, he told the rhythm of the rails. I will shoot him through his smooth, lying heart, and then I will shoot her, and then myself, and we shall be done with this miserable farce. The train shrieked for a junction, a long, iron wail in the green dark.

By evening, the sea at Shaphambury was a sheet of tarnished pewter, and the air had grown strangely heavy, spiced with a faint, sweet odour like crushed almonds and summer hay. William crouched behind the tamarisk hedge of the cliff-top garden, his boots soaked with dew, watching the lighted French windows of the boarding-house. They were there. He had seen Verrall’s silhouette pass the lace curtain, tall and easy, and then Nettie’s—the quick, birdlike tilt of her head as she reached up to adjust a lamp.

A newsboy ran down the promenade below, his voice thin and cracked against the boom of the surf: Extra! Great naval battle! Extra! Nobody came out to buy. A strange lethargy seemed to be creeping over the town; the streetlamps burned dim and watery through a rising, luminous fog—not the grey sea-fret of the Channel, but a soft, emerald vapour that drifted down from the zenith like pollen.

William stepped onto the gravel path. The pebbles crunched loudly under his heels. He drew the revolver. His heart was a madman hammering at a locked door, his breath ragged in his throat. He reached the veranda, raised his arm, and saw Nettie turn toward the glass, her eyes wide, not with fear of him, but with wonder at the green mist curling around the eaves. Nettie! he tried to shout, his finger tightening on the iron curve.

Then the sweetness took him. It poured into his lungs like cool water, dizzying, profound. His knees buckled. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and clattered onto the flagstones, and William Leadford sank down into the emerald dark, dreaming of nothing at all.

Light. Not the yellow, soot-filtered glare of Staffordshire, nor the gas-sickly flicker of midnight, but a clear, washing radiance that seemed to rise from the earth itself.

William opened his eyes. He was lying on the damp grass beside the veranda, his cheek pressed against a tuft of clover. For a long moment he did not know his own name, only that he was breathing, and that the air was extraordinarily sweet. He sat up slowly, stiff in the joints, his coat wrinkled and stained with earth. Beside his boot lay the little bulldog revolver, its blued steel dull with dew. He looked at it as a man might look at a stone idol dug from a ditch—curious, heavy, meaningless.

I came to murder them, he thought, and the memory brought no heat, only a vast, quiet astonishment, as if he were recalling the tantrum of a feverish child. Why had he hated Verrall? Why had he thought Nettie a possession to be locked in a drawer like a Sunday collar? The old William—that knotted, snarling bundle of envy and soot and wounded pride—seemed to have dissolved in the night’s green rain. He stood up and brushed the clover from his knees. Down on the promenade, a milkman had stopped his cart; he and a policeman were standing together by the sea-wall, looking out over the sunlit water, talking in low, earnest voices. There were no newsboys crying slaughter. The gulls wheeled in the spotless blue, their cries sharp and clean. A strange pity, wide as the morning sea, welled up in William’s breast—for himself, for his mother coughing in the grey Midlands, for all the blind, angry millions waking from their long nightmare.

The French windows stood open to the morning. Nettie came out onto the veranda, wearing a simple cotton gown, her dark hair loose about her shoulders. She stopped when she saw him, her hand resting on the painted doorframe, and behind her, Verrall appeared in his shirtsleeves, his face pale and stripped of its old, careless insolence.

Neither of them screamed. Neither of them backed away from the shabby man standing by the gravel path.

—Will, Nettie said softly. Her voice was clear, without tremor or guilt.

—Good morning, Nettie, he answered, and marvelled at how easily the words came, unburdened by the old bile. He stooped, picked up the revolver by its barrel, and tossed it over the tamarisk hedge into the long grass of the cliff. It made no sound when it fell.

Verrall stepped forward, looking at William not as a gentleman looks at a clerk, nor as a rival looks at a thief, but with the grave, searching eyes of a brother met after a long voyage.

—We were mad yesterday, Verrall said quietly. —All of us. The whole world.

—Yes, William said. He looked past them, out to where the Channel glittered like hammered silver under the risen sun. Somewhere out there, the great ironclads were drifting silent, their guns cold, their crews staring at the same clean sky. He looked back at Nettie—not with the old, clutching hunger, but with a sudden, luminous tenderness that asked for nothing in return. —You mustn’t be afraid of me anymore, Nettie. None of us need be afraid.

She smiled then, a slow, wondering smile that touched her eyes, and came down the steps to take his rough, coal-scarred hand in hers, while the morning wind blew warm and salt from the open sea.
