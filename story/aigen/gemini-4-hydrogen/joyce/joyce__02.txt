Soot on the sill, soot in the lungs, the black breath of the potbanks rising to choke the pale Staffordshire evening. William Leadford stood by the slag-heap gate, his knuckles raw from the ledger-desk, watching the smoke-plumes curl toward a sky the colour of a bruised plum. Up there, above the tall brick chimneys of Clayton, hung the green star. A cold, emerald needle-prick in the dusk. The comet, the men at the taproom called it, spitting into the sawdust, a sign of blood and iron. Let it fall, he thought. Let the whole rotten kiln of England crack and spill its cinders. He thought of his mother at the range, coughing into her apron, boiling tea-leaves for the third time to save a farthing. In his waistcoat pocket, Nettie’s note crackled like a dried leaf—three lines of her looping, schoolgirl hand, smelling faintly of lavender water and treason. Gone with Edward. Do not follow, Will. Edward. Mr. Verrall, of the big house on the hill, with his soft, uncalloused palms and his motor-car coughing blue oil down the lane. William spat onto the clinkers. A newsboy trotted past the gaslamp, his boots slapping the wet cobbles, his shrill cry tearing the twilight: War! Ultimatum! Fleet steaming north! War for the gentlemen, grime for the clerks, and Nettie’s warm mouth pressed against a silk lapel. He turned down the alley toward the pawnshop, his shadow stretching long and crooked behind him, a black finger pointing at the green eye in the sky.

The third-class carriage smelled of stale tobacco, wet wool, and boiled cabbage. Clack-clack, clack-clack, the iron wheels hammered out his litany: Verrall, Nettie, Verrall, Nettie. William sat wedged between a snoring drover and a woman nursing a fretful child, his right hand buried deep in his coat pocket, his thumb tracing the cold, knurled grip of the six-chambered revolver. Twelve shillings sixpence at the corner of Bridge Street, and a box of cartridges thrown in for luck. A little persuader, the pawnbroker had wheezed, his yellow teeth clicking. The drover snorted in his sleep, a wet, rattling sound; the child whimpered against a sour shawl. William stared at his own ink-stained thumbnails, remembering the feel of Nettie’s waist in the dark lane behind the Baptist chapel, soft and yielding beneath her cheap serge. Outside the streaked window, the Midlands bled away into dark hedgerows and sleeping orchards, yet always that pale green lantern hung above the telegraph wires, growing larger with every mile south. Now she was laughing in some seaside parlour, her fingers tangling in Verrall’s sleek hair, while out in the North Sea the ironclads were clearing their decks for slaughter. Let them blow each other to red rags, William muttered against the glass. His reflection stared back at him, hollow-cheeked, fierce-eyed, a ghost from the potteries come down to Shaphambury to settle an account in lead.

Salt in the nostrils, sharp and clean after the sulphur of the Four Towns. Shaphambury lay beneath the chalk cliffs like a handful of scattered shells, its promenade lamps trembling in the sea-fret. In the grand hotels along the esplanade, chandeliers blazed behind crimson curtains, spilling warm yellow light across the wet asphalt—gentlemen drinking port while the boys from the coal-pits waited in the dark. His boots were caked with Staffordshire clay, leaving grey smears on their clean flagstones. William walked the shingle, the loose stones grinding under his worn heels, his eyes fixed on the heavens. The comet was no longer a needle-prick; it was a vast, luminous fan of emerald mist, trailing a tail of ghostly fire across half the firmament. Beneath its sickly radiance, the English Channel looked like a sheet of beaten pewter. Far out beyond the headland, the long white fingers of searchlights swept the dark water, hunting for cruisers in the gloom. War tonight, a fisherman muttered, leaning on his upturned dinghy, his oilskin gleaming green in the starlight. They say the Germans are off the Dogger Bank. William gave no answer. He climbed the winding cliff-path toward the whitewashed villas above the town, where the hydrangeas grew thick as cabbages and the rich came to hide their sins. Every window was shuttered or glowing dimly behind lace. Then, through a gap in a tamarisk hedge, he heard it—the soft, silvery cadence of a woman’s laugh, light as dropped coins, followed by the low murmur of a man’s baritone. His hand closed on the iron in his pocket.

He pushed through the wicket gate, the latch clicking sharp as a cocked hammer. On the terrace above the sea stood a summerhouse of painted lattice, and there they were, caught in the eerie, emerald wash of the sky. Verrall leaned against the rail in his white flannels, a cigarette glowing orange between his fingers, his profile clean-cut as a sovereign. Nettie stood beside him, a pale shawl drawn over her shoulders, looking out toward the dark horizon where a sudden, distant flash of gunfire bloomed and died—a silent spark of red. Look, Edward, she whispered, they’re fighting. William stepped out onto the gravel. His boots crunched loud in the stillness. Nettie, he said, his voice raw as a rasp. She spun round, her hand flying to her throat, her eyes widening into dark pools. Verrall straightened, his lazy ease stiffening into gentlemanly outrage. Leadford? Good God, man, what are you doing here? William drew the revolver. The barrel caught the green starlight, dull and ugly. I’ve come for what’s mine, he said, though his tongue felt thick as flannel, and his heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird. Verrall stepped in front of her, brave enough in his fine linen, foolish enough to think words could stop lead. Put that down, you madman. William raised the muzzle, lining the foresight with the pale triangle of Verrall’s shirt-front. Above them, the green tail of the comet seemed to stoop, pouring a sudden, swirling fog down over the cliffs, thick and sweet as crushed elderflowers.

He tried to squeeze the trigger, to loose the pent-up bile of twenty bitter years, but his forefinger was numb, heavy as tallow. The green mist rolled over the parapet, curling around his ankles, rising to his knees, his chest, his mouth. It was not smoke; it was a cool, living vapour that smelled of bruised mint and summer rain after long drought. Nettie gave a little sigh, her knees buckling softly against the wicker chair. Verrall swayed, clutching at the lattice, his cigarette falling to hiss out in the damp grass. William gasped, drawing the strange air deep into his soot-lined lungs. Instantly, a great singing silence rushed into his skull. The green light seemed to soak into his very marrow, washing the grime from his blood, unknotting the tight, cold fist that had lived in his chest since boyhood. His arm dropped; the revolver slipped from his slack fingers and thudded onto the turf. He sank to his knees, not in defeat, but in an overwhelming, weightless weariness. Somewhere far off, a foghorn groaned, a long, mournful note that faded into nothing. As his cheek pressed against the cool clover, his mind unspooled—the potbanks, the ledger, the pinched face of his mother, the ironclads steaming through the dark, all dissolving into a vast, emerald sea of dreamless peace. There was no hatred left in him, only a faint, distant wonder at the softness of the earth beneath his hands, and then the green dark closed gently over his eyes.

Morning broke, not with the grey, bleary drizzle of the world he knew, but in a blaze of crystalline gold. William opened his eyes. The air he breathed was sharp and pure as spring water, stinging his nostrils with the scent of thyme and sea-salt. Beside his hand lay the cheap revolver, rusted already with the night’s heavy dew, looking small and absurd as a child’s cast-off toy. He sat up slowly, his head clear as a washed pane of glass. Across the terrace, Verrall was stirring on the flagstones, blinking into the sun, his white flannels stained with grass. Near him, Nettie sat up, brushing her tangled hair from her forehead, looking about her with the bewildered, innocent gaze of a waking child. William looked at them—really looked at them—and felt no stab of jealousy, no sourness in his belly. He saw Verrall not as a thief in fine cloth, but as a frightened, mortal boy, fragile as himself; he saw Nettie not as a prize stolen from his grasp, but as a creature of breath and bone, free to wander where her heart led. Down in the town, a church bell began to toll, not an alarm of war, but a slow, sweet peal across the quiet bay, where the great ships lay idle and harmless on the blue water. William stood up, brushing the earth from his shabby knees. The kilns of Staffordshire were still waiting, dark and foul, and his hands were still poor; yet as he walked toward the two waking figures in the sunlight, he knew the old madness was dead, and the work of the world was only beginning.
