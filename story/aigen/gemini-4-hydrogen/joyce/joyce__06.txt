Grease under the quick of his nails, black-ingrained from the pit-head and the Potteries' belch, and her note crumpled in the lining of his waistcoat like a dry leaf. Dear Willie, I cannot. Cannot. The word rang flat as a cracked saucer on the cobbles of Clayton. Up above the bottle-ovens, where the soot-curled smoke thinned into the Staffordshire dusk, that pale green stranger hung—a smear of verdigris on the bruised slate of evening.

Verrall. The name tasted of brass and saddle-soap, of the big house beyond the park palings where the turf was kept velvet for the gentry's heels. And Nettie, with her quick little laugh and the ribbon at her throat, sitting beside him in the motor-car, dust-veil flying, leaving Leadford to his cold tea and his mother's sighing by the grate. He's above you, Willie. Above. Everything was above: the masters, the rates, the Kaiser's dreadnoughts steaming out of Kiel, and that cold green eye widening night by night.

He spat into the canal. The scummy water took it without a ripple, reflecting the furnace-glow of the ironworks—red wounds in the dark—and the faint, sickly shimmer of the comet. A bargeman's lantern swung past, yellow as tallow. She's mine, the blood hammered behind his ears, thick and hot. By right of the long walks, by right of the damp grass behind the brickworks, by right of every shilling saved in the tin box. Let the newspapers howl of Ultimatums and the North Sea; his own war was already declared, and its theatre was a small, locked room in his chest.

The pawnshop smelled of camphor, old serge, and the sour breath of the poor. Six shillings and fourpence for the silver watch his uncle left him; nine shillings out for the little bull-dog revolver, nickeled and squat, and a box of cartridges that rattled like dried peas in his pocket. Heavy against his hip it rode, a cold iron teat.

In the Crown at the corner, men with clay-stained knees leaned over their pints, their voices thick with the Daily Mail and beer.

—They've sunk a cruiser off the Dogger, says Ted, tapping his pipe-bowl on his boot.

—Aye, and the reserves are called up at Lichfield. My lad's got his papers.

—Bloody Germans. Bloody Kaiser.

Leadford drank his half-pint bitter in two swallows, the froth sticking to his lip. Fools, all of them, barking at a paper moon while the real thief walked the lanes in gaiters. Verrall's father owned half the shares in the colliery; Verrall's kind made the wars and sent the Leadfords to stop the lead. Well, here was lead enough for one gentleman's belly.

Outside, the twilight had turned the colour of stagnant pond-water. Women stood in their aprons at the entries, shielding their eyes to look up at the sky. The comet's tail was a great fan of pale emerald feathers now, sweeping half the zenith, dimming the Plough. A child began to whimper, a thin, piping sound against the clatter of a tramcar. Leadford buttoned his coat tight over the iron weight, his boots striking sparks from the setts as he turned toward the station.

Clack-clack, clack-clack, the third-class carriage jolted southward through the sleeping shires, every axle-beat spelling Verrall, Verrall, Verrall. Opposite him, a commercial traveller snored behind a spread sheet of the Evening Standard: MOBILISATION ORDER. FLEETS AT SEA. WAR IMMINENT. The black capitals danced in the carriage's dim oil-lamp, blurring with the green glare that leaked through the soot-filmed windowpane.

Shaphambury. The name had been scribbled on a torn envelope his mother found by the scullery door. Bungalow Town, by the dunes. A place for clerks on holiday and gentlemen with stolen girls. He pressed his forehead to the cold glass. Outside, the fields of Warwickshire looked drowned, bathed in a ghostly, phosphorescent noon—trees like pale coral, hedges silver-edged, sheep huddled in the corners of pastures, staring upward with idiot patience.

What would she say when she saw him? Would she cry Willie! and shrink behind Verrall's tweed shoulder? Let her. He had rehearsed the scene a hundred times between Crewe and Rugby: the knock on the painted door, the sudden whitening of Verrall's sleek face, the small, sharp bark of the bull-dog. Justice. Not the magistrate's justice, which fined a man five shillings for a poached hare and gave the landlord a seat on the bench, but the old justice of blood and iron.

The train groaned to a halt at a wayside junction. On the platform, two soldiers in khaki stood beneath a green-washed lamp, their bayonets catching the celestial fire. One of them laughed, a hollow sound in the eerie stillness. Leadford pulled his cap low and waited for the whistle.

Salt in his nostrils now, sharp after the Midlands smoke, and the shingle grinding under his worn heels like broken teeth. The sea lay flat as a pewter dish, save where a long, oily swell broke in lines of pale green fire along the beach. Above the low roofs of the bungalows, the comet filled the whole vault of heaven—a vast, silent cataract of emerald light, so bright he could read the maker's stamp on his thumbnail.

Out beyond the headland, something thudded. Boom. Boom-boom. Not thunder. The air shivered against his eardrums, heavy and metallic. Away on the horizon, faint yellow sparks pricked the green gloom—ships at it in the Channel, men tearing iron and flesh while the sky itself burned cold.

Then he saw them.

They were walking along the wooden boardwalk above the marram grass, two dark figures against the luminous sand. Verrall had his arm about her waist; she was leaning her head against his shoulder, her white blouse glowing like a moth's wing in the unnatural light. A sudden, scalding wave broke behind Leadford's eyes. He did not think; his hand went straight to his pocket, his fingers closing on the checkered grip.

"Nettie!"

His voice cracked, hoarse from the train-dust and the salt. They stopped. She turned, her hand flying to her throat, and even at twenty paces he saw her face blanch—not with the comet's light, but with terror of him. Verrall stepped half a pace forward, squaring his shoulders, his cane rising slightly.

"Who's there? Leadford? What the devil—"

"Keep back, Verrall," Leadford panted, dragging the nickel-plated barrel clear of his lining. The metal gleamed like ice. "I've come for her. Or I've come for you. By God, I don't care which."

"Willie, don't!" Nettie's cry was thin, swallowed by the vast, hushed breathing of the sea.

Verrall's jaw set. "Put that down, you mad fool. There's a war started tonight—listen to the guns! Have you no sense?"

"My war's here!" Leadford thumbed the hammer back; the sharp click echoed off the shuttered bungalows. He raised his arm, sighting along the short barrel at the pale oval of Verrall's shirt-front. His heart hammered like a steam-hammer in the foundry, driving his finger onto the trigger.

Then the wind changed.

It came off the sea, warm and strangely sweet, carrying a scent like crushed clover and ozone, unlike any earthly air. Above them, the great green fan of the comet seemed to stoop, dissolving into a luminous mist that tumbled down across the dunes, wrapping the boardwalk, the shingle, the dark sea in a swirling veil of emerald cloud.

Leadford coughed. The trigger felt impossibly heavy under his index finger. A sudden, immense weariness washed through his limbs, soft as warm water, loosening the knotted sinews of his neck and shoulders. The gun dipped. He saw Verrall stagger, clutching at the wooden rail, his eyes wide and bewildered; he saw Nettie sink slowly to her knees in the marram grass, her hands open like a sleeping child's. The distant booming of the fleet faded into a murmur, and Leadford, still clinging to the cold iron, slumped sideways onto the sand as the green dark folded gently over his eyes.

Light. Not the bruised, sultry glare of the pit-fires, nor the sickly phosphor of the night before, but a morning washed clean as new glass.

Leadford sat up, brushing dry sand from his cheek. His head was clear—strangely, piercingly clear, as though a thick cobweb of soot and fever had been swept from the rafters of his brain. The air tasted of honey and salt. Down by the water's edge, the tide lapped quietly over the pebbles, and far out in the Channel, where the grey shapes of two cruisers drifted motionless, no smoke rose, no gun spoke.

Beside him on the boards, Verrall was stirring, propping himself on one elbow, blinking at the pale blue sky. Nettie sat with her knees drawn up, her hair loose about her shoulders, looking down at her own hands in quiet wonder.

Leadford looked at the little revolver lying in the sand beside his boot. It seemed an absurd, ugly toy—a lump of bent iron made by frightened children in the dark. He picked it up, weighed it once in his palm, and tossed it into a clump of sea-holly, where it sank out of sight.

Verrall met his eyes. There was no hatred in the gentleman's face now, only a bewildered, naked shame, and Leadford felt no triumph, no sting of old pride—only a vast, aching pity for the three of them, and for all the blind, screaming millions in the smoky towns behind him.

"Willie," Nettie said softly, her voice stripped of fear.

He stood up, his patched coat hanging loose, and held out his hand—first to her, then to the man beside her—to help them both to their feet in the new day.
