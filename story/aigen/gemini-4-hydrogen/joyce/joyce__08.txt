Smoke-choked Swathinglea lay under its pall of kiln-ash, a sprawling scab of brick and slag-heap where the sky itself seemed ground between the potters’ wheels. William Leadford walked the greasy cobbles, his bootsoles thin as wafers, letting the cold damp of the Midlands seep into his marrow. In his breast pocket, folded till the creases tore, her letter crackled like dry leaves. Do not follow, Will. I am sorry. Sorry. The word tasted of copper and stale vinegar. He saw her still in the lane behind the vicarage, the pale oval of her face tilted up, the way her fingers—stained once with clay and berry-juice—had smoothed the lapel of Verrall’s tweed coat. Verrall of the big house, Verrall of the humming motorcar that spat blue grease upon the hedges and left the poor choking in its wake. Let them choke, the lot of them. Above the jagged teeth of the bottle-ovens, where the twilight hung bruised and sulphurous, a strange green smear widened in the east. Folk at the corner-shops gaped at it between spits of tobacco, muttering of judgment and the end of days, their dull eyes turned upward from the gutter. Leadford spat too. Let it fall, he thought, let the green star drop like a millstone on the belfries and the banks and the soft white hands that never turned a lathe. His knuckles whitened in his pockets, hard as flints, while the blood hammered behind his eyes: mine, she was mine, before his gold bought her laughter.

In the kitchen, the tallow dip guttered against the grease-spotted wall, throwing long, twitching shadows over his mother’s shawl. She coughed—that dry, rattling bark of the flint-mills—and stirred the thin broth over the dying coals. On the oilcloth table lay the Sentinel, its ink smudged by his thumb, screaming of ultimatums in the North Sea, of ironclads steaming through the grey fog toward Kiel, of kings and cabinet-ministers rattling their sabres in gilded rooms. War. Always their war, while the likes of him fed the furnaces and swallowed the dust. He turned the page, though his eyes saw neither the print nor the tallow’s flame, only the cold, oily weight resting beneath his mattress upstairs. Five chambers, six, a dull blue barrel bought from a pawnbroker in Hanley with the last of his saved shillings—the shillings meant for a parlour clock, a linen tablecloth, a life. A poor man’s justice, he muttered against his teeth. His mother looked up, her eyes rheumy and fearful in the dimness. "Willum," she whispered, "the sky’s gone queer. Like marsh-fire." He did not answer. He rose, the floorboards groaning under his tread, and fetched the heavy coat from its peg. Let Europe burn; let the Kaiser and the squires tear each other’s throats out over their borders and their coal-pits. He had a smaller, sharper reckoning to settle by the sea, where the rich went to hide their shame behind white curtains and salt wind.

The third-class carriage rattled eastward through the night, a wooden box of stale pipe-smoke and snoring clerks, its wheels beating a relentless catechism into his skull: Verrall and Nettie, Verrall and Nettie, ash to ash. Outside the soot-streaked glass, the Midlands fell away—no more the red glare of blast-furnaces, no more the skeletal pit-head gears—and in their place stretched the flat, sleeping fields of the fens, bathed in a sickly, unearthly emerald. The comet had grown. It hung now like a great, luminous jellyfish trailing veils of verdigris across the stars, its light bleaching the hedgerows and turning the sheep-dotted meadows the colour of drowned flesh. Leadford sat with his chin sunk into his collar, his right hand locked around the cold butt of the revolver in his pocket. Beside him, a commercial traveller with a bulbous nose woke, blinked at the green-washed window, and crossed himself before muttering about the German fleet. Leadford watched the man’s trembling jowls with a cold, acid contempt. Fear, always fear, driving them like sheep to the slaughterhouse or the altar. He felt no fear. Only a tight, whistling emptiness where his heart had been, a clear sharp hunger for the moment when Verrall’s smooth, condescending smile would crack into terror. When the train hissed to a halt at Shaphambury, the station lamps looked pale and foolish, tiny yellow sparks drowned in that vast, descending tide of green.

He found them on the chalk downs above the town, where the sea sighed against the shingle far below like a beast turning in its sleep. The night was breathless, thick with a strange, sweetish heaviness that clung to the throat, neither salt nor heather, but something older, sharper, like bruised mint and lightning. Ahead, two silhouettes paused by the cliff-path’s rail—the wide brim of her straw hat, the tall, easy slouch of Verrall’s shoulders. Leadford’s boots grated on the flint. They turned together. "Will?" Her voice came thin across the turf, stripped of all its summer warmth, and Verrall stepped half a pace before her, his white shirt-front gleaming like a target in the emerald gloom. "Leadford," Verrall said, calm as a magistrate, "this is madness. Put your hand out of your pocket." A laugh tore out of Leadford’s chest, dry and jagged. He drew the iron out; it looked small and black against the glowing sea, yet it held the whole weight of his twenty-four bitter years. "You stole her," he choked, his thumb dragging back the hammer with a crisp click. "You with your—" Then the wind stopped. Not merely died, but vanished, as if the world had drawn a single, immense breath and held it. A green mist, luminous and soft as swan’s-down, rolled up over the chalk lip of the cliff, swallowing the grass, swallowing Verrall’s outstretched hand, swallowing the muzzle of the gun. Leadford’s knees buckled. A vast, cool drowsiness poured through his skull, washing away the words, the rage, the sharp edges of the flint, until he sank sideways into the clover and knew nothing more.

When his eyelids lifted, the sun was up, yet it was a sun he had never seen before—golden and clear, pouring through an air so pure it stung his lungs like spring water. He lay upon the downland turf, his cheek pressed against a cluster of wild thyme, and for a long, silent moment he did not remember his name. He only knew the sky. It was an immense, trembling dome of azure, washed clean of every smudge of smoke, every phantom of that green and terrible star. Slowly, he pushed himself up on his elbows. Beside his knee lay the revolver, its dull steel beaded with morning dew. He stared at it, and a queer, breathless wonder took hold of him, followed by a flush of deep, scorching shame. What fever had possessed him? What blind, gibbering ape had crawled out of the Staffordshire soot with a tube of iron in its paw, hungry to spill blood on the grass? It seemed to him that his whole life until this hour had been spent in a dark, airless cellar, squinting through keyhole cracks at shadows, mistaking his own bruised vanity for justice. He picked up the pistol—not in anger, not even in haste—and tossed it over the cliff’s edge, watching it glint once in the sunlight before the sea took it. Far down in the town, no chimneys smoked; no whistles blew. A profound, listening stillness lay over the roofs of Shaphambury, as though the entire waking earth were drawing its first clean breath.

A few paces off, Verrall sat upon a flat boulder, his elbows on his knees, staring out across the glittering water as if he too were reading a script newly written upon the waves. Nettie stood beside him, her hat gone, her dark hair loose about her shoulders, her eyes wide and strangely steady in the morning light. When Leadford walked toward them, there was no trembling in his limbs, no knot of bile beneath his ribs. "Will," Nettie said softly, and this time there was no fear in her voice, only a quiet, wondering recognition, as if they were three travellers meeting after a long wreck upon a strange shore. Verrall looked up, his face stripped of its old arrogance, pale and humble in the dawn. "We were fools, Leadford," he said simply, his voice carrying clearly in the windless air. "I more than you, perhaps. I thought a woman could be carried off like a prize from a fair, and you thought—well, we were all asleep in the dark." Leadford sat down upon the grass near them, feeling the warmth of the earth soak through his worn trousers. Below, on the horizon where yesterday the grey cruisers had prowled like wolves, the sea lay empty and bright, unbroken save for a single white sail drifting homeward. He thought of his mother coughing in the dark kitchen, of the men at the kilns, of the madness that had nearly drowned the world in blood, and knew—with a certainty deeper than words—that the kilns would burn clean, that the guns were rusting in the grass, and that their real lives had only just begun.
