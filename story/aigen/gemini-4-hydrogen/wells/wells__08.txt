I see myself still, as if through the wrong end of a telescope, a small, frantic creature hurrying along the soot-stained platforms of the Midland Railway with a cheap, five-chambered revolver knocking against my hip. In those last days before the Change, my mind was a dark little cellar of wounded pride and class hatred. Nettie was gone—Nettie, whose hands I had held beside the Staffordshire canal, whose laughter had been the one bright thread in the grimy fabric of my clerkship—gone with young Verrall, the son of the great colliery owner, as effortlessly as a bird flies over a brick wall. I had scraped together my last sovereigns, bought the pistol in a Birmingham pawnshop, and set out southward to find them. Around me, the whole of England seemed to share my fever. Every station newsstall was choked with men snatching at damp evening papers that screamed of the German ultimatum, of cruisers steaming into the North Sea, of a continent sliding helplessly toward the pit. Nobody looked up. Nobody paused to marvel at the sky, where, above the drifting smoke of the potteries and the red glare of the blast-furnaces, the great green comet hung like an unearthly lantern, growing larger and more luminous with every passing hour. We were too busy nursing our little hatreds, national and personal, to heed the silent stranger leaning over the rim of our world.

By the time I reached Shaphambury-on-Sea, night had fallen, though it was a night unlike any I had ever known. The gas-lamps along the esplanade looked yellow and sickly beneath the vast, emerald radiance spilling from the heavens. Yet the promenade was crowded with holidaymakers and townsfolk, chattering with a shrill, unnatural gaiety that could not quite mask their terror. A newsboy ran past me, his mouth stretched wide: "War declared! Great naval battle imminent!" A gentleman in a silk hat cursed him and bought a paper anyway, his fingers trembling as he fumbled for a penny. I pushed through them, deaf to their panic, my eyes searching every brightly lit hotel veranda and shadowy bench. It was an old boatman, smoking a clay pipe by the breakwater, who pointed me toward the lonely stretch of shingle beyond the town, where a cluster of wooden bungalows sat facing the tide. "A young swell and his lady," he muttered, squinting at my desperate face. "Took the furthest one, by the tamarisk hedge. You'll find 'em if you've a mind to." I did not thank him. I turned onto the crunching pebbles, the sea sighing softly on my left, the great green star blazing directly ahead. My hand closed around the cold, greasy iron in my pocket, and a terrible, righteous joy swelled in my chest.

I found them standing near the low timber porch of the last bungalow, looking out toward the dark horizon of the Channel. Nettie wore a pale cloak that caught the greenish phosphorescence of the sky; she looked slight and terribly young, her hand resting lightly on Verrall's sleeve. The sight of that careless, possessive touch snapped the last thread of my restraint. I scrambled up the bank of shingle, my boots scattering the stones, and called her name. She gasped and shrank back against Verrall, who stepped forward at once with that easy, aristocratic poise I had hated all my life. "Leadford?" he said, his voice steady enough, though I saw his eyes dart to my right hand as I drew the revolver. "Put that thing away, man. You're mad." I laughed, a dry, barking sound that scarcely seemed my own. I poured out all the accumulated bitterness of my twenty-two years—the cramped cottage, the grinding poverty, the stolen hours, the contempt of his kind for mine. Nettie began to weep, begging me to listen, saying she had never meant to break me, that she had only wanted to breathe a freer air. Far out at sea, a dull, heavy thud vibrated through the soles of our boots, followed by another and another—the distant thunder of the fleet opening fire in the dark. Even as I raised the barrel toward Verrall's chest, the air around us seemed to thicken, growing strangely sweet and heavy.

My finger tightened on the trigger. There was a sharp, futile crack, and the bullet whined harmlessly off into the night, for at that very instant a sudden, inexplicable faintness swept through my limbs. I staggered, dropping to one knee upon the shifting pebbles. Looking up, I saw that the comet had vanished behind a vast, swirling veil of luminous green mist that was descending swiftly upon the earth, swallowing the stars and blotting out the distant flashes of the naval guns. A curious scent filled my nostrils, like crushed clover and warm rain, intoxicating and profound. Verrall was swaying where he stood, one arm still thrown clumsily around Nettie's shoulders as if to shield her from a storm he could not understand. "What is it?" Nettie whispered, her voice already thick with sleep. "Willie—the air—" I tried to rise, tried to summon back the hot, sustaining fury that had driven me across half of England, but it was slipping away like water through my fingers. The revolver dropped from my numb hand and clattered softly among the stones. All along the coast, from the crowded promenade of Shaphambury to the great darkened cities beyond, I felt rather than heard a vast, collective sigh as a million restless hearts surrendered at once. My cheek met the cool shingle, the emerald fog wrapped me in a velvet silence, and the old, mad world went out like a blown candle.

I woke to a silence so pure and crystalline that for a long moment I fancied myself dead. Above me stretched a sky of unimaginable depth and clarity, washed clean of every smudge of smoke and cloud, glowing with the pale, pearly light of early dawn. I sat up slowly, drawing a deep breath, and at once a marvel happened within me. It was as though a heavy, dirty shutter had been flung open in a long-closed room. The air I breathed was crisp and buoyant, and with it came a luminous, astonishing sanity. I looked down at the revolver lying beside my knee—a squat, ugly lump of blackened metal—and a wave of gentle, almost humorous pity washed over me for the poor, blind, tortured creature I had been only a few hours before. How small my pride seemed now! How absurd that I should have wished to extinguish another human life because a girl's heart had turned elsewhere! I picked up the pistol, weighed it briefly in my palm, and tossed it far out into the gently lapping tide, watching the ripples widen and fade. Then I turned my head. A few yards away, huddled together upon the shingle beneath the pale light of the morning, Verrall and Nettie were beginning to stir.

Nettie opened her eyes first, sitting up and brushing a stray lock of hair from her forehead with a gesture of quiet wonder. When she saw me, there was no terror in her face, no shrinking—only a deep, searching recognition, as if we had both survived a long and perilous fever and were meeting now in the cool light of convalescence. Beside her, Verrall rose unsteadily to his feet, looking down at his rumpled evening clothes and then out across the glassy, silent sea where no guns thundered anymore. "Leadford," he said softly, and his voice had lost its brittle arrogance, sounding merely human and bewildered. I stood up and walked toward them, feeling neither triumph nor humiliation, only an immense, tender kinship with these two fellow-wanderers. Nettie reached out and took my hand, and then Verrall's, holding us both for a quiet moment while the sun broke cleanly over the edge of the world, turning the wet pebbles to gold. Down along the distant esplanade, figures were beginning to appear—townsfolk, soldiers in rumpled khaki, mothers with children in their arms—all pausing in the streets to look at one another with the same startled, forgiving eyes. We three turned together toward the waking town, stepping lightly over the shingle, knowing without a word being spoken that the long night of our folly was over, and that everything we had yet to build was waiting for us in the morning light.
