I was, in those last dark months of the old dispensation, a bitter and narrow-hearted young man. My world was bounded by the blackened brick walls of Clayton, by the eternal, coughing grime of the potteries, and by the cramped ledger-desk where I earned eighteen shillings a week. Yet I had one bright thing—or so my jealous, starving soul insisted—and that was Nettie Stuart. When she vanished from her father’s lodge at Checkhill Park, and the whisper went round the taprooms that young Edward Verrall had taken her away in his motor-car to the south coast, something snapped inside my head like a rusted spring. I did not merely grieve; I raged with the wounded, proprietorial fury of my class and time. I bought a cheap five-chambered revolver from a pawnbroker in Swathinglea, wrapped it in an old silk handkerchief, and set out to kill them both. All England, indeed, seemed to share my fever. That very evening, as I walked to the station beneath a sky stained crimson by the blast furnaces, the newsboys were shouting of ultimatums in the Balkans and the mobilisation of the North Sea fleet. Above the murk and the clamour, hanging low in the east like a cold, emerald eye, burned the great green comet. The astronomers wrote of harmless cyanogen and spectral lines, while the preachers called it a portent of wrath; I scarcely looked at it, save to wish its pale light might guide my bullet straight to Verrall’s heart.

The southward train was packed with men in khaki and flushed, shouting clerks who passed cheap newspapers from hand to hand. Everywhere I heard the same harsh, foolish chatter—of German cruisers, of honour, of teaching the foreigners a lesson before the week was out. They were drunk on the prospect of blood, just as I was drunk on my own petty, murderous grievance. I sat wedged in a corner of the third-class carriage, my hand pressed constantly against the hard lump in my coat pocket, staring out at the darkening fields of the Midlands. As night deepened, the comet grew terribly bright. It was no longer a mere smudge of mist among the stars, but a vast, luminous fan of pale jade that seemed to lean down toward the spinning earth, paling the signal lamps along the embankment and casting a ghostly, unearthly sheen over the huddled roofs of London as we rattled through the suburbs. Nobody in the carriage looked at it for long; they were too busy arguing about the price of bread and the strength of the Channel Squadron. When I stepped out at last onto the platform at Shaphambury, the salt wind from the sea was warm and heavy, and the eastern horizon pulsed with a faint, rhythmic glow—whether from the searchlights of the fleet or the approaching tail of the comet, I could not tell. I found a wretched attic room near the railway arches, flung myself fully clothed upon the horsehair mattress, and lay awake till dawn, rehearsing the words I would spit at Nettie before I fired.

By ten o’clock the next morning, Shaphambury was a bright, bustling little hell of flags and holidaymakers. The newspapers on the promenade announced in vast black type that war had been declared at midnight, and a brass band was braying patriotic airs near the pier, though the sea beyond looked strangely leaden and still under a sky that had lost its honest blue. I wandered along the esplanade with my collar turned up, my boots dusty with Staffordshire clay, a gaunt, scowling shadow among the white flannels and parasols. For three hours I hunted them through the hotels and tea-gardens, until at last, high up on the chalk path that leads toward the downs above Bungalow Village, I saw Nettie’s straw hat. She was walking beside Verrall, her hand resting lightly on his tweed sleeve, and she was laughing. That light, careless laugh struck me like a blow across the mouth. They looked so clean, so effortlessly secure in their stolen paradise, while I was a thing of soot and gall. I followed them at a distance as they left the crowded promenade and climbed the steep, springy turf of the headland, where the gorse bushes offered cover and the sea-gulls wheeled below the cliff edge. Far out in the haze of the Channel, I could see the dark, slender silhouettes of four warships steaming eastward, their funnels trailing long ribbons of black smoke against the sickly green glare that was already beginning to swallow the midday sun.

They stopped in a little hollow of the downs, screened from the town by a crest of yellow gorse. Verrall spread his coat upon the grass, and Nettie sat down, unpinning her hat and shaking out her dark hair. I came over the ridge behind them, my breath whistling through my teeth, my right hand buried in my pocket. “Nettie!” I called, and my voice sounded cracked and hideous even to my own ears. She gave a sharp little cry and scrambled to her feet, her face going white as the chalk beneath us. Verrall rose more slowly, stepping between us with that insolent, well-bred calm I had hated all my life. “Leadford?” he said, frowning as if I were some troublesome beggar. “What the devil are you doing here?” I did not answer him with reason; reason had no place in that old, frantic world. I poured out all the accumulated poison of my days—of how she was mine, of how he had bought her with his father’s money, of the filth and the toil that had bred me only to be robbed by his kind. As I shouted, I dragged the revolver from my pocket and levelled it at his chest. Nettie shrieked and threw her arms around him. I tightened my finger on the trigger, waiting for the roar—when suddenly the air grew thick and sweet, like bruised almonds and warm rain. A blinding curtain of green mist swept in from the sea, swallowing the sun, swallowing the distant ships, and my knees buckled beneath me as a vast, irresistible slumber struck me down into the turf.

How long we lay there upon the hillside, I have never rightly known, though the astronomers tell us the earth was three hours passing through the heart of that benevolent vapour. I remember no dreams—only a sensation of profound, cool stillness, as though a fevered room had suddenly had all its windows flung wide to the morning air. When I opened my eyes, the green fog had vanished, and the sky above me was a dome of the most exquisite, crystalline blue I had ever beheld. The sun was low in the east, flooding the Channel with pure gold. I sat up slowly, blinking, and looked down at my right hand. The cheap revolver still lay nestled in my palm, its nickelled barrel gleaming in the fresh light. I stared at it for a long moment with a curious, detached perplexity, much as a grown man might stare at a wooden sword he had brandished in his nursery. All the hot, suffocating rage that had driven me from Staffordshire—the wounded vanity, the cruel lust to possess and destroy—had been washed clean out of my brain. I felt light, clear-headed, and extraordinarily compassionate, as if I were seeing the world for the first time without the smoke of my own selfishness in my eyes. Down below, Shaphambury lay utterly silent, its roofs sparkling in the dew, no longer clamorous with bands or newsboys. Even the distant booming of the practice guns from the forts along the coast had ceased, leaving only the gentle, rhythmic sigh of the tide against the chalk.

A few paces away, Nettie stirred upon the grass and sat up, pushing the damp hair from her forehead. Beside her, Verrall raised himself on one elbow, looking about him with a dazed, gentle wonder. Neither of them shrank from me as I rose to my feet; indeed, when Nettie’s eyes met mine, there was no terror in them, only a quiet, puzzled recognition, as though we had both awakened from the same dark and troubled dream. I walked across to the edge of the cliff, tossed the little revolver far out into the empty air, and watched it spin down into the green water below. When I turned back, Verrall was standing, brushing the clover from his coat, and looking out toward the sea. Following his gaze, I saw those four great warships again; they lay motionless now upon the sunlit water, their guns silent, their black smoke gone, like iron monsters that had suddenly forgotten why they were made. “It is over, isn’t it?” Nettie said softly, rising to stand between us. “The madness, I mean.” I looked at her, and then at Verrall, and for the first time in my life I felt no envy of his fine clothes or his easy grace, and no bitter claim upon the woman I loved. I saw them simply as two fellow-creatures, frail and bewildered like myself, standing at the threshold of a cleaner day. “Yes,” I answered, and my voice was steady and kind. “The old world is dead, Nettie. Let us go down into the town and see what we must build in its place.”
