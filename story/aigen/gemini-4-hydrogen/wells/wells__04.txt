William Leadford stood in the drizzle outside the pawnshop in Clayton, the heavy little revolver nestled like a cold toad in the pocket of his frayed overcoat. Behind him, the Potteries crouched under their eternal canopy of coal smoke, a labyrinth of sooty brick, slag-heaps, and bottle-necked kilns belching dull orange sparks into the darkening afternoon. His mother had wept silently by the cold grate when he told her he was leaving, her worn hands twisting her apron, knowing nothing of the murder in his heart, only that the strike had left them starving. Nettie had left a single, blotted note—cruel in its timid brevity—and gone south with young Verrall, whose father owned half the mines and all the smooth, insolent leisure of Checkshill.

Leadford turned his face upward. Above the murk of the chimneys, the clouds were tearing apart, and there, hanging low in the eastern sky, was the Comet. For weeks the newspapers had been full of it, printing columns of frantic astronomy beside screaming headlines about the German fleet. Now it was no longer a mere smudge of light; it was a vast, pale-green scimitar, luminous and cold, casting an unearthly tint upon the wet slate roofs and the stagnant canal. The men at the pithead said it was a sign of war; the preachers in the tin chapels said it was the wrath of God. Leadford spat into the gutter. Let the world burn, let the empires tear one another to rags—he had only one errand left, and six brass cartridges to see it done.

The third-class carriage rattled southward through the Midlands, choked with stale tobacco smoke and the damp smell of wool. Leadford sat wedged in a corner, his hat pulled low, his right hand never leaving the iron grip in his pocket. Opposite him, two commercial travellers and a red-faced sergeant argued furiously over a crumpled Daily Telegraph.

"They’ve crossed the line, I tell you," the sergeant barked, jabbing a thick finger at the newsprint. "Ultimatum expires at midnight. Our cruisers are steaming out of Rosyth. It’s war at last, and high time we taught those fellows a lesson."

One of the travellers nodded solemnly, though his eyes strayed nervously toward the window. Outside, in the gathering dusk, the green glow of the Comet seemed to walk beside the train, illuminating the flooded meadows and skeletal elms with a spectral, copper-sulphate radiance. "It’s an ugly sky for it," he muttered. "The Professor says the tail’s sixty million miles long, and we’re steering straight into the thick of it."

"Gas and moonshine," scoffed the sergeant. "Let the astronomers mind their telescopes; we’ve got battleships to mind."

Leadford listened with dull contempt. Theirs was the same blind stupidity that ruled everything—the masters who ground wages down, the politicians who played with armies, the rich young gentlemen who took a poor man’s sweetheart as idly as they might pluck a flower from a hedge. He closed his eyes, seeing only Nettie’s dark hair and Verrall’s effortless smile. The train’s iron rhythm beat a single word into his skull: Kill, kill, kill.

By the time he reached Shaphambury, night had fallen, though it was a night unlike any the world had ever known. The seaside town lay drenched in a pale, emerald twilight. Gas lamps along the esplanade burned yellow and sickly against the overwhelming, phosphorescent glare of the Comet, which now spanned half the heavens like a gigantic, misty fan of green fire.

Newsboys ran along the promenade, their shrill voices cracking in the salt wind. "War! War declared! Great naval battle in the North Sea!" Gentlemen in evening dress and ladies clutching their wraps stood in frightened knots outside the grand hotels, staring upward with white faces. Yet Leadford moved among them like a ghost, impervious to their panic. A clerk at a small boarding-house, bribed with the last of Leadford’s silver and cowed by his desperate glare, had named the secluded bungalow on the western cliffs where a young gentleman from Staffordshire had taken rooms for a lady.

Leadford climbed the winding chalk path away from the town. Below him, the sea heaved in long, oily swells that caught the green light and broke in ghostly foam upon the shingle. A distant, rhythmic thudding vibrated through the air—whether it was the guns of the Channel Fleet far out in the dark, or simply the pounding of blood in his temples, he could not tell. Everything in him was narrowed to a single, burning point. Ahead, nestled among wind-twisted tamarisks at the cliff’s edge, a square of warm lamplight glowed through a curtained window. He drew the revolver, cocked it with a sharp click, and stepped over the low stone wall.

The French windows stood slightly ajar, letting out a murmur of voices and the faint clink of china. Leadford crept across the damp turf until he stood within three paces of the threshold. Through the gap in the curtains, he saw them.

Nettie sat by a small table, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. She did not look happy; there was a pinched, startled look about her mouth, and her dark eyes were fixed upon the floor. Verrall stood by the mantelpiece in a loose tweed jacket, looking down at her with a mixture of impatience and tender perplexity.

"You mustn't brood like this, Nettie," Verrall said, his voice carrying that smooth, cultivated cadence Leadford hated above all things. "Everybody’s lost their heads over this wretched war and that green thing in the sky. Tomorrow we’ll cross to France, and leave all that squalor behind."

"It isn't just the war," Nettie whispered. "It’s Willie. I keep thinking of what he’ll do."

Leadford stepped forward and flung the glass doors wide. The cold night air rushed in, making the lamp flame gutter wildly. Nettie gave a sharp cry and sprang to her feet, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall spun round, his face darkening with shock and instant recognition.

"Leadford!" Verrall barked, stepping between the intruder and the girl. "Put that thing down, you madman!"

Leadford did not answer. His chest heaved; his finger tightened on the trigger. For months he had been starved and mocked by life, and now the whole edifice of his misery stood before him in this confident young man. "Stand aside, Verrall," he hissed. "I’ve come to settle the account."

Verrall did not flinch, though his eyes widened as he saw the black muzzle trembling inches from his chest. "If you shoot me, Leadford, you’ll only hang for it," he said, keeping his voice steady. "It won't bring her back."

"Willie, please!" Nettie sobbed, darting forward to seize Verrall’s arm. "Don't! For God's sake, Willie, don't!"

Leadford’s vision swam in a red haze of fury. He raised the barrel, aiming straight at Verrall’s heart—and at that exact instant, the world outside seemed to draw a great, shuddering breath.

The roaring of the surf below the cliffs fell silent, as if a heavy curtain had been drawn across the sea. A strange, cool wind swept through the open doors, carrying a faint, sweet odour—a scent like crushed laurel leaves and ozone, sharp and intoxicatingly pure. Outside, the darkness had vanished; the sky had dissolved into a luminous sea of emerald mist that rolled silently over the downs and poured into the room like water.

Leadford tried to pull the trigger, but his fingers turned to lead. A profound, delicious numbness crept up his arm and spread through his chest. The red rage in his brain evaporated like smoke. Across the room, Nettie swayed, her eyes closing peacefully as she sank to her knees. Verrall reached out to catch her, staggered, and looked at Leadford with a curious, dazed wonder rather than fear before sliding gently to the carpet.

Leadford’s knees buckled. The revolver slipped from his nerveless hand and clattered against the fender. As he sank into the cool, green-lit darkness, he heard no sound of war—only a vast, crystalline silence settling over the Earth.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was shining. It was not the smoky, grudging dawn of Staffordshire, nor the feverish glare of the night before, but a clear, golden morning of unimaginable brilliance. He lay upon the floorboards near the open window, breathing deeply of an air so crisp and vital that every inhalation felt like a draught of cold spring water.

He sat up slowly, rubbing his forehead. Beside him lay the little pawnshop revolver. He picked it up, turned it over in his palm, and stared at it with a sudden, overwhelming astonishment. It looked so small, so ugly, so pitifully absurd—a greasy scrap of iron designed by frightened apes to tear one another’s flesh. What madness had possessed him? Looking back across the gulf of that green sleep, his jealousy, his pride, and his murderous rage seemed like the frantic nightmares of a sick child.

Across the room, Verrall was stirring. He sat up, blinking in the bright sunlight, and looked first at Nettie—who was waking softly with a sigh—and then at Leadford. There was no hatred in Verrall’s eyes, only a clear, grave recognition, as if two strangers who had fought in the dark had suddenly seen each other’s faces by day.

"Leadford," Verrall said quietly, his voice stripped of its old arrogance. "Are you hurt?"

"No," Leadford answered, his own voice sounding strange and gentle in his ears. He walked to the doorway and tossed the revolver far out into the dew-drenched grass beyond the garden wall.

Outside, the sea sparkled beneath a stainless blue sky. Somewhere beyond the horizon, the warships were drifting silent and forgotten, their crews waking to the same unclouded sanity. He turned back to Nettie, trembling in the golden light, and felt no bitterness—only a deep, quiet pity for all they had suffered in the dark world that had passed away.
