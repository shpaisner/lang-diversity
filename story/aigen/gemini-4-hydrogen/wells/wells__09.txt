In those last dark days of the old world, I lived with my mother in a cramped brick cottage at Swathinglea, in the very throat of the Staffordshire Potteries. By day the sky was a smeared rag of coal-smoke; by night the blast-furnaces glared like open wounds along the horizon. I was twenty-two, a clerk out of a berth, with frayed cuffs, an empty pocket, and a heart sour with the insolence of my betters. Only one thing in that mean, blackened landscape seemed worth the drawing of breath, and that was Nettie. Yet even Nettie had grown distant, her eyes fixed upon things beyond our narrow street.

Then came the Tuesday evening when I found her little note tucked beneath my mother’s teapot, and the whole edifice of my miserable life collapsed. She had gone. She had slipped away on the afternoon train with Edward Verrall, the young gentleman from Checkshill Towers, whose father owned half the mines in the valley. I stood by the scullery window, crushing the paper in my fist until my nails bit my palm. Above the jagged slate roofs and smoking chimneys, the new comet hung like a green lantern in the east. For weeks the penny papers had written of it, a wandering vapour rushing down upon the sun, swelling night by night until its pale emerald glare outshone Jupiter. Now it seemed to mock me—a cold, indifferent jewel set above my ruin. I swore then, between clenched teeth, that Verrall should not keep her, though I had to walk to the edge of the world to tear her from his arm.

The next morning I pawned my dead father’s silver watch in Clayton and bought a cheap, five-chambered revolver from a dusty shop near the canal. The iron weight of it in my ulster pocket gave me a dreadful, intoxicating comfort. All that day I haunted the railway station and the servants’ alleys of Checkshill, piecing together their flight from stray words and bribed porters. They had taken the southern express; they were bound for Shaphambury, a quiet watering-place on the coast where Verrall’s family kept a summer bungalow.

While I nursed my private, murderous rage, the world around me was sliding into a madness of its own. Every newsboy at the junctions was bawling of the European crisis. The great powers had snapped their leashes at last; ultimatums were flying across the Channel like sparks from a foundry, and the North Sea was dark with ironclads. On the platform at Birmingham, I was jostled by laughing, half-drunken reservists singing patriotic choruses as they waited for their troop trains. I looked at their flushed, foolish faces with utter contempt. Let the empires tear each other to pieces, I thought; let London burn and the fleets go down in blood. What were their flags and frontiers to a man whose soul had been stolen by a rich man’s son? When my train rattled southward through the gathering dusk, the comet was brighter than ever, casting an uncanny greenish sheen upon the hedgerows and telegraph wires. I sat in the third-class carriage, my hand closed tight around the pistol, rehearsing the moment I should stand before them.

I reached Shaphambury late the following afternoon, stepping out into a town gripped by panic and strange light. The little seaside resort had lost all its holiday ease. Along the esplanade, carriages were piled high with trunks as wealthy visitors fled inland from the threat of coastal bombardment, while out at sea, dark smears of smoke marked patrolling cruisers. Even the air felt unnatural, heavy and breathless, as if the atmosphere were waiting for a thunderclap that refused to break. Above the whitewashed villas and restless grey water, the comet had grown into a vast, luminous fan of green fire filling a quarter of the heavens.

I did not linger on the promenade. Following a frightened cabman’s directions, I struck out along the chalk cliffs toward the lonely stretch of coast where the Verralls’ bungalow stood behind a windbreak of stunted pines. My boots were white with dust, my throat parched, and my mind burned with a single, clarifying fever. I was no longer William Leadford the underpaid clerk, bullied by foremen and despised by squires’ sons; I was the instrument of a fierce, elemental justice. As the sun sank behind the downs, the world did not grow dark in the accustomed way. Instead, that immense celestial plume drenched the sea and turf in a spectral, copper-green twilight. Looking down at my hands, I saw that my skin looked livid, like the flesh of a drowned man. Ahead of me, a warm yellow lamp flickered through the pines, and I heard the faint, careless sound of a woman’s laughter.

They were walking together along the narrow turf path at the cliff’s edge, Nettie’s hand resting lightly upon Verrall’s sleeve. She wore a pale summer dress that seemed to drink in the eerie emerald glow, and he was leaning toward her, speaking in that easy, confident voice I had hated since boyhood. I stepped out from the gorse bushes, twenty paces ahead, and drew the revolver from my pocket.

Nettie saw me first. Her hand flew to her mouth, and she gave a sharp cry: "Willie!" Verrall stopped dead, thrusting her gently behind his shoulder. To do him justice, he did not flinch, though his face went white beneath the starlight. "Leadford," he said, his voice tight and wary. "Put that thing down. You’re out of your mind."

"You stole her," I shouted, though my voice sounded thin and ridiculous against the booming surf below. "You bought her with your father’s money, as you buy everything else!" I raised the pistol, my finger tightening on the trigger. Nettie shrieked and caught Verrall’s arm; I fired twice, and the sharp cracks echoed wildly off the chalk walls. The bullets went wide, whistling harmlessly over the black sea. Before I could steady my wrist for a third shot, a sudden, rushing wind swept in from the water. It carried a curious, sweetish odour, like crushed almonds and summer clover, so thick it caught in the throat. The green sky seemed to descend upon us in a blinding, incandescent mist. My knees buckled, the pistol slipped from my numb fingers, and I fell forward into the grass as a profound, dreamless darkness swallowed the world.

When I opened my eyes, the green terror was gone from the sky. Above me stretched a dome of pure, washed blue, so crystalline and vast that it made my chest ache to behold it. The sun was newly risen over the Channel, pouring a flood of golden, immaculate warmth across the dewy grass. For a long minute I lay quite still, listening to a lark singing high above the downs, and wondering at the extraordinary lightness in my head. It was as though a heavy, festering bandage had been unwound from my brain, leaving every thought cool, distinct, and wholly free from pain.

I sat up and looked about me. A few yards away, half-hidden in a tuft of clover, lay my cheap revolver, its barrel beaded with morning moisture. I stared at it with sudden, overwhelming astonishment. What grotesque frenzy had possessed me to carry that ugly piece of iron across England? The hot, suffocating knot of jealousy and class-hatred that had been my very soul only hours before had vanished like coal-smoke in a gale. I felt no shame, only a deep, wondering pity for the blind, frantic creature I had been.

Near the cliff’s edge, Nettie and Verrall were stirring in the wet grass. Verrall pushed himself up on one elbow, blinking in the bright sunlight, and looked straight at me. There was no fear in his eyes now, nor any trace of his old, careless arrogance. He looked at me as one bewildered traveller looks at another after a long, dark journey. Beside him, Nettie sat up, brushing the grass from her hair, and she did not shrink away.

I rose and walked toward them, stepping over the discarded pistol without a second glance. Neither of them drew back as I approached. Instead, Verrall stood up and held out his hand—not with the condescension of a master, nor the bravado of a rival, but with a quiet, grave simplicity. I took it, and we stood for a moment in silence, three ordinary human beings washed ashore together by some vast, invisible tide.

"I meant to kill you both," I said, and my voice was calm and clear. "I cannot understand why, except that the world we lived in made us all a little mad."

"We were blind," Nettie murmured, looking out across the sunlit sea. "I thought I was running toward something free, Willie, and you thought you were defending something yours. Now everything looks so small, and so clear."

As she spoke, a coastguard came trudging along the cliff path, his cap pushed back and a look of radiant bewilderment upon his weathered face. He stopped when he saw us, eager to share his news. "The cruisers out yonder," he said, pointing toward three great warships lying motionless upon the glassy water. "They’ve hauled down their battle-flags. Word just came from Dover—the German fleet is steaming home, and the armies on the frontier have stacked their rifles to talk. Nobody knows how, sir, only that the green fog passed, and when we woke, the war was dead."

He walked on, whistling softly. I looked back at Nettie and Verrall, feeling no bitterness at seeing them together. The smoky walls of Swathinglea, the pride of Checkshill Towers, the whole cruel nightmare of our past had dissolved into the morning air, and I knew I would walk into this waking world without hatred, and without fear.
