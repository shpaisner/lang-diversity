In the mean, smoke-blackened streets of Clayton, where the potbanks crouched like sullen beasts under a perpetual canopy of coal-smoke, William Leadford sat by his attic window and nursed his bitterness. Before him on the deal table lay a cheap revolver, its dull steel catching the last, jaundiced light of the Staffordshire evening, and beside it a crumpled newspaper whose black headlines screamed of ultimatums, mobilized fleets, and the imminent collision of empires. Europe, it seemed, was staggering blindly toward the abyss. Yet to Leadford, cramped in his narrow, ink-stained existence as a clerk, the approaching war was merely a larger, noisier version of the cruelty he already knew. His own world had ended three days ago, when Nettie Stuart—his Nettie, with her soft eyes and hesitant laughter—had vanished into the south with Edward Verrall. Verrall, with his crisp flannels, his effortless money, his insolent gentleman’s smile! Leadford looked up from the weapon to the sky above the jagged slate roofs. There, in the clear space between two chimney stacks, hung the comet. Night after night it had swollen, no longer a faint, misty smudge among the constellations, but a great, pale-green portent trailing a luminous fan across the zenith. The colliers in the taverns muttered that it meant judgment, or pestilence, or the end of all things. Leadford only knew that its cold, emerald radiance illuminated the squalor of his room and hardened his resolve. He slipped the revolver into his coat pocket, took his hat, and went down into the darkening street, bound for the railway station and the sea.

The south-bound train was a feverish microcosm of a nation losing its wits. At every junction along the Midlands, the platforms seethed with reservists in ill-fitting khaki, weeping women, and red-faced youths waving cheap paper flags and roaring patriotic ballads out of tune. Inside the third-class compartment, the air was thick with shag tobacco and shouted rumors. Men leaned forward, tapping their newspapers with soiled forefingers, boasting of dreadnoughts and swift victories in the North Sea, their eyes bright with a strange, vicarious ferocity. Leadford sat wedged in a corner, saying nothing, his hand resting against the hard, reassuring lump in his pocket. He despised them—these noisy, deluded clerks and laborers rushing to die for the very masters who starved them in peacetime—yet he felt no kinship with their victims either. His heart was a small, locked furnace of private hatred. As the train clattered through the darkening countryside, past quiet hedgerows and sleeping villages, the clamor in the carriage subsided into uneasy dozing. Leadford pressed his forehead against the cool glass. Outside, the comet dominated the heavens. It had grown visibly larger since the night before, a vast, feathery broom of greenish-white fire that seemed to sweep the stars aside. Its light fell upon the rushing fields, turning the ripening wheat to an unearthly silver and casting long, spectral shadows from the telegraph poles. Watching it, Leadford felt a momentary, chilling sense of human insignificance, as though all the marching armies of Europe, and even his own desperate errand, were nothing more than the scurrying of ants beneath a descending foot.

Shaphambury lay bright and indifferent beside the English Channel, a town of white stucco, manicured lawns, and wide esplanades built entirely for the comfort of people who had never seen a coal pit. Leadford arrived in the glare of noon, dusty and hollow-eyed, his cheap serge suit glaringly out of place among the straw hats and parasols of the holidaymakers. Even here, however, the shadow of the coming war had begun to fall. Crowds gathered anxiously around the telegraph office, and out on the slate-grey horizon, two lean, dark shapes—torpedo-boat destroyers, someone whispered—steamed slowly westward, leaving smudged trails of smoke against the sky. Leadford scarcely glanced at them. For four hours he prowled the seafront and the cliff-top gardens, sustained by a bitter, sleepless vigilance, until at last he saw them. They were sitting on the terrace of the Grand Hotel, shaded by a striped awning. Verrall was leaning back in a wicker chair, laughing at something Nettie had said, his tanned hand resting lightly near hers on the white tablecloth. Nettie wore a summer dress of pale muslin; she looked younger, softer, and utterly untroubled, as though the smoky streets of Clayton and the man who had loved her there had never existed at all. A waiter poured wine into their glasses. At that sight, a hot, blinding wave of class-hatred and wounded pride surged through Leadford’s veins. He did not approach them in the daylight. Instead, he retreated to the tamarisk bushes along the cliff path, sat down on the dry turf, and waited for the sun to sink and the green star to rise.

Night fell with a sultry, breathless stillness, broken only by the distant wash of the tide and the sudden, frantic shouting of newsboys running along the parade. "War! War declared! Great naval battle expected!" The cries echoed off the white facades of the hotels, and windows were flung open as anxious guests leaned out into the gloom. Out over the Channel, a white searchlight beam snapped on, sweeping the dark water like a nervous finger. Leadford rose from his hiding place and stepped onto the gravel path. Ahead of him, walking slowly toward the quieter end of the cliff, were two familiar figures. Verrall had his arm around Nettie’s waist, speaking low and earnestly to reassure her against the distant clamor. Leadford’s footsteps crunched on the gravel. They turned. In the pale, eerie glow of the comet, which now filled a third of the sky with its ghostly emerald luminescence, Nettie’s face went white. "Will!" she gasped, clutching Verrall’s sleeve. Leadford drew the revolver. His hand was trembling violently, yet his voice came out flat and harsh. "Stay where you are, Verrall. You’ve stolen everything else in this world—you shan't have her without paying." Verrall stepped smoothly in front of Nettie, his jaw set, though his eyes widened at the sight of the barrel. "Put that down, Leadford," he said, trying to keep the tremor from his gentlemanly tones. "You're mad. There’s a war—" "I have my own war!" Leadford cried, raising the weapon. Then, suddenly, the words died in his throat. A strange, cool wind had begun to blow from the sea, and the light around them was thickening into a swirling, luminous mist.

At first it seemed as though a sea-fog had rolled up over the cliffs, yet no fog had ever carried this unearthly, translucent greenness, nor smelled so faintly of bruised mint and summer rain. The comet’s tail had struck the earth. From the town below, the shouts of the newsboys and the murmur of frightened crowds faltered and ceased, swallowed by a profound, velvety silence. Leadford tried to pull the trigger, to summon back the hot rage that had driven him across half of England, but his fingers had grown heavy and numb. The revolver slipped from his grasp and thudded softly onto the turf. Beside him, Nettie swayed, her hands falling from Verrall’s arm, her eyes wide with a quiet, unafraid wonder as she watched the emerald vapor curl around them like living silk. Verrall reached out to steady her, stumbled, and sank slowly to his knees on the grass. A vast, delicious weariness was stealing through Leadford’s limbs—not the exhaustion of his long journey, but a gentle unfastening of every knot in his body and mind. His chest rose and fell in deep, slow breaths of the cool, sweet air. He sank down beside the fallen weapon, his cheek pressing against the damp clover. Looking up, he saw the searchlight over the Channel flicker and die, leaving only the glowing, greenish canopy that veiled the stars. Somewhere in the dark, guns were waiting to fire and men were waiting to die, yet all of it seemed infinitely far away, like a childish nightmare fading before morning. Then Leadford closed his eyes, and slept.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the green mist was gone, and the morning sun was shining over the Channel with a clarity he had never seen in all his life. The air tasted crisp and extraordinarily pure. For a long moment he lay still on the cliff grass, listening to the gulls wheeling overhead and the gentle lap of the tide below. He felt as though he had been washed clean—as though a thick, smoky pane of glass between his mind and the world had been shattered, leaving everything sharp, luminous, and intelligible. Slowly, he sat up. There on the turf lay the revolver, its steel already dulling with dew. He picked it up, turned it over in his hands, and felt a sudden, overwhelming pity for the cramped, angry, miserable creature he had been the day before. How small, how pitifully blind that rage seemed now! A few paces away, Verrall was stirring, sitting up and passing a hand across his forehead, while Nettie woke beside him, blinking in the bright sunlight. Their eyes met Leadford’s. There was no terror in Nettie’s face, no arrogance in Verrall’s, and in Leadford’s own heart not a shred of the old, gnawing jealousy. Verrall looked at the revolver in Leadford’s hand, then up at Leadford’s quiet face, and a faint, rueful smile touched his lips. "We were all mad yesterday, weren't we?" Verrall said softly, his voice stripped of its old condescension. Leadford dropped the weapon into the tall grass and rose to his feet, looking out toward the sea, where the warships lay silent and motionless under the clear sky. "Yes," Leadford answered, offering his hand to help Nettie up. "The whole world was mad. Now we must begin again."
