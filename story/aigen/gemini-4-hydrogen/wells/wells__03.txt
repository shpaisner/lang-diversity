In the murky twilight of Swathinglea, where the blast furnaces stained the low clouds a bruised and sultry crimson, William Leadford sat in his cramped lodging and cleaned a cheap Belgian revolver. His fingers, stained with the ink of a colliery clerk’s desk, trembled not from fear, but from an acid, consuming vanity. Nettie was gone. She had slipped away from the soot-blackened terraces and the pinched, respectable poverty of her father’s house, and she had gone with Edward Verrall—Verrall, with his easy laugh, his Cambridge manners, and his father’s money. All week the Potteries had simmered with strikes and bitter talk, men standing idle at street corners with gaunt cheeks and angry eyes, while the London newspapers screamed of German cruisers in the North Sea and an ultimatum that could not wait until Monday. Leadford cared nothing for the Kaiser or the fleet, save that the general madness seemed to echo the fever in his own blood. When he stepped out into the narrow cobbled street to post a lying note to his mother, he paused and looked up between the towering brick chimneys. There it hung, high above the smoke-drift: the comet. A month ago it had been a mere smudge for astronomers; now it was a great, pale plume of greenish light, ghostly and indifferent, trailing across the stars. A neighbour’s boy pointed at it and laughed shrilly, and someone muttered that it boded war. Leadford thrust his hand deeper into his pocket, feeling the cold, oily barrel against his knuckles, and turned toward the station.

The southward train rattled through the darkened Midlands like a shuttle through a loom of iron and fire. Leadford sat in the corner of a third-class carriage, his collar turned up, watching the red glare of distant foundries dwindle into the black countryside. Opposite him, two commercial travellers and a red-faced recruiting sergeant were shouting over the clatter of the wheels, waving crumpled evening editions. Every headline was a drumbeat: mobilisation orders, reserves called up, the Channel Fleet steaming under sealed orders. The sergeant slapped his knee and swore they would teach the Germans a lesson before the month was out, while the travellers nodded fiercely, their eyes bright with a vicarious, cowardly bloodlust. Leadford despised them. He despised their loud voices, their cheap cigars, their comfortable certainty that the world was made for such noisy fools. Yet in his own breast burned the very same savage stupidity—a narrow, possessive fury that told him Nettie was his property, stolen by a superior class, and that only blood could wash out the slight. He closed his eyes and saw Verrall’s smooth face, Nettie’s quick, timid smile. Whenever he opened them again and glanced through the soot-streaked window, the green star was there, keeping pace with the train above the flying hedges. Its light was neither warm like the sun nor silver like the moon, but a strange, translucent emerald that touched the sleeping pastures with an unearthly pallor. By midnight, when the train reached the junction for the coast, the carriage was silent, its occupants slumped in uneasy sleep beneath that watching, spectral glow.

Shaphambury lay white and quiet under the morning mist, a little cluster of lodging-houses and chalk cliffs facing the grey expanse of the English Channel. To Leadford, whose lungs were seasoned to coal smoke and sulphur, the salt wind felt almost indecently clean. He had no money for a hotel, so he walked the length of the esplanade with a crust of bread in his pocket, his boots white with chalk dust, searching every veranda and bathing-machine for a glimpse of Nettie’s blue serge skirt. The town was half-empty, for the season had been blighted by the war scare; elderly ladies hurried indoors with their lapdogs, and a pair of coastguards stood by the flagstaff with brass telescopes trained upon the misty horizon. Twice Leadford heard the rumour that gunfire had been heard off the Downs, a dull, subterranean thumping across the water. He found them at last near noon, not hiding in shame as he had imagined, but walking together along the cliff path toward the golf links. Verrall wore a light flannel suit and carried a walking-stick, his head bent attentively as Nettie spoke. Seeing them there, framed against the bright sea, Leadford felt a sudden, sickening sense of his own insignificance—his frayed cuffs, his sallow, clerkish face, the clumsy weight of the pistol in his coat. He did not rush out. Instead, he slunk behind a clump of tamarisk and watched them turn back toward a row of neat red-brick villas above the bay, nursing his hatred until dusk should give him courage.

When night fell, it did not bring darkness. The comet had swollen until it spanned a third of the heavens, a stupendous, luminous fan of green vapour that turned the sea into a plain of rippled verdigris and bleached the white cliffs to the colour of bone. Even the wind had died, leaving a breathless, expectant stillness over the Channel. Leadford crouched among the gorse bushes above the cliff path, his teeth chattering despite the mildness of the air. From the town below came the faint, discordant sound of a brass band playing a patriotic air, followed by ragged cheering—men celebrating a war that had not yet killed them. Then the garden gate of the villa clicked. Nettie and Verrall came out onto the turf, walking slowly toward the cliff edge to look at the sky. As they drew near, Leadford heard Nettie’s voice, low and troubled. She was not speaking of him; she was asking Verrall whether he thought the world was truly coming to an end, or if the warships were already fighting in the dark beyond the headland. Verrall murmured something soothing, his arm slipping lightly around her waist. At that familiar gesture, the last shred of Leadford’s patience snapped. All his years of pinched meals, of touching his cap to his betters, of silent, impotent longing, surged into his throat like bile. He rose from the gorse, his shadow stretching long and grotesque across the green-lit grass, and stepped directly into their path with the revolver trembling in his outstretched hand.

Nettie gave a sharp, breathless cry and shrank back against Verrall’s shoulder. For an instant, nobody moved. In the uncanny emerald light, Verrall’s face looked carved from pale stone; he did not cower, though he stiffened, thrusting Nettie behind him with one arm. “Leadford?” he said, his voice tight with astonishment. “What madness is this? Put that thing away.” Leadford tried to speak—to deliver the bitter, ringing indictment he had rehearsed in the train—but his tongue felt thick and dry. “You stole her,” he croaked, and the words sounded pitifully small beneath that vast, silent sky. He raised the barrel, sighting vaguely at Verrall’s white shirtfront. At that precise moment, the air around them changed. It was as though a cool, invisible wave had broken over the cliff, carrying a faint, sweet odour like crushed mint and summer rain. A sudden, dizzying languor seeped into Leadford’s limbs. High above, the green veil of the comet seemed to rush downward, swallowing the stars in a soft, luminous fog. His finger jerked upon the trigger; the pistol cracked sharply, a futile little sound that the thick air swallowed instantly, the bullet burying itself harmlessly in the turf at Verrall’s feet. Leadford tried to raise his arm again, but the revolver had grown impossibly heavy. His knees buckled. Across the grass, he saw Verrall sinking gently to the ground, still holding Nettie’s hand, while she bowed her head as if overcome by a sudden, gentle weariness. Then the green mist closed over Leadford’s eyes, and he fell into a sleep deeper than death.

Leadford woke to a world washed in pure, golden morning. For a long moment he lay still upon the short turf, staring up at a sky of washed and delicate blue, from which every trace of the green vapour—and of the comet itself—had vanished. The air he drew into his lungs was extraordinarily sweet, brisk with salt and thyme, and his mind felt as clear and quiet as a sunlit pool. Beside his outstretched hand lay the cheap Belgian revolver, its blued steel glistening with dew. He looked at it with a faint, detached curiosity, as one might regard a broken toy left behind by an angry child, and pushed it gently aside into the grass. A few paces away, Verrall was sitting up, rubbing his eyes and looking about him in quiet wonder, while Nettie stirred against his shoulder, blinking in the bright sunlight. There was no terror in their faces when they saw him, and in Leadford’s own heart there was neither jealousy nor shame—only a profound, aching pity for the blind, feverish creature he had been yesterday. He rose stiffly to his feet and walked over to them, extending his hand to help Verrall rise. Down in the bay, smoke was rising peacefully from the chimneys of Shaphambury, and men and women were stepping out into the sunlit street, calling to one another in gentle, bewildered voices. Far out upon the shimmering Channel, a grey cruiser lay motionless upon the water, its guns silent, as if the madness of the nations, like the madness in Leadford’s blood, had been swept away forever in the night.
