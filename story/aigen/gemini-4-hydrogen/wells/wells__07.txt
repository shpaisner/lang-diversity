A pall of greasy smoke hung perpetually over Clayton, blotting out the cleaner heavens as if the Potteries were ashamed to look the universe in the face. William Leadford stood in the narrow brick yard behind his mother’s cottage, a crumpled scrap of cheap notepaper crushed in his fist. His knuckles were white, stained at the joints with the indelible ink of the counting-house where he slaved for eighteen shillings a week. Nettie was gone. She had slipped away on the morning omnibus, leaving behind only a few hurried, apologetic lines that smelled faintly of the lavender water young Verrall had bought her at the fair. Verrall—with his tweed knickerbockers, his careless laugh, and his father’s collieries paying for every idle whim.

Leadford looked up above the jagged silhouettes of the bottle-kilns. There, even through the sulphurous haze of twilight, hung the great green star. For weeks the astronomers had chattered of it in the penny papers, measuring its parabolic path and its vast, luminous tail; yet to Leadford it seemed less a heavenly body than a cold, indifferent eye watching his humiliation. Inside the cottage, his mother coughed weakly over the fender, her life ground down by the same mean poverty that had driven Nettie into a richer man’s arms. A cold, concentrated fury settled into Leadford’s breast, hard as a lump of slag. He was tired of being the meek clerk, the beaten dog. If Nettie and her gentleman thought they could hide among the southern cliffs, they had misjudged the reach of a desperate man.

The next morning found him in a third-class carriage rattling southward, a heavy, second-hand revolver knocking against his ribs in his coat pocket. He had bought it from a squinting pawnbroker in Burslem, parting with his last sovereigns in a kind of grim exultation. All around him, the compartment was thick with tobacco smoke and the feverish chatter of men who had forgotten their own small lives in the greater madness of the hour. Every newspaper unfolded upon their knees screamed of ultimatums, of German cruisers steaming through the grey North Sea, of mobilization orders pasted upon the walls of continental towns.

"They’ll be at each other’s throats by Friday," declared a red-faced commercial traveller, tapping the broadsheet with a stubby finger. "And high time, too. A sharp war clears the air."

Leadford said nothing, staring out through the grime-streaked glass at the flying hedgerows of Warwickshire. What was their war to him? Europe was drifting toward a precipice of its own making, driven by the same blind, possessive rage that burned behind his own eyes. As the afternoon wore on and the train plunged through the chalk cuttings toward the coast, the sky took on an unnatural, jaundiced clarity. Even before sunset, the comet was visible—a vast, greenish plume stretching halfway across the zenith, faint and ghostly against the blue, yet swelling perceptibly with every passing hour. The red-faced traveller glanced up at it uneasily and fell silent, as though some uninvited witness had leaned over the edge of the world.

Shaphambury lay bathed in a queer, metallic twilight when Leadford stepped out upon the promenade. Here there were no belching chimneys, no grime-caked pavements—only smooth white terraces, manicured lawns, and the measured, indifferent murmur of the Channel against the shingle. In his frayed black coat and dusty boots, he felt like a blot of soot dropped upon a gentleman’s linen. Holidaymakers strolled past him in white flannels and summer muslins, laughing softly, though every now and then a group would pause beneath a streetlamp to peer upward at the encroaching green glow in the heavens, or gather around a shouting newsboy whose placards announced the first naval skirmishes off the Dogger Bank.

Leadford walked with his hand buried in his pocket, his fingers curled around the cold, ridged grip of the revolver. He had spent two hours combing the seafront boarding-houses before he saw them. They were sitting upon a bench near the edge of the western cliffs, where the tamarisk bushes grew thick and dark against the sea. Verrall leaned back with an arm draped easily along the painted slats, gesturing with a silver-tipped cane, while Nettie sat beside him in a pale summer frock, her face upturned and radiant. Watching her, Leadford felt a spasm of such bitter, scalding hatred that his vision swam. She had never looked at him like that in the smoky alleys of Staffordshire. He waited in the shadow of a bathing-machine until they rose and strolled away along the lonely cliff-path toward the downs.

Out upon the open turf of the downs, the wind blew damp from the sea, carrying the distant, rhythmic thud of the waves below. The comet now dominated the night; it was no longer a mere star with a tail, but a huge, luminous fan of pale emerald light that cast sharp, unfamiliar shadows across the chalk. Far down in the town, a church bell began to toll—not for prayer, but with the frantic, irregular clamour of an alarm, as if the news of war had finally broken the fragile crust of peace.

Leadford stepped out from behind a clump of gorse, blocking the narrow path. Nettie gave a sharp, breathless cry and clutched at Verrall’s sleeve.

"Will!" she gasped, her face turning the colour of chalk in the eerie green radiance. "What are you doing here?"

Verrall thrust her gently behind him, his aristocratic composure stiffening into something cold and contemptuous. "Look here, my man," he began, raising his cane a fraction. "If you’ve come to make a scene—"

"Stand back from her," Leadford said, his voice ragged and thin against the sea wind. He drew the revolver from his pocket. The metal gleamed dull green in the comet’s light. "You’ve taken everything else, Verrall. You and your kind take the bread, the air, and the women, and leave us the cinders. Well, I’ve brought you something back."

Nettie shrieked, throwing herself between them, her hands outstretched. Leadford’s finger tightened on the trigger. His whole cramped, miserable existence had narrowed down to that tiny, violent arc of iron, and he meant to shatter their world as his own had been shattered.

Yet even as the hammer began to lift under his thumb, the wind shifted. A strange, sudden stillness fell upon the downs, so abrupt that the distant clamour of the town bells seemed to be muffled in cotton wool. Leadford hesitated, blinking. A faint, sweetish odour—like crushed almonds and summer rain—crept into his nostrils, sharp and curiously refreshing after the staleness of the railway carriage.

Above them, the emerald fan of the comet seemed to dissolve, spilling downward in a soft, luminous mist that rolled across the cliffs like a silent tide. Verrall lowered his cane, staring upward with parted lips. Nettie’s hands dropped to her sides, her terror melting into a wide-eyed, childlike wonder.

Leadford tried to raise the pistol again, to summon back the hot, righteous fury that had sustained him all the way from Staffordshire, but his arm felt extraordinarily heavy. His anger slipped through his fingers like dry sand. A profound, overwhelming drowsiness washed over his limbs, gentle as warm water. Around them, the grass and the gorse bushes shimmered in the pale green fog, and from the town below came no more shouting—only an immense, unbroken hush. Leadford’s knees buckled. The revolver thudded harmlessly into the turf as he sank down upon his side, watching Verrall and Nettie sway and fold gently to the earth a few paces away. The last thing he saw before his eyes closed was the vast, green veil of the heavens wrapping the whole warring, feverish earth in a single, merciful slumber.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the green mist was gone, and the morning sun was pouring over the edge of the Channel in a flood of crystal gold. He sat up slowly, bracing his palms against the dew-wet turf, and drew a deep breath. The air tasted wonderfully pure, as though the atmosphere itself had been washed clean of a thousand years of smoke and bitterness. He looked at his own ink-stained hands, then at the dark, ugly revolver lying in the grass beside him, and felt a sudden, quiet shame—not the hot shame of humiliation, but the clear-eyed regret of a man waking from a feverish nightmare.

A few yards off, Verrall was stirring, sitting up with a bewildered hand pressed to his brow. Beside him, Nettie rose to her knees, brushing the dry grass from her skirt. When her eyes met Leadford’s, there was no terror in them, only a deep, lucid sorrow and recognition.

"Will," she said softly, and for the first time in months, her voice held no evasion.

Leadford stood up, picked up the revolver, and tossed it far out over the cliff’s edge into the sparkling sea below. He walked toward them—not as an avenger, nor as a beaten clerk, but simply as a fellow creature beneath the immense, brightening sky. Down in Shaphambury, smoke no longer rose from the chimneys, and the frantic bells were silent; across the great breadth of Europe, he somehow knew, soldiers were blinking in the sunlight and laying down their rifles in wonder.

"We have all been very foolish," Leadford said gently, looking from Nettie to Verrall, and then out toward the shining horizon. "Come. Let us walk down into the town and see what kind of world we have woken into."
