For three days I carried that cheap, nickel-plated revolver in the pocket of my threadbare coat, feeling its cold weight knock against my thigh at every stride. Behind me lay the smudged, cinder-strewn valleys of Staffordshire, the belching chimneys of the potteries, and the cramped little kitchen where my mother still bent over her endless washing. I had left them all behind—my clerk’s stool, my paltry eighteen shillings a week, my whole pinched, soot-choked youth—to hunt down the two people who had made a mock of me. Nettie had gone with Verrall. She had slipped away from the grey streets I knew, drawn by his smooth voice, his easy sovereigns, and the gleaming motor-car that had carried her off like a prize from a fair.

All the way south in the jolting third-class carriage, I sat in a corner with my hat pulled low, nursing my hatred like a live coal. The world outside the smeary window seemed as feverish as my own brain. At every junction, newsboys were screaming themselves hoarse beneath the flaring gas-jets. Ultimatum! Mobilization! The Fleet at Sea! Men in greasy caps leaned out to snatch the damp sheets, their faces twitching with a fierce, stupid excitement, as though war were a football match they meant to cheer from the stands. And high above the station roofs, dwarfing the yellow lamps and the drifting engine-smoke, hung the comet. Night after night it had swollen in the northern sky, a monstrous, pale-green smudge with a trailing plume of livid fire. The papers called it a wonder; to me it was merely a cold, indifferent eye watching a world that was rotten to its core.

By the time I reached the coast at Shaphambury, night had fallen, and the green starlight lay upon the slate roofs and the restless sea like a wash of verdigris. I had walked the last four miles from the station, my boots white with chalk dust, my throat dry with bitterness. How clean and insolent this southern town looked after the grime of the Black Country! Along the esplanade, well-dressed folk strolled beneath the electric globes, though even here the shadow of the coming war hung heavy. Groups clustered around the shuttered newspaper shops, speaking in hushed, angry voices, and once, far out across the black water, a dull, jarring thud shook the air—the distant mutter of naval guns.

I cared nothing for their ironclads or their empires. Let Europe burn, I thought, so long as I found Edward Verrall before the world ended. I haunted the seafront for two hours, a gaunt, shabby figure slinking past the bright hotels, my hand clamped around the butt of my pistol. At last I saw them. They were walking together away from the parade, taking the narrow cliff path that led toward the lonely downs. Even in that ghastly green twilight I knew Nettie’s quick, graceful step, and the tall, loose-limbed swagger of the man beside her. He was laughing softly, bending his head to hers, wrapped in the effortless security of his class. A sudden red mist swam before my eyes. I slipped off the gravel path into the dark shadow of the tamarisk bushes and followed them up the slope.

Half a mile beyond the last street-lamp, where the chalk cliff dropped sheer to the whispering shingle below, I stepped out into their path. Nettie gave a sharp, fluttering cry and shrank back against the low stone wall. Verrall halted instantly, thrusting her behind his shoulder, his handsome face hardening as he peered through the greenish gloom.

“Who’s there?” he demanded, his voice sharp with the habit of command. Then he saw my face, and a look of contemptuous recognition crossed his features. “Leadford! Good God, man, what are you doing skulking here?”

I drew the revolver from my pocket and levelled it at his chest. My hand was shaking, not with fear, but with the accumulated poison of twenty years. “You stole her,” I said, my voice cracking in the salt wind. “You’ve everything in the world—money, ease, the whole earth to play in—and you had to come into our smoky hell and take the one thing that was mine.”

“William, don’t!” Nettie sobbed, clutching at Verrall’s sleeve. “Put it down! I chose to come—he didn’t force me!”

“Chose?” I laughed bitterly. “What choice has a girl in Staffordshire against a gentleman with a motor-car? You’ve bought her, Verrall, just as your sort buys our sweat and our lives!”

Verrall’s jaw set. He took a half-step forward, his eyes fixed on the gleaming barrel. “You’re mad, Leadford. Put that toy away before you hang yourself.” Out at sea, the distant guns boomed again, three heavy, sullen strokes against the horizon. Everything—the war, the comet, the cruel, crooked world—seemed to tighten into a single knot inside my skull. I cocked the hammer.

My finger tightened upon the trigger, and in that instant the sky above us seemed to tear apart. There was no thunder, only a vast, rushing whisper, as though an immense wing had swept across the zenith. A blinding flood of emerald light poured down from the heavens, turning the chalk downs, the restless sea, and Verrall’s startled face to the colour of luminous jade. I tried to fire, to shout, to cling to my righteous fury, but a sudden, overwhelming sweetness filled the night air.

It was a strange, penetrating vapour, smelling faintly of crushed thyme and cold rain, and it sank into my lungs like a draught of clear spring water. Instantly, the knot of rage in my brain began to untie. My knees buckled beneath me. Beside the wall, Nettie swayed and sank gently onto the turf without a sound; Verrall stumbled forward, his hand reaching out not to strike me, but as if to steady himself against an invisible tide, and then he too collapsed upon the grass.

I dropped to my knees beside him. The revolver slipped from my numb fingers, striking a flint stone with a dull clack, and lay harmlessly in the dew. Around us, the green mist thickened, swirling softly over the cliffs and the town below, muffling the distant rumble of the sea. I felt no pain, no terror—only an immense, cradle-like weariness, as if a fever that had burned in my blood since childhood were finally breaking. I laid my cheek against the cool grass, watched the emerald sky fade into a deep, merciful dark, and slept.

I woke to a dawn of inconceivable clarity. The sky above the Channel was a pale, spotless blue, washed clean of every rag of cloud, and the comet had shrunk to a faint, harmless ghost low in the west. For a long moment I lay staring up at the light, bewildered by the sensation in my own body. The sourness of my stomach, the dull ache behind my eyes, the tight, envious knot that had lived in my chest for as long as I could remember—all of it had vanished. I felt as though I had been rinsed inside and out by that strange night-wind.

Sitting up, I saw the nickel-plated revolver lying beside my boot. I picked it up, turned it over in my palm, and felt a sudden, sharp disgust—not the melodramatic shame of a sinner, but the simple astonishment of a sane man waking from a lunatic’s dream. Without a second thought, I tossed it over the cliff edge into the sea below.

A few paces away, Nettie and Verrall were stirring. Nettie sat up first, brushing the grass from her hair, her eyes wide and clear. Verrall rose slowly to his knees, looking at me across the turf. There was no arrogance left in his face, no hatred in mine. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and stripped of its old, theatrical pride.

“Leadford,” he said gently, “what a wretched, blind business we were making of it.”

I nodded, looking from him to Nettie, and felt no pang of the old proprietorial madness. “We were out of our minds,” I answered simply. “All of us.”

Together, the three of us walked back down the cliff path toward Shaphambury, saying very little, yet bound by a strange, effortless companionship. As we entered the town, we saw that the same quiet miracle had touched every soul within it. Along the esplanade, men and women stood in little clusters, blinking in the pure morning sun, speaking in low, wondering voices as though they had just arrived in a foreign land. A company of soldiers who had been marching toward the pier the night before had halted by the sea-wall; their rifles leaned neglected against the stonework, and they had unbuckled their heavy belts, staring out over the empty, sparkling water where the ironclads lay silent and forgotten.

The morning newspapers, full of yesterday’s screaming hatreds, blew unread into the gutters. Nobody wanted them now. I looked at the neat, white-painted houses of the town, and my thoughts travelled northward—back to the smudged valleys of Staffordshire, to the slag-heaps, the mean brick rows, and my mother’s tired hands. For the first time in my life, I did not hate those things with a helpless, spiteful fury; I saw them simply as an ugly, childish mess that we—all of us together, rich and poor alike—were going to sweep away and build anew.

Beside me, Nettie slipped her hand lightly through my arm, and with her other she touched Verrall’s sleeve, smiling at us both with a grave, unselfish tenderness. There was no jealousy left in the world, no cramped, possessive hunger—only the wide, sunlit morning, and the quiet certainty that the long night of mankind’s folly was over at last.
