The soot of Staffordshire seemed to have settled into the very marrow of my bones during those last bitter days in Swathinglea. I walked the cinder-blackened lanes with my hands jammed deep into my pockets, feeling the cold, cheap iron of the revolver I had bought in Checkley knocking against my knuckles at every stride. Nettie was gone. She had slipped away from the narrow, smoky respectability of her father’s house to join Verrall—Verrall, with his smooth motor-car, his effortless, condescending laugh, and his pockets lined with the profits of the very mines that kept men like me bent and coughing. In my small, cramped lodging, the walls had seemed to press inward until I could scarcely breathe for the humiliation of it. Everywhere I looked, the world was ugly and unjust. Even the newspapers, flapping on the hoardings outside the stationers, screamed of a grander, darker madness: ultimatums flashing across the Channel, reserves called to the colours, ironclads steaming through the North Sea in the gathering gloom. Europe was sliding toward the abyss of war, and men gathered in knots beneath the gas-lamps to argue with flushed, angry faces. Yet I cared nothing for kings or chancelleries. My own private war burned hotter than any continental quarrel. Overhead, through the drifting yellow smoke of the blast furnaces, the great comet hung like a malignant, pale-green eye, swelling night by night until it outshone the gaslight, casting a faint, spectral tint over the slag-heaps and the mean brick rows. I looked up at it once, spat into the gutter, and turned my face toward the railway station.

The third-class carriage was stifling, thick with the smell of damp wool, cheap shag tobacco, and fear. Across from me, a clerk in a frayed collar rattled his evening paper, his voice rising shrill above the clatter of the wheels as he read aloud the latest dispatches from Berlin and Paris. Two miners in their Sunday black nodded grimly, muttering of mobilisation and the price of bread, their eyes reflecting a helpless, bewildered dread. I pressed my forehead against the smudged glass and stared out into the rushing twilight. We were leaving the black scarred valleys behind, speeding through the quiet English shires toward the coast, where a crumpled, tear-blotted letter I had found in Nettie’s drawer told me Verrall had taken her. The countryside looked strange and unfamiliar beneath the swelling radiance of the comet. It was no longer a mere distant smear in the heavens; its vast, feathery tail now spanned a third of the sky, washing the hedgerows, the silent elms, and the winding rivers in a cold, luminous emerald haze. The stars had vanished, drowned in that eerie, phosphorescent glare. My fellow passengers grew quieter as the night deepened, craning their necks to peer out at the portentous sky, whispering of the end of the world or the wrath of God. I let them whisper. My hand never left the pocket of my coat. In my mind, I rehearsed the meeting a hundred times—the look of terror in Verrall’s arrogant eyes, the sharp crack of the powder, the sudden, terrible justice that would wipe away my long years of poverty and contempt.

By the time I stepped out onto the gravel promenade of Shaphambury, the town had the hushed, breathless air of a place waiting for a storm. Great white hotels towered above the cliffs, their balconies lit with warm, amber electric globes that looked strangely brassy and feeble against the vast green canopy of the sky. A few nervous holidaymakers hurried indoors, glancing upward with anxious faces, while down on the dark sea, the searchlights of a cruiser squadron swept nervously across the horizon, probing the night for an unseen enemy. I walked slowly along the cliff path, my boots dusty from the road, my cheap serge suit shabby and threadbare beside the evening dress of the gentlemen strolling past the hotel gardens. Then, near the edge of the grassy headland where the tamarisk bushes grew twisted by the salt wind, I saw them. Nettie stood by the white railings, a pale shawl wrapped around her shoulders, looking out toward the illuminated water. Beside her was Verrall, tall and immaculate in his dark coat, speaking to her in a low, confident murmur. Even in that unearthly light, I could see the familiar curve of her cheek, the slight, helpless droop of her head that had once belonged to me. A hot, suffocating wave of rage surged into my throat, blinding me to the vastness of the sea and the portentous sky alike. I stepped out from the shadow of the bushes, my boots crunching loudly on the gravel, and blocked their path back toward the lights of the town.

Nettie gave a sharp, startled cry and shrank back against the railings, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall stiffened, stepping instinctively between us, his face hardening into the cold, disdainful mask of a master confronting an insolent servant. "Leadford," he said, his voice tight and irritated. "What madness brings you here? Stand aside." I did not answer him with words. My fingers closed around the butt of the revolver, and I dragged it free from my pocket, levelling the trembling barrel at his chest. Nettie screamed my name, pleading, her eyes wide with terror in the strange emerald glow. Yet even as my finger tightened on the trigger, a sudden, inexplicable stillness fell over the cliff. The distant rumble of the surf seemed to fade into a muffled whisper, and the searchlights out at sea flickered and dimmed. Looking up for an instant, I saw that the comet had vanished behind a vast, billowing curtain of luminous green vapour that was rolling silently down from the zenith, swallowing the stars and the horizon alike. A strange, sweetish scent, like crushed almond blossoms and ozone, filled the night air. My knees buckled beneath me. I pulled the trigger, and the shot rang out flat and feeble, swallowed instantly by the thickening mist, tearing harmlessly into the turf. Verrall staggered back, clutching at his throat, not from my bullet, but from the heavy, intoxicating sweetness that was pouring into our lungs. The cliff tilted beneath my feet; the lights of the hotels blurred into soft green halos, and with a quiet sigh, I sank down into the cool grass and knew no more.

When I opened my eyes, the night was gone, and a morning of unimaginable clarity lay over the world. The heavy green mist had vanished, leaving the air so pure and transparent that every distant headland, every white sail upon the channel, stood out with the sharpness of a jewel. I sat up slowly on the dewy turf, expecting the familiar, gnawing ache of bitterness in my breast—the old, cramped misery that had been my companion since boyhood. Instead, I felt an astonishing lightness, as though a fever that had raged in my blood for twenty years had broken at last, leaving me cool, clear-sighted, and profoundly at peace. Beside me in the grass lay the cheap revolver. I looked at it with a sudden, quiet wonder, unable to fathom the dark, frantic stranger who had carried such a thing across England to shed human blood. I picked it up by the barrel and tossed it far over the cliff edge, watching it disappear into the sparkling blue water below. A few paces away, Verrall was rising unsteadily to his feet, brushing the damp earth from his coat, while Nettie sat leaning against the white railings, gazing out at the sea with eyes that held no terror, only a deep, bewildered awe. We had all breathed the same strange air of the comet’s passing; the old, thick veil of selfishness and blind passion had been burned away from our minds, leaving us naked and lucid in the morning sun.

Verrall looked at me across the narrow strip of grass, and for the first time in our lives, there was neither arrogance in his eyes nor hatred in mine. He walked toward me slowly, extending his hand—not with the patronising ease of a wealthy man, but with the simple, hesitant grace of a brother waking from a shared nightmare. I took it, and then we both turned to Nettie. She stood up, her face pale and washed of all its old, timid evasions, and looked from one to the other of us with a quiet, dignified sorrow for the pain we had caused one another in the dark days now ended. Below us, the town of Shaphambury was stirring. Yet there were no shouting newsboys running along the promenade, no frantic crowds gathering to cheer for slaughter. From the harbour came no thunder of departing ironclads; instead, the church bells began to ring, slow and perplexed, across the sunlit roofs, as men and women stepped out of their doors to stare at one another and wonder how they could ever have dreamed of war. We three stood together on the cliffside, knowing that the old life—the grime of Staffordshire, the cruel gulf of wealth and poverty, the jealousies that had driven us to the brink of murder—had crumbled like a bad dream at dawn. Whatever awaited us in the vast, bright world stretching out beneath that serene sky, we would meet it with open eyes and clean hands.
