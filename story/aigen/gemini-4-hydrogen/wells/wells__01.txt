In those last feverish weeks of the old order, the sky above our Staffordshire pot-banks was never truly dark. Between the belching orange glare of the blast-furnaces and the creeping, sulphur-tainted fog that clung to the slag-heaps of Clayton, there hung a new and spectral light. Night after night, the green comet swelled in the northeast, a pale, feathery smear against the stars, watched by anxious astronomers and ignored by the blind, quarrelling multitudes below. I paid it little heed myself. My world had narrowed to the four damp walls of my lodging and the consuming, venomous ache of my own humiliation. Nettie had gone. The girl who had walked with me beside the blackened canal, who had promised me her life among the soot and the red-brick squalor, had slipped away in the night with young Verrall—Verrall, with his easy laugh, his Norfolk jackets, and his father’s money. A crumpled note on my deal table was all that remained of five years of patient, hungry devotion. I sat in the twilight, my fists clenched upon my knees, listening to the distant newsboys bawling through the High Street of mobilization, of German cruisers, of the great European war that was already rattling its sabres at the door. The whole world, it seemed, was spoiling for a fight, and in my narrow, embittered breast, I felt a kindred madness. If empires could bleed for their pride, why should I sit meekly in the ashes while another man stole the one thing I had ever truly coveted?

By Tuesday morning my resolution had hardened into a cold, methodical fury. I drew the last of my meagre savings from the post-office—money I had hoarded, penny by penny, for a cottage with a strip of garden—and walked down into the smoke-stained bowels of the town. In a pawnbroker’s window near the station, wedged between a tarnished cornet and a tray of wedding rings, lay a small, nickel-plated revolver. It cost me thirty shillings, and the box of cartridges another three. The pawnbroker, a wheezing man with spectacles pushed high on his forehead, scarcely looked at me as he wrapped it in brown paper; his eyes were glued to a damp newspaper spread across the counter, its headlines screaming of ultimatums and troop trains. With the heavy iron tucked into my coat pocket, I boarded the afternoon express for the south. All through the long, rattling journey across the Midlands, the third-class carriage was thick with tobacco smoke and the boastful, frightened chatter of clerks and artisans. They spoke of nothing but the coming slaughter, of how we should teach the Kaiser a lesson before Christmas, their faces flushed with a cheap, vicarious bloodlust. I sat in the corner with my cap pulled over my eyes, saying nothing, my fingers curled around the cold grip in my pocket. Through the soot-streaked window, as dusk fell over the quiet green shires, the comet appeared again, larger now, casting a faint, uncanny luminescence over the hedgerows and the rushing telegraph wires. I watched it with a dull, indifferent hatred, thinking only of Shaphambury, and of the two people waiting there in the gilded security of their stolen paradise.

Shaphambury lay beneath the immense, brooding vault of the night sky like a painted toy-box of wealth and leisure. I had never seen the sea before, and the sudden immensity of it—that dark, heaving plain stretching away to the horizon—struck me for a moment with a sense of my own smallness. Yet the town itself, with its white-stuccoed terraces, its electric lamps, and the distant strains of a string band drifting from the pier, only inflamed my bitterness. Here were the people for whom we toiled in the Staffordshire grime; here they strolled in their evening dress, sipping iced drinks and murmuring of the war as though it were a regatta arranged for their amusement. I spent the evening wandering the esplanade, a dusty, hollow-eyed figure in a frayed serge suit, out of place among the rustling silks and white waistcoats. Around ten o’clock, I found them. They were sitting on the terrace of the Grand Hotel, bathed in the amber glow of the French windows. Verrall was leaning back in a wicker chair, his handsome face upturned as he gestured with a cigar toward the heavens. Nettie sat beside him, wrapped in a pale shawl I had never seen, her profile turned toward the sea. Above them, the comet now spanned half the sky—a vast, luminous plume of greenish-white fire that seemed to pale the stars and turn the chalk cliffs along the bay to the colour of dead bone. My hand tightened on the pistol. I had no plan beyond the simple, brutal geometry of revenge: to wait until they left the lights, to step out of the shadow, and to end this intolerable comedy once and for all.

It was past midnight when they finally left the hotel, strolling out along the high cliff-walk toward the headland where the town lights thinned into darkness. I followed at a distance, my boots muffled by the dry summer turf, my heart hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird. Down in the town behind us, a solitary newsboy was still crying a late special—some fresh madness from the Continent, the word of war echoing faintly up the chalk slopes like the cry of a gull. Ahead of me, Nettie and Verrall paused by the white wooden railing that guarded the sheer drop to the shingle below. The sea whispered far beneath, silvered by that monstrous, silent star overhead. I stepped out from the black shadow of a tamarisk thicket, ten paces behind them, and drew the revolver. "Nettie," I said. My voice sounded thin and cracked in the vast night air. She spun round with a sharp little gasp, her hands flying to her throat. Verrall moved instantly, stepping half in front of her with the instinctive, clumsy gallantry of his class, peering at me through the greenish gloom. "Leadford?" he said, his voice tight with astonishment. "Good God, man, what are you doing here? Put that thing down!" I raised the barrel, levelling it straight at the white triangle of his shirt-front. "You've had your holiday, Verrall," I heard myself pant, though my throat felt parched as lime. "You've taken everything I had. Now you'll pay for it." Nettie cried out my name, a sound of pure, helpless terror that only drove the iron deeper into my soul. My thumb drew back the hammer with a sharp, metallic click that seemed to echo across the sleeping bay.

And then, in the very heartbeat before my finger could tighten upon the trigger, the world held its breath. A strange, profound stillness fell over the headland. The whisper of the surf below seemed to die away, and the distant clamour of the town vanished as if a heavy curtain had been drawn across the earth. I blinked, my arm wavering slightly. A peculiar odour was rising from the sea—not the sharp tang of salt and kelp, but a sweet, faint, penetrating fragrance, like bruised laurel leaves mixed with the clean freshness of rain after thunder. Looking past Verrall’s rigid shoulder, I saw that the horizon was no longer dark. A vast, luminous mist of pale emerald was rolling inward across the waters, swallowing the headland, rising up the chalk cliffs like a silent tide. Before I could grasp what was happening, the green vapour reached us. It washed over my face, cool and strangely intoxicating. A sudden, overwhelming vertigo seized me; the revolver felt as heavy as a lump of pig-iron in my shaking hand. I saw Nettie sway, her knees buckling gently beneath her as she sank down against the wooden rail. Verrall threw out an arm to catch her, staggered, and looked at me with wide, bewildered eyes that held no anger now, only a sleepy, childlike wonder. I tried to shout, to fire, to cling to my precious, righteous rage, but the green air filled my lungs like a draught of cool water. My eyelids grew impossibly heavy. The pistol slipped from my nerveless fingers into the grass, and without a sound, I crumpled onto the cool turf beside them, plunging swiftly into a deep and dreamless darkness.

I woke to a morning of such crystalline, unbelievable clarity that for a long moment I lay staring up at the sky, unable to remember my own name. The green mist was gone, and the comet had vanished into the daylight, leaving behind a heaven of deep, washed sapphire. A lark was singing somewhere high above the cliffs, its notes falling like drops of clear water. I sat up slowly, rubbing my eyes, and saw the nickel-plated revolver lying in the dew-drenched grass a yard from my hand. I stared at it with a sudden, sharp pang of astonishment—not horror, precisely, but the bewildered pity one feels upon discovering a cruel toy from one’s forgotten childhood. Why on earth had I carried this ugly piece of iron across England? What fever had possessed me? Looking back at the man I had been twelve hours ago, I felt as though I were peering into the cramped, smoky cage of some suffering beast. A few paces away, Verrall was sitting up, blinking in the bright sunshine, while Nettie stirred beside him, pushing back her tumbled hair. Our eyes met—mine and Verrall’s—and for the first time in our lives, there was neither envy in my gaze nor condescension in his. We looked at one another simply as two men waking from the same dark, suffocating nightmare. When Nettie rose to her feet and looked from him to me, her face pale and solemn in the morning light, I felt no sting of possession, no desire to claim or to punish—only a vast, aching tenderness for her, for Verrall, and for all the bruised, bewildered millions waking across the world. Far below, the little town of Shaphambury lay bathed in golden sun, utterly silent, the war-cries of yesterday forgotten like the babble of fevered sleepers. A new day had begun, and for the first time, we were truly awake to see it.
