The pub smelled of wet wool and coal smoke. William Leadford sat at the scarred oak table with a pint of bitter before him. Outside, the Potteries were dark under the low sky, and the blast furnaces threw an orange glare against the clouds.

"They're mobilizing in Berlin," the man at the bar said.

"Let them," the bartender said. He wiped a glass with a gray rag. "It's nothing to Staffordshire."

"It's everything to the ironworks," the man said. "You'll see."

William did not listen to them. He drank his beer. It was warm and flat, and it tasted of copper. In his coat pocket his hand rested on the cold iron of the revolver. He had bought it from a pawnshop in Hanley for twelve shillings. Twelve shillings was two weeks' bread, if a man lived carefully, and he had not lived carefully since Nettie went away.

He finished the pint and went out into the street. The cobbles were slick with black mud. Above the chimneys of the kilns, the sky had cleared to the east, and the comet hung there, pale and green like gaslight through a dirty pane. People stood on the corners looking up at it. Nobody spoke much. They were tired from the pits and the clay works, and the green star looked cold.

William walked past the row of brick cottages where his mother still coughed in the front room. He did not go in. He turned down the lane toward the station, his boots scraping the grit. Verrall had a motorcar and clean hands and a house by the sea. Nettie had gone with him because he was clean and had money. William felt the weight of the gun against his hip as he walked.

The train was crowded with reservists going south. They had their kit bags in the racks and they passed a bottle of gin down the carriage, laughing too loudly. William sat by the window with his cap pulled low over his eyes. He watched the black country give way to flat fields and hedges, and then to the dark woods of the south.

At a junction, they waited an hour on a siding while a troop train thundered past toward the coast. The flatcars carried field guns covered in gray canvas.

"Look at the sky," a young soldier said, leaning over William's shoulder.

The comet was larger now. It was no longer a star with a tail, but a great green fan spread across the dark. It threw a faint, green light on the grass beside the tracks and on the faces of the men in the carriage.

"It's a sign," an older man said from the corner. "Like in the Bible."

"It's gas," the young soldier said. "I read it in the paper. Poison gas from space."

"Rubbish," the older man said. "Pass the gin."

William closed his eyes. He thought of Nettie's hair when they walked on the moor above Clayton. It had smelled of woodsmoke then. Now she was in a hotel with white sheets and silver on the table, and Verrall was beside her. He touched the pocket of his coat. The revolver was hard and real. When the train finally jolted forward, William did not open his eyes. He slept in short, bad bursts, waking whenever the whistle blew, seeing the green light through the glass each time.

In the morning he reached the sea. The town was white and clean, built on chalk cliffs above a gray bay. There was no coal dust here. The wind smelled of salt and wet kelp, and it blew hard off the water.

William walked along the esplanade. His boots were worn down at the heels and gray with dried mud from Staffordshire. A few people in good coats were walking their dogs, looking out toward the horizon where three warships lay at anchor, dark and low in the swell. Smoke came from their funnels, blowing flat across the water.

He found the hotel at the end of the cliffs. It was brick and ivy, with a wide gravel drive. He sat on a stone bench across the road, behind a low hedge of tamarisk, and waited. His stomach was empty. He had three pennies left in his trousers pocket, and he did not want to spend them on bread.

At noon, Verrall and Nettie came out. Verrall wore a tweed suit and a soft hat. He walked with his hands in his pockets, looking at the sea. Nettie wore a blue dress and a light coat. She looked taller than she had in Staffordshire, and her face was brown from the sun.

William watched them walk down the path toward the shingle beach. He stood up and followed them, keeping fifty yards behind. The wind was cold against his face. He felt no anger now, only a tight, dry feeling in his throat, like he had swallowed ash. They stopped by a breakwater where the waves broke white against the timber. William stepped down onto the stones.

"Nettie," William said.

She turned quickly. Her hand went to her throat. Verrall stepped half a pace in front of her, his face hardening. He was younger than William had remembered, with clear eyes and a clean jaw.

"What do you want, Leadford?" Verrall said.

"I want to talk to Nettie," William said.

"She doesn't want to talk to you. Go back to the station."

"I didn't come all this way to go back," William said. He pulled the revolver from his pocket. His hand was steady, though his fingers were stiff from the sea wind.

Nettie made a small sound, not loud, just a catch in her breath. Verrall did not move back. He looked at the gun and then at William's face.

"Put that away," Verrall said. "You'll only hang for it."

"I don't care about hanging," William said. "Look at the sky. Look at the ships out there. Everybody's going to die anyway."

Above them, even in the noon light, the green glare of the comet was visible behind the thin clouds. The air had gone very still. The wind off the sea had dropped all at once, and the waves seemed to slap the shingle more slowly, as if the water had grown heavy.

"Will," Nettie said. "Please. I couldn't stay there. It was killing me."

"It's killing me too," William said.

He raised the barrel toward Verrall's chest. Then he smelled it. It was not salt, and it was not coal smoke. It was sweet and sharp, like crushed mint and ozone. A green mist was rolling in from the sea, low over the water, swallowing the warships first and then the breakwater.

Verrall coughed once. He reached out to steady himself against the wooden breakwater, his knees bending.

"What is that?" Nettie said. Her voice sounded far away, though she was only six feet from him.

William tried to pull the trigger, but his finger would not press the iron. His arm felt as though it were made of wet clay. The revolver slipped from his hand and fell onto the shingle with a dull clatter of stones.

The green fog covered them. It was thick and cool against his skin. Looking down, William saw his own boots disappear in it, then his knees. Nettie sat down slowly on the stones, leaning her back against the timber. She looked up at him without fear now, her eyes heavy.

"I'm tired," she said.

"Yes," William said.

He tried to step toward her, to see if Verrall was dead or only sleeping, but his legs gave out cleanly. He dropped onto the pebbles. They were round and smooth under his cheek, and not uncomfortable. The sound of the sea faded until he could only hear his own breathing, slow and deep, pulling the sweet green air into his lungs.

From the town above the cliffs, a church bell began to toll, then stopped mid-stroke. A gull cried once overhead and was quiet. William thought of the furnaces in Staffordshire, burning red in the dark, and wondered if they had gone out. Then he did not think of anything at all. The green light closed over his eyes like water.

When he woke, the sun was high and very bright. The green fog was gone. The sky was a clean, pale blue, washed clear of smoke and cloud, and the air tasted like spring water.

William sat up. His back was stiff from the stones, and his mouth was dry, but his head was clear. It was clearer than it had ever been. He looked at the revolver lying beside his knee. The blue steel looked foolish and small against the gray shingle, like a toy a child had dropped. He picked it up, walked three paces to the water's edge, and threw it as far as he could into the sea. It made a small splash and sank.

He turned back. Verrall was sitting up against the breakwater, rubbing his face with both hands. Nettie was kneeling beside him, her blue dress wrinkled and stained with salt. They both looked at William.

"Are you all right?" Verrall asked. His voice was quiet, without the edge it had before.

"Yes," William said. "I'm all right."

Nettie stood up and brushed the sand from her skirt. She looked at William for a long time. There was no fear in her face, and William felt no pain in his chest when he looked at her. The tight, dry knot inside him had untied itself while he slept.

"What happened?" she asked.

"The comet," William said. He looked out at the bay. The three warships were still there, anchored in the bright water, but no smoke came from their funnels. Nothing moved on their decks. "The gas came down."

"We should go up to the town," Verrall said, getting to his feet. "People will need help."

"Yes," William said.

They walked together up the shingle path toward the white cliffs, the three of them side by side in the clean morning light.
