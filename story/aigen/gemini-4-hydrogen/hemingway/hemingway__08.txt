The coal dust settled on everything in Clayton. It was on the sills and in the creases of William Leadford’s knuckles, and yellow soap never got it all out. In the evenings, the furnaces lit the low clouds red from below, and above the clouds, high in the clean dark, the comet hung like a green blister.

Leadford sat at the kitchen table beside his mother’s cold tea. Nettie’s letter lay flat on the oilcloth. She had written it in a hurry, the ink smudged where her wrist had dragged. She was gone with Verrall. Verrall had a motorcar and a house by the river, and his father owned half the pits in the valley.

Leadford folded the letter and put it in his pocket. He took his cap from the peg and walked down the lane toward the pawnshop behind the station. Men were coming off the afternoon shift, their faces black except for their eyes and the wet pink of their mouths. Two of them were talking about the Kaiser and the fleet at Kiel.

"They'll fight before the harvest," one said.

"Let them," the other said. "It's no worse than the pits."

Leadford did not stop. At the pawnshop, the old man behind the grille looked at the shillings Leadford laid on the counter, then slid the small revolver across the wood. It was a Belgian make, dull nickeled, with five cartridges in a greasy paper packet. Leadford loaded it in the alley, dropped it into his coat pocket, and looked up at the green star.

The train south was crowded with soldiers and commercial travelers. Leadford sat by the window with his knees pressed against a sample case, watching the flat Midland fields slide past under a pale sky. Everywhere the grass looked dry and gray.

At a junction town they waited two hours. Leadford went into the station buffet and ordered a pint of bitter and a pork pie. Two men in tweed coats stood at the bar with a newspaper spread between them.

"The ultimatum expires at midnight," the taller one said. "My brother-in-law is in the reserves. He got his papers Tuesday."

"It's the comet," the other man said, tapping his pipe on his boot. "Old people say it brings blood. You can smell the sulfur already."

"There's no sulfur," the tall man said. "It's just a light in the sky."

Leadford ate the pie. The crust was lard-heavy and cold. He drank the bitter in three swallows and went back to the platform. His right hand stayed in his pocket, his thumb resting on the checkered grip of the revolver. The metal had warmed to his skin.

When the coast train came, the sun was down behind the chimneys, and the green light in the east was bright enough to cast a shadow on the gravel. Leadford sat in the corner of the third-class carriage and did not sleep. He thought of Nettie’s hair when she took the pins out, and of Verrall’s white hands on the wheel of the motorcar. The train clattered over the switches toward the sea.

Shaphambury smelled of salt and wet shingle and the tar on the fishing boats. It was a clean town, with whitewashed boarding houses facing the esplanade and red geraniums along the seawall. Leadford walked from the station with his collar turned up against the wind. He had fourpence left and the revolver.

It did not take him long to find the motorcar. It stood outside a timbered bungalow at the edge of the cliffs, past the last of the bathing machines. The brass lamps were polished and the leather seats were covered with canvas against the damp.

Leadford sat on a bench fifty yards away, looking out at the North Sea. The water was gray and flat, and far out on the horizon, three smudges of black smoke hung low. Those were the cruisers, patrolling before the declaration came.

At dusk, the bungalow door opened. Verrall came out in a white flannel shirt and gray trousers, carrying a wicker basket. Nettie followed with a rug over her arm. She laughed at something he said, and the sound carried across the turf. She looked different here. Her face was brown from the sun, and she walked without the quick, nervous step she had in Clayton.

They went down the path toward the cove. Leadford stood up. He waited until they were below the bank, then walked across the grass. The comet was huge now, hanging low over the dark water, filling the sky with a cold brightness that made the white stones shine like bone.

They had spread the rug on the shingle above the tide line. Verrall was trying to light a spirit lamp behind a pile of driftwood, shielding the flame with his cupped hands. Nettie sat with her knees drawn up, watching the sea.

Leadford came down the path noiselessly in his worn boots. When his shadow fell across the rug, Nettie looked up. She did not scream. She only drew in her breath and went very still.

"Willie," she said.

Verrall turned his head, the match burning between his fingers until it scorched him and he dropped it into the sand. He stood up slowly, brushing the grit from his knees.

"What do you want, Leadford?" he asked. His voice was steady, the voice of a man used to giving orders at the pithead.

"I came for her," Leadford said. He took his hand out of his pocket. In the green light, the nickeled barrel looked like wet ice.

"Don't be a fool," Verrall said. "Put that away."

"Willie, please," Nettie said. She stood up, her hands held out a little from her sides. "It was my doing. I wrote to you."

"You belonged to me," Leadford said. His chest felt tight, as though a strap were buckled around his ribs. He raised the pistol and aimed at Verrall’s white shirt.

Just then the wind died. It stopped all at once, leaving the cove so quiet they could hear the hiss of the spirit lamp. A sweet smell came down from the sky, thick as warm milk and sharp as ammonia. Leadford tried to pull the trigger, but his finger felt heavy and distant. The green light brightened until the cliffs and the sea and Verrall’s face were all the same color. Leadford’s knees gave way, and he fell forward onto the shingle as the world went out.

When Leadford woke, the sun was up and the sky was a deep, clear blue he had never seen in Staffordshire. He lay on his side on the shingle. His mouth tasted of salt and fresh water, and his head was light and empty, as though he had slept for a week after a fever.

He sat up. The spirit lamp lay overturned on the sand beside the wicker basket. A few yards away, Verrall sat on a flat rock with his elbows on his knees, staring out at the water. Nettie lay curled on the rug, still asleep, her cheek on her hand.

Leadford looked down. The Belgian revolver lay in the pebbles beside his boot. He picked it up. It felt foolish in his hand, a heavy piece of iron made for nothing good. He broke the cylinder open, shook the five cartridges into his palm, and threw the gun out into the surf. It made a small splash and disappeared. Then he threw the cartridges after it, one by one.

Verrall turned his head and watched him. There was no anger in Verrall’s face, only a quiet, puzzled look, like a man trying to remember a dream.

"What happened?" Verrall asked.

"The comet," Leadford said. "The gas."

He stood up and brushed the sand from his coat. His old rage was gone. He looked for the hot, tight knot that had carried him from Clayton, but there was nothing there. Only the morning air, smelling of kelp and clean grass, and the tide coming in slow and even over the stones.

Nettie woke then and sat up, pushing the hair back from her forehead. She looked at Leadford, and then at Verrall, and she did not seem afraid of either of them.

"I am hungry," she said.

"There is bread and cold meat in the basket," Verrall said. He brought it over, handed a piece of bread to Nettie, and held the basket out to Leadford.

Leadford took a slice of bread and some cheese. They sat together on the dry shingle above the tide line and ate. Nobody spoke for a long time. Out on the horizon, where the three cruisers had been the night before, the sea was empty. The black smoke was gone.

"They won't fight now," Leadford said, looking at the water.

"No," Verrall said. "I don't suppose they will. It wouldn't make any sense."

Nettie looked at Leadford’s hands, at the coal dust still faintly graying the creases of his knuckles. She reached out and touched the back of his wrist with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a dog to see if it is tame.

"Are you going back to Clayton, Willie?" she asked.

"Yes," Leadford said. "Tomorrow, maybe. There's work to do there. The town's built all wrong."

"It's a terrible place," Verrall said softly. "I never saw how terrible it was until just now."

"We'll tear the worst of it down," Leadford said. He finished his bread and wiped the crumbs from his trousers. The sun was warm on his shoulders, and the sea was bright and flat all the way to the edge of the world, and for the first time in his life, he had no hurry to be anywhere else.
