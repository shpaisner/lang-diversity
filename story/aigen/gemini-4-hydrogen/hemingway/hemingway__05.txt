The revolver cost twelve shillings at the pawnshop in Hanley, and the man behind the counter wrapped it in an old newspaper. William Leadford put it in his coat pocket and walked back up the hill toward Clayton. The coal smoke lay low over the brick kilns. It tasted of sulfur in the back of his throat.

In the kitchen, his mother was boiling cabbage. She did not look up when he came in.

"You've been out," she said.

"Yes," William said.

He went upstairs to his small room under the slate roof and took the revolver out of the newspaper. The paper had a headline about the German fleet in the North Sea and the ultimatum. He laid the paper on the washstand and looked at the gun. It was cheap nickel-plate, and the cylinder had a little play in it, but the hammer snapped clean when he pulled it back.

He thought of Nettie. He thought of her in the parlor at Checkley, and then he thought of her in the motorcar with Verrall, her hair pinned up under a veil, laughing while the dust blew over the hedge. Verrall had a house near the coast and a father who owned half the ironworks. William had three shillings left and black dust under his fingernails that never washed out.

Outside the window, the sky was clearing after the rain. Above the dark chimneys, the comet hung bright and green, larger than it had been the night before. The tail spread across the pale sky like spilled milk. Everyone in the potbanks talked about it, and some of the women were afraid, but William only looked at it and then put the six cartridges into the cylinder and snapped it shut.

He took the morning train south. The third-class carriage was full of men going down to the coast and two young clerks from Birmingham who had a bottle of whisky. One of the clerks read aloud from the morning paper about the mobilization and the ships steaming out of Portsmouth.

"We'll sink them before Sunday," the clerk said.

"You'll sink nothing," an old man in the corner said. "You'll sit in a ditch and eat wet bread."

William sat by the window with his hands in his pockets. The revolver was heavy against his thigh. He watched the hedges and the small brick towns go by, and then the green fields of the south. He had not slept on the wooden bench. His eyes burned, and he felt a tight band around his chest that had been there since the day Nettie stopped answering his letters.

At a junction, a troop train passed them going the other way. The carriages were packed with boys in khaki leaning out the windows, shouting and waving their caps. They looked very young and loud. The train smelled of stale tobacco and sweat.

When night came, the lamps in the carriage were dim, and the glass turned black except for the reflection of the men's faces. Through the top of the window, William could see the comet again. The green light seemed stronger now, almost like the flare of a copper furnace. The clerk with the whisky had fallen asleep with his mouth open. William felt the cold iron in his pocket and watched the dark trees rush past until the train slowed for the sea.

Shaphambury was a quiet town of white boarding houses and pebble beaches, except for the searchlights. Out on the dark water, two cruisers lay anchored, and their long white beams swept the black sea and sometimes touched the chalk cliffs.

William walked along the cliff road. The wind came off the Channel cold and salt, and there was no coal smoke in it. He asked a boy near the pier where the new bungalows were, and the boy pointed east along the downs.

He found the place after midnight. It was a small wooden house with a veranda, set back from the cliff path behind a hedge of tamarisk. There was a light in one window behind a yellow blind. William climbed over a low flint wall and sat down in the dry grass and gorse. The ground was cold.

He took out the revolver and held it in his right hand. His fingers were stiff from the wind. He could hear the sea breaking steadily on the shingle below the cliff, a long hiss and then the rattle of stones pulling back. Once, far out to sea, there was the dull thump of a gun, like a door shutting in a cellar. Then it was quiet again.

The light behind the yellow blind went out. William did not move. He sat with his back against the flint wall and watched the dark shape of the bungalow. He was not hungry, though he had not eaten since noon, and he did not feel cold anymore. He only waited for the morning so he could see Verrall's face when he stepped out onto the grass. Above the sea, the green star filled half the sky, and the grass around William's boots looked pale and strange in its light.

At first light, the mist came in from the water. It was not white like sea fog. It had a faint green color, and it smelled sharp and clean, like crushed mint or ozone after lightning.

The door of the bungalow opened, and Verrall came out in a white flannel shirt and gray trousers. He carried a towel over his shoulder, going down to bathe. Nettie followed him onto the veranda. She wore a dark blue coat over her dress, and her hair was loose down her back.

William stood up from the gorse. His legs were stiff. He walked toward them across the wet turf, holding the revolver down by his leg.

"Nettie," he called.

She stopped and put her hand on the wooden rail. Verrall turned quickly. When he saw William, his face went tight, and he stepped in front of her.

"Put that away, Leadford," Verrall said. His voice was steady, though he was pale.

William raised the revolver. He did not aim carefully. He wanted to shoot Verrall in the chest and then shoot himself, or shoot them both. He pulled the trigger. The crack was flat and small in the open air, and the bullet went wide into the wooden wall behind them. Nettie gave a short cry.

Before William could cock the hammer again, the green mist rolled over the edge of the cliff. It was thick and sweet. It filled his lungs all at once, and a sudden heaviness took him in the knees. He saw Verrall stagger and sit down hard on the veranda steps. Nettie sank beside him, leaning her head against the post. William tried to lift his arm, but the revolver was too heavy, and he fell forward into the wet grass.

When William woke, the sun was high and warm on his neck. The green mist was gone. The sky above him was a deep, clear blue, cleaner than any sky he had ever seen in Staffordshire.

He sat up and rubbed his eyes. His head did not ache. The tight band around his chest was gone, and for the first time in months he could draw a full breath without feeling the old sour weight in his stomach.

The revolver lay in the grass beside his knee. The nickel plating had dulled in the dew. He picked it up, broke open the cylinder, and shook the remaining five cartridges into his palm. He stood up and walked to the edge of the cliff. Far below, the tide was out, and the wet shingle shone in the sun. Out on the water, the two gray cruisers still lay at anchor, but no smoke came from their funnels, and their boats hung untouched in the davits. There were no guns firing anywhere.

William drew back his arm and threw the revolver and the cartridges out over the cliff. He watched them fall against the bright air until they disappeared into the sea.

He turned back toward the bungalow. His clothes were wrinkled and stained with grass and coal dust, and his boots were scuffed, but he did not feel ashamed of them. He felt very light, as if he had been sick with a fever for a long time and the fever had broken in the night.

Verrall was sitting on the steps of the veranda, looking out at the sea with his elbows on his knees. Nettie sat beside him. Neither of them was speaking.

William walked across the turf and stopped at the bottom of the steps. Verrall looked up at him, and Nettie looked at him too. There was no fear in her face now. She looked tired, as if she had slept deeply and only just come back to the world.

"Are you hurt?" William asked.

"No," Verrall said. "I don't think anyone is hurt. We all slept."

"I threw the gun in the sea," William said.

Verrall nodded slowly. "That was good of you, Leadford."

"I'm sorry I shot at you," William said. He sat down on the grass near the steps because his legs were still a little weak. "It seems like a strange thing to have done."

"Yes," Nettie said. Her voice was quiet. "Everything before this morning seems strange."

She stood up and went inside the bungalow. After a few minutes she came back with a pot of tea and three cups on a tray. She poured the tea and handed a cup to William and one to Verrall. The tea was hot and strong. William held the warm cup in both hands and drank it slowly.

"Look at the ships," Verrall said, pointing toward the Channel.

A small boat had put out from one of the cruisers, rowing steadily toward the shore. The oars caught the sunlight as they dipped. There was no haste in it.

"There won't be a war," William said.

"No," Verrall said. "I don't think there will be a war. Nobody wants to kill anybody today."

They sat together in the sun, drinking their tea. Nettie looked at William, not with the old pity or anger, and William looked back at her. He still loved her, he knew, but it did not hurt him anymore. The sea was flat and bright, and the wind came warm over the grass.
