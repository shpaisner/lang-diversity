The coal smoke hung low over Clayton. William Leadford walked down the cobbles with his hands in his coat pockets. In his right pocket was the revolver he had bought from the pawnbroker in Burslem. It was heavy and cold against his thigh.

He stopped at the Red Lion. Inside, the men sat at the long tables with their pints of dark beer.

"They've mobilized the reserves," a man named Bates said.

"Let them," another said. "It's good for the ironworks."

Leadford ordered a bitter and stood by the door. He did not care about the Germans or the ironworks. He thought of Nettie's small white hands and the way she had laughed when Verrall drove his motorcar up the lane. Verrall had money from the mines. He had clean fingernails and tweed coats that smelled of dry grass.

"Look at the sky," Bates said, looking out the dirty window.

Above the kiln chimneys, the comet hung in the dusk. It was a pale green smear against the purple dark, with a tail like spilled milk.

Leadford drank his beer. It was flat and bitter. He walked out into the street and turned toward the station. The letter Nettie had left was on the table at home, weighted down by a brass candlestick. I am going with him, Willie, it said. Do not follow us.

He knew where Verrall went in the summer. Everyone in the Five Towns knew. The big hotel at Shaphambury, where the sea was gray and the rich people walked on the promenade.

Leadford felt the weight of the gun. His jaw ached from clenching it.

The train was hot and smelled of wool and coal dust. Two soldiers sat across from Leadford, their tunics unbuttoned at the throat. They passed a bottle of whisky between them.

"Have a drink, mate," the younger one said.

"No," Leadford said.

"Going down to join up?"

"No."

The soldier shrugged and looked out the window. The fields of the midlands were dry and brown. At every station, crowds stood on the platforms, shouting and waving small flags. Newsboys ran along the carriages, yelling about the fleet in the North Sea and the ultimatums. Leadford bought a paper, looked at the black headlines, and dropped it on the floor.

As night came on, the train neared the coast. The green star in the sky was larger now. It threw a faint, sickly light across the carriage seats, making the sleeping soldiers look like drowned men.

Leadford checked the cylinder of the revolver under his coat. Six chambers, all full. He had never shot a man, only rabbits in the woods behind the pit-mounds, and once a stray dog that bit his mother. You held the barrel steady and squeezed. You did not jerk the trigger.

The train stopped at Shaphambury just before midnight. The air off the water was salt and cool, a sharp change from the sulphur of Staffordshire. Leadford walked down the station steps toward the seafront. The streetlamps were lit, but the comet above the water was brighter than the gas. People stood in small knots along the promenade, their faces turned upward, silent and uneasy.

Leadford slept for three hours on a bench behind the sea wall. When the sun came up, it was pale and looked small beside the great green streak that still hung in the western sky.

He bought a cup of tea and a roll at a stall near the pier. His boots were white with chalk dust. He walked past the Grand Hotel, watching the wide glass doors. Men in morning coats came out with newspapers, reading them as they walked. Two boys ran past shouting that the first shots had been fired at sea. Nobody cheered. They only stood and read the papers, then looked up at the green smear in the sky.

At noon, Verrall and Nettie came out of the hotel.

Nettie wore a white dress and a straw hat with a blue ribbon. She looked younger than she had in Clayton, away from the smoke. Verrall walked beside her, carrying a walking stick. He was tall and tanned, and he leaned down to hear what she was saying. When she laughed, Leadford felt a cold knot tighten in his stomach.

They walked east, away from the town, toward the chalk cliffs and the gorse bushes. Leadford followed them at fifty yards. He kept his right hand in his pocket, his thumb on the hammer of the revolver.

The wind blew off the Channel, carrying the smell of seaweed and hot grass. A mile from the hotel, the path dipped into a small hollow behind the dunes where the sea was hidden. Nettie sat down on a patch of turf and took off her hat. Verrall stood above her, lighting a cigarette. Leadford walked over the crest of the dune and stood looking down at them.

Nettie saw him first. Her hand went to her throat, and the color left her face.

"Willie," she said.

Verrall turned, dropping his cigarette into the grass. He looked at Leadford's dusty boots, his cheap coat, and then at his face.

"What do you want here, Leadford?" Verrall asked. His voice was steady, the voice of a man used to giving orders at the pithead.

Leadford pulled the revolver from his pocket. The metal shone dull in the strange light.

"You took her," Leadford said.

"She came," Verrall said. "Put that away. You're making a fool of yourself."

"Don't, Willie," Nettie said. She stood up, holding her straw hat against her chest. "Please go home."

"I'm going to kill him," Leadford said. His voice sounded thin in the open air. He raised the gun and pointed it at Verrall's chest. "Then I'm going to kill myself."

Verrall did not move. He looked at the dark hole of the barrel, then up at Leadford's eyes. "You're a madman," he said.

Leadford pulled back the hammer. It made a sharp, dry click.

At that moment, a shadow fell across the dunes. It was not like a cloud; it was a thick, green mist rolling in from the sea, fast and silent. The air turned cold and smelled of sweet crushed grass and ozone.

Leadford tried to pull the trigger, but his finger felt heavy and slow. The green fog swirled over the crest of the dune, swallowing Verrall's face, swallowing Nettie's white dress. A ringing started in Leadford's ears, high and clear, like a glass struck with a spoon. His knees gave way. He dropped the revolver into the turf and fell forward into the sweet, cold dark.

When Leadford opened his eyes, the mist was gone. The sky above the hollow was a deep, clean blue, brighter than any sky he had ever seen in Staffordshire. The comet was no longer there.

He sat up and rubbed his face. His head did not ache. The tight knot in his stomach, the one he had carried for months, was gone. He felt light, as if he had just woken from a long, dreamless sleep after a hard shift.

He looked at the revolver lying in the grass beside his knee. It looked small and ugly, a piece of black iron that made no sense. He picked it up, broke open the cylinder, and let the six brass cartridges fall into the turf. Then he threw the gun over the edge of the dune toward the beach.

Nettie was sitting a few yards away. She had her knees drawn up, looking out toward the edge of the hollow. Beside her, Verrall was getting to his feet, brushing dry grass from his trousers.

Verrall looked at Leadford. There was no fear in his face, and no anger. He looked like a man who had just realized he was wearing clothes that did not fit him.

"Are you hurt?" Verrall asked.

"No," Leadford said. "I'm fine."

Nettie looked at both of them. Her eyes were clear, without tears. "What happened?" she asked.

"The comet," Verrall said. He took a deep breath. "The air is different."

Leadford stood up. He looked at his own rough, coal-stained hands, then at Nettie's face. He still loved her, he knew that, but the pain of it had vanished. It was like looking at a fire that gave off light without burning.

They walked back toward Shaphambury together along the cliff path. None of them spoke for a long time. Below them, the sea was very flat and green, breaking in small, white lines against the chalk.

Near the town, they passed a bench where a newspaper lay spread open on the slats. The wind turned a corner of the page. Leadford stopped and looked at the thick black letters about the ultimatum and the fleet.

"They were going to fight," Leadford said.

"Yes," Verrall said, stopping beside him. "I suppose they were. Yesterday it seemed necessary."

"It seems foolish now," Nettie said.

They left the paper on the bench and walked down into the town. The streets were quiet. Men and women were coming out of the houses and the hotels, standing in the doorways and looking at one another with surprised faces. Nobody was shouting. Down by the railway station, a group of soldiers sat on the stone wall, their tunics unbuttoned, laughing as they shared a loaf of bread.

The three of them found a small café near the pier that had just opened its shutters. The owner, an old man in a white apron, brought them a pot of black coffee and three thick cups without asking for money.

They sat at a round iron table on the pavement, feeling the warm sun on their shoulders. Verrall poured the coffee. Leadford picked up his cup and drank. It was hot and strong. He looked across the table at Nettie, and then at Verrall, and then out at the bright, empty sea where there were no warships, only a single fishing boat with a brown sail moving toward the harbor.
