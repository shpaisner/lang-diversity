Leadford walked up the hill from the pottery works. The slag heaps were black against the evening sky and the smoke from the kilns lay low in the valley. Above the smoke, in the clear green of the upper sky, the comet hung like a bright smudge of phosphorus.

In the kitchen his mother was boiling water for tea. The oilcloth on the table was cracked and there was a newspaper beside the sugar bowl. The headline said the German fleet had left Kiel.

"There's a letter," his mother said.

He took it and read it by the window. Nettie's handwriting was neat and slanted. She wrote that she could not marry him. She wrote that she was going away with Mr. Verrall, and that she was sorry, and that he must not look for her.

Leadford folded the paper and put it in his waistcoat pocket. His hands were still grey with clay dust from the yard.

"Is it from Nettie?" his mother asked.

"Yes," he said.

"Will she be coming Sunday?"

"No," Leadford said. "She won't be coming Sunday."

He drank his tea. It was hot and bitter and had no milk in it. Across the table the newspaper talked about dreadnoughts and the North Sea, and men in London shouting for war. Leadford looked out the window at the slate roofs and the green star growing brighter over the chimneys.

In the morning he took his savings from the tin box under the floorboards. There were four sovereigns and some silver. He put on his best black coat, though the cuffs were frayed, and walked down into the town to the ironmonger's shop.

The revolver was small and nickel-plated and smelled of machine oil. The ironmonger laid a box of cartridges on the counter beside it.

"For rats?" the ironmonger asked.

"Yes," Leadford said.

He put the revolver in his coat pocket. It was heavy and bumped against his hip when he walked to the station.

The train south was crowded with soldiers in khaki. They had their kit bags in the racks and were passing a bottle of beer from hand to hand. One of them had a newspaper with big black type about an ultimatum.

"We'll be in Berlin by Michaelmas," a young corporal said. He had a red face and his boots were new.

Leadford sat by the window and watched the Midlands go by. The coal pits gave way to green fields and hedges, and then to the flat chalk country near the coast. He did not talk to the soldiers. Every now and then he put his hand in his pocket and felt the cold cylinder of the gun.

At nightfall they changed at the junction. The sky was very clear and the comet filled half the eastern heaven. It was no longer a smudge. It had a long, pale green tail that swept up toward the zenith, and it cast a faint, sickly light on the railway tracks and the white faces of the soldiers waiting on the platform.

A newsboy ran along the carriages shouting about a battle in the Channel. Nobody bought a paper because the train was already moving. Leadford leaned his head against the glass and slept a little, dreaming of Nettie's hair and the smell of the kilns.

Shaphambury was a quiet place by the sea, with white bungalows built along the cliffs and a golf course behind the dunes. Leadford got off the train in the early morning. His black suit was dusty from the cinders and his collar was wilted.

He walked along the parade. There were no chimneys here and the air tasted of salt and dry grass. A few gentlemen in white flannels were walking down toward the bathing machines with towels over their shoulders. They looked at Leadford's black coat and dusty boots and looked away.

He went into a small public house near the station and ordered bread and cheese and a pint of bitter. The barman was reading the morning paper behind the taps.

"They sank two of ours off the Dogger Bank," the barman said. "That's what the wire says."

"Is there a Mr. Verrall staying here?" Leadford asked.

"Young Mr. Verrall? Up at the White Bungalow on the cliff. Him and his lady." The barman wiped a glass with a grey cloth. "Came down Tuesday in a motorcar. Fine car, too."

Leadford paid for his beer and walked out toward the cliffs. The sun was up now, hot and bright, but the green comet was still visible in the daylight, a pale ghost hanging over the sea.

He found the White Bungalow easily. It stood alone at the end of a sandy lane, with a wide veranda facing the water and a low wall of flint stones. Leadford did not go up the lane. He climbed down into the hollow behind the gorse bushes where he could watch the veranda. He took the revolver out of his pocket, checked the chambers, and laid it on the dry turf beside him.

He waited for three hours. The gorse smelled warm and sweet in the sun, and down on the shingle the waves made a steady, scraping noise. Once, far out to sea, he heard a dull thudding, like heavy doors shutting in a cellar. It might have been the guns in the Channel, or only thunder below the horizon.

At noon the French windows of the bungalow opened. Verrall came out first, wearing a cream linen suit and a panama hat. He carried a wicker basket. Then Nettie came out. She wore a blue dress Leadford had never seen before, and she was laughing at something Verrall said.

Leadford stood up from the gorse. His legs were stiff from kneeling on the chalk. He put the revolver in his right hand and walked across the short grass toward the flint wall.

Nettie saw him first. Her mouth opened a little, and the laugh stopped. Verrall turned around, holding the basket.

"Leadford," Verrall said. "What are you doing here?"

"I came for Nettie," Leadford said. His voice sounded thin in the sea air.

"Go back to Staffordshire, man," Verrall said. He was not afraid, only annoyed. "You have no business here."

Leadford raised the gun. His hand was shaking, and the nickel barrel caught the sun. Nettie cried out and stepped in front of Verrall, her hands up. Leadford pulled the trigger. The crack was sharp and small against the sound of the sea.

The bullet chipped the white paint off the veranda post behind them. Leadford pulled the trigger again, but the hammer only clicked on a bad cartridge.

Then he smelled it—a faint, sweet odor, like crushed mint and ozone, drifting in from the sea.

A green mist was rolling over the edge of the cliff. It was not like sea fog, which is grey and wet. It was dry and luminous, and it moved very fast across the grass toward the bungalow.

"What is that?" Nettie said. She dropped her hands and looked out over the water.

Verrall did not answer. He had set the wicker basket down on the turf and was rubbing his eyes.

Leadford tried to cock the revolver again, to clear the bad shell, but his fingers felt thick and clumsy. The sweet smell was in his throat now. It was not unpleasant. It felt like cold water when you are very thirsty.

A gull flew over the cliff, beating its wings hard, and then it stopped flying and dropped straight down into the gorse.

"William," Nettie said.

She took two steps toward him, her blue dress brushing the grass, and then her knees gave way. She sank down quietly on her side, with one arm under her head, as if she were very tired. Verrall sat down hard against the flint wall. His panama hat rolled off onto the path.

Leadford felt his own knees go. He dropped the revolver. It fell into a clump of thrift and disappeared. He tried to stay upright, to remember why he had come down on the train from Staffordshire, to hold onto the anger that had kept him warm all night.

The green mist covered him. He lay down on the warm chalk beside the wall. The air was very cool and still, and all the noise of the sea seemed to go far away, until there was nothing left but the green light behind his eyelids.

When Leadford woke, the sun was low in the west and the mist was gone. The sky was a deep, clear blue, cleaner than he had ever seen it. There was no sign of the comet.

He sat up. His head did not ache, and the stiffness in his legs was gone. He looked down at his dusty black coat, and it seemed like something belonging to a stranger.

Near his hand, the nickel revolver lay in the grass. He picked it up, walked to the edge of the cliff, and threw it out over the shingle. It made a small splash in the shallow water.

When he turned back, Verrall was sitting up by the wall. Nettie was awake too, sitting in the grass with her knees drawn up, looking at the sea.

Leadford walked over to them. Neither of them flinched.

"Are you hurt?" Leadford asked.

"No," Nettie said. "I don't think so."

"There was a war," Verrall said. He looked puzzled, as if trying to remember a dream. "The ships were fighting out there."

"Look," Leadford said.

Out on the Channel, a grey cruiser was steaming slowly toward the coast. There was no smoke from her funnels, and no guns were firing.

"They've stopped," Verrall said.

"Yes," Leadford said.

He looked at Nettie. She was very pretty in the blue dress, and he knew he loved her, just as he had in Staffordshire. But the tight, hard knot in his chest was gone. It did not matter anymore.

"Come inside," Nettie said, standing up and brushing the dry grass from her skirt. "There's tea in the basket, and you've come a long way, William."

"Thank you," Leadford said. He followed them up onto the veranda, and the air was fresh and clean in his lungs.
