The coal smoke lay flat over the Staffordshire kilns in the late afternoon. William Leadford sat at the oilcloth table with his mother’s tea growing cold beside his elbow. On the table lay a folded newspaper and a small envelope. The paper said the German fleet was coaling at Kiel and British cruisers were moving in the North Sea. Outside, the men from the pits stood by the public house, their faces gray with dust, waiting for strike pay that had not come.

William read Nettie’s letter a third time. The ink was pale blue and the handwriting small and neat. She wrote that she was sorry, and that she had gone with Edward Verrall to the south coast. A woman could not live in the smoke forever, she wrote, waiting on a clerk’s wages.

He folded the letter and put it in his waistcoat pocket. Above the chimney pots, the sky was clearing to a hard green, and the comet hung there before sunset, bright as a magnesium flare.

“You haven’t touched your tea, Will,” his mother said from the scullery.

“I’m going out,” he said.

He went up the narrow stairs to his room and took the old six-chambered revolver from his trunk. He had bought it two years ago in Burslem when he thought he might go to Canada. He checked the cylinder. All six cartridges were dull but sound. He slipped the heavy iron into his coat pocket and walked down the stairs and out into the street toward the station.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool and cheap tobacco. Across from William sat two young men in khaki tunics with their kit bags between their boots. They were passing a flat bottle of gin and talking about Portsmouth.

“They’ll send us across by Tuesday,” the taller soldier said. “My brother’s in the artillery. He says the horses are at the docks.”

“Let them,” the other said. “It’s better than the mill.”

William looked out the smeared window. The train ran fast through the dark midlands, and the comet had risen above the black hedgerows. It was no longer a small star. It had a long green tail that swept across the sky, turning the white steam from the engine a sickly color.

He kept his right hand in his pocket, his fingers curled around the grip of the revolver. His knuckles ached from the cold. He thought of Verrall, with his father’s money and his clean fingernails and his motorcar. He thought of Nettie sitting beside Verrall in a bright room near the sea, laughing at the way people in Staffordshire talked.

The taller soldier offered him the gin. William shook his head.

“Suit yourself, mate,” the soldier said, and drank. “Look at that bloody thing. Looks like the end of the world.”

“It’s just gas,” William said.

“Everything’s gas nowadays,” the soldier said, and laughed.

William leaned his forehead against the cold glass. The train clattered over the points at Crewe, heading south, and the green light fell across his face.

Shaphambury was quiet when he stepped out of the station, though it was not yet ten o’clock. The air smelled of salt and wet chalk instead of coal. Along the esplanade, the big white hotels had their blinds drawn, and only a few gas lamps burned along the sea wall. The green light from the comet was stronger than the moon. It lay across the Channel like oil on water.

William walked down to the front and went into a small tavern behind the pier. Three fishermen stood at the bar, and an old man read a late edition by the window.

“Bitter,” William said, and laid two pennies on the zinc.

The landlord pulled the pint slowly. “You’re from the north,” he said.

“Staffordshire.”

“Bad business up there with the pits. Bad business everywhere.” The landlord nodded toward the window. “They say the reserves are called up tonight. My boy’s on a destroyer out of Dover.”

William drank the bitter. It was flat and cold in his stomach. “Do you know the Grand Hotel?”

“Up on the cliff. You won’t get a bed there on a clerk’s coat, friend.”

“I don’t want a bed.”

He finished the beer and walked out onto the promenade. The wind had died completely. The sea was flat as slate, and the comet stretched overhead from the cliffs to the horizon. There was a sweetish smell in the air, like cut grass after rain, though there was no grass near the shingle. William walked up the gravel path toward the hotel, his hand tight on the iron in his pocket.

He saw them coming down the cliff path from the hotel terrace. Verrall wore a light flannel suit and carried a walking stick. Nettie walked beside him in a dark coat, her hands tucked into her sleeves. Even in the green dusk, William knew the way she walked, with her head tilted slightly forward.

They stopped by the low stone wall above the beach. Verrall pointed his stick toward the dark water, where the riding lights of two patrol boats moved slowly eastward.

William stepped out from the shadow of the tamarisk bushes. His boots crunched on the gravel.

“Nettie,” he said.

She turned and caught her breath. Verrall stepped half a pace in front of her, squinting through the light.

“Leadford?” Verrall said. His voice was steady, though he tightened his grip on the cane. “What are you doing here?”

“I came to see Nettie,” William said. He pulled the revolver from his pocket. The barrel looked dull and black.

“Don’t be a fool, William,” Nettie said. “Put that away.”

“You left without a word,” William said. His throat was dry and hot. “You ran off with him.”

“I’ll give you money, Leadford,” Verrall said. “If that’s what you’ve come for.”

“Shut up,” William said, and raised the gun.

Then the air seemed to thicken. A low, rushing sound came from above the sea, like a heavy train crossing a bridge far away. A green mist dropped swiftly over the cliffs, warm and dense, smelling of sweet clover. William tried to pull the trigger, but his fingers would not close. His knees went weak, and he saw Nettie sinking onto the gravel beside Verrall. Then the green dark took him, and he fell.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was well up and the sea was bright blue. There was no green light left in the sky. The air was clear, and when he drew it into his lungs, it tasted clean, as if the world had been washed in the night.

He sat up slowly on the gravel. His coat was damp with dew. Beside his left boot lay the revolver. He looked at it for a long time. It seemed small and ugly and foolish, like a toy a child had dropped. The anger that had burned in his chest for three days was gone. He felt hollow and quiet, like a man waking after a long fever.

Twenty yards away, by the wall, Verrall was sitting up, rubbing the back of his neck. Nettie lay curled on the grass beside him, her dark coat pulled around her shoulders. She stirred, pushed her hair back from her face, and sat up too.

William stood and brushed the gravel from his trousers. He did not pick up the gun. He walked over to the wall and looked down at them.

“Are you hurt?” he asked.

Verrall looked up. The arrogance was gone from his face; he looked pale and ordinary, like any young man who had slept in his clothes. “No,” Verrall said. “My head is clear. What happened?”

“The comet,” William said. “The green mist.”

Nettie looked at William, and for the first time since he had stepped from the bushes, there was no fear in her eyes. She reached out and touched his sleeve. “You’re shivering, Will,” she said.

They walked down the hill together toward the town. The streets were full of people who had come out of their houses and hotels, standing in small groups in the morning sun. Nobody was shouting. Down on the shingle, the fishermen were pushing their boats into the surf, and the patrol vessels in the Channel had turned back toward the harbor.

They found a small tea shop open near the pier. The owner was sweeping broken glass from a dropped tray off the threshold, whistling softly.

“Any news from London?” Verrall asked as they sat at a wooden table by the window.

“The telegraph boy went past ten minutes ago,” the owner said, setting down three thick white cups. “Says the fleet’s ordered back to port. Says the Germans sent a wire saying they aren’t coming.” He shook his head and smiled. “Seems nobody wants to fight today.”

Verrall poured the tea. His hands were steady. “I’m sorry, Leadford,” he said quietly. “About all of it. I behaved badly.”

“It doesn’t matter now,” William said. He wrapped his hands around the warm cup. He looked at Nettie across the table. She was looking out at the blue water, her face calm in the sunlight. He knew he still loved her, and he knew she did not belong to him, and the knowledge did not hurt him at all.

“What will you do, Will?” she asked, turning back to him. “Will you go back to Staffordshire?”

“I think so,” William said. “There’s work to be done there. The pits and the smoke don’t need to stay the way they are.”

They sat together in the clean light, drinking their tea while the gulls wheeled white against the sky, and nobody spoke of the gun on the cliff path.
