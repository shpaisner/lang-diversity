William Leadford sat at the oilcloth table and read the letter again. The kitchen smelled of cabbage and the damp coal they burned in Staffordshire. Outside, the pottery kilns smoked against the grey sky.

Nettie had written it in pencil. She was sorry, she said, and she had gone with Verrall. Verrall had money and a motorcar and did not work in a clerk’s office for eighteen shillings a week.

William folded the paper and put it in his pocket. He drank his cold tea. His mother was in the scullery washing up. He could hear the clatter of the plates.

"I am going out," he said.

"Where are you going, Will?" she called.

"Out," he said.

He walked down the cinder path past the slag heaps. The men from the pits were coming off shift, their faces black with dust, carrying their tin bottles. They stood in small groups at the corners, looking up at the sky and talking about the Germans. The newspapers said the fleet was steaming into the North Sea.

Above the smoke of the furnaces, the comet hung in the northeast. It was a pale green smudge, very bright even in the late afternoon.

William walked to the pawnshop in the High Street. Inside, it was dark and smelled of old wool and brass. He laid two sovereigns on the counter—all he had saved for the wedding—and bought a heavy Webley revolver and a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker wrapped them in brown paper without asking any questions. William put the parcel in his coat pocket. It pulled the wool down on his right side, heavy and solid against his hip.

The train south was crowded and hot. William sat in a third-class carriage with his knees pressed against the knees of a reservist in a khaki tunic. The reservist had a kitbag between his boots and smelled of beer and wet serge.

"They'll have us across the Channel by Tuesday," the reservist said. He offered William a cigarette.

William took it and struck a match on the heel of his boot. "Is it certain, then?"

"Certain as death," the reservist said. "The Kaiser won't back down. Not with the big ships all lit up at Spithead."

Another man in the corner lowered his newspaper. "It's the comet," he said. "It makes men's blood hot. My brother in Birmingham says nobody can sleep for it."

William looked out the smeared window. The flat fields of the Midlands were sliding past under a low sky. He kept his right hand in his coat pocket, his fingers curled around the cold grip of the revolver. He had loaded all six chambers in the station lavatory before boarding.

He thought of Verrall. He had seen Verrall once at a cricket match, wearing white flannels and laughing with the gentry. Verrall had soft hands and a smooth voice. He thought of Nettie sitting beside Verrall in the motorcar, her dark hair blown back by the wind, her small chin tilted up. The thought was like a sour lump in his chest that would not go down.

At the junction, they waited an hour on a siding while a troop train rattled past in the dark, the carriages full of singing men. High above the telegraph wires, the green star burned through the mist, wider now, with a faint tail like milk spilled on black glass.

Shaphambury was cold when he got down from the train at dawn. The wind blew off the Channel, carrying the smell of salt and kelp. The town was mostly empty, with boarding houses shuttered for the season, though down by the front a crowd stood watching the sea.

William walked along the esplanade. Out past the breakwater, three cruisers lay anchored, dark and low on the grey water. Thin trails of black smoke rose from their funnels. Somewhere to the east, a heavy gun boomed, a dull thump that shook the soles of his boots.

"They're ranging," an old fisherman said beside him. "Won't be long now."

William walked past the town, out toward the dunes where the new bungalows sat among the coarse grass. He had found the address in the pocket of Nettie’s apron before he left—an envelope from a Shaphambury post office, torn in half, that she had forgotten to burn.

The bungalow was painted white, with a red-tiled roof and a low wooden fence. No smoke came from the chimney. The curtains were drawn.

He crouched behind a ridge of marram grass fifty yards away. The sand was wet with dew and soaked through the knees of his trousers. He took out the revolver and laid it on his thigh. His hands were shaking from the cold and from not having eaten since the night before.

Overhead, the dawn light did not come properly. The eastern sky was a sickly yellow-green, and the comet had grown until its tail stretched halfway across the sky. The sea looked like lead. He waited, watching the white door of the bungalow, his teeth clamped tight together.

At seven o'clock, the door opened. Verrall came out in a tweed jacket and a cap, carrying a tin bucket for water. He walked down the wooden steps toward the pump in the yard.

William stood up from the grass and walked toward the fence. His legs were stiff.

Verrall saw him when he was twenty yards away. He stopped with the bucket in his hand.

"What do you want?" Verrall called out. His voice was sharp, not smooth like William had imagined.

William did not answer. He climbed over the low fence and raised the revolver.

"Hold on," Verrall said, dropping the bucket. It clattered on the stones. "Don't be a fool, Leadford."

The door of the bungalow opened and Nettie stepped out. She wore a blue dressing gown, and her hair was down over her shoulders. When she saw William, she put her hand to her mouth.

"Will," she said. "Don't."

"You went with him," William said. His voice sounded thin in the wind.

"I couldn't stay there, Will. You know I couldn't."

"You lied to me," William said. He looked at Verrall’s face, pale against the white wall of the house. "You stole her."

He pulled the trigger. The revolver kicked hard against his palm, and the crack was loud in the quiet morning. Wood splintered from the doorpost two inches from Verrall’s head.

Nettie screamed. Verrall lunged forward, tripping over the fallen bucket, and William cocked the hammer again. He meant to shoot Verrall in the chest, and then shoot Nettie, and then turn the barrel on himself.

Just then, a strange smell hit his nostrils. It was sweet and sharp, like crushed mint and clean rain. He looked up. A thick green fog was rolling in from the sea, swallowing the cruisers and the breakwater, moving fast across the dunes.

The green mist reached the bungalow in ten seconds. It was not cold like sea fog. It was warm and heavy, and when William breathed it in, his knees went weak.

Out on the water, a siren wailed once and then cut off. A second gun boomed, muffled by the green cloud, and then there was silence.

William tried to raise the revolver, but his arm felt as though it were made of wet clay. The gun slipped from his fingers and fell into the sand. He saw Verrall sitting against the wooden steps, his head lolling back against the rail, his eyes half-shut. Nettie was down on her knees by the gate, her blue gown spread out on the grass, leaning forward as if she were very tired.

William took two steps toward them and fell. His cheek pressed into the damp sand. He could taste the salt and the grit.

He tried to remember why he was angry, why he had come all the way from Staffordshire with two sovereigns' worth of iron in his pocket. He thought of the coal smoke and the cold tea and the letter in pencil, and it all seemed very small and far away, like something he had read about in a book when he was a boy.

The green vapor swirled over him, thick and pale. It smelled wonderful. His chest rose and fell slowly, drinking it in. He heard the wind drop until the marram grass stopped rustling. Everything was very quiet. Even the sea seemed to have stopped breaking on the shore. He closed his eyes and went to sleep.

When William woke, the sun was high and the sky was a clear, deep blue he had never seen before. The green mist was gone. The air was so crisp that every blade of grass and every grain of sand stood out sharp and clean.

He sat up and brushed the sand from his face. His head felt light and clear. He looked at the revolver lying a foot away in the dirt. It looked ugly and silly, like a broken toy. He picked it up, walked to the edge of the dunes, and threw it as far as he could into the sea. It made a small white splash and sank.

When he turned back, Verrall and Nettie were awake. Verrall was sitting on the steps, rubbing his eyes. Nettie was standing by the fence, looking out toward the water where the three cruisers lay silent and motionless, their flags hanging limp in the still air.

"William," Nettie said. Her voice was quiet. She did not look afraid.

"Good morning, Nettie," he said. He walked over to the fence.

Verrall looked up at him. "You missed," Verrall said. He smiled slightly. It was a good smile. "I'm glad you missed."

"So am I," William said. He looked at Nettie’s face in the bright sunlight. He still loved her, he knew, in a plain, quiet way, without the sickness in his chest. "I was a fool."

"We were all fools," Verrall said, standing up. "Listen."

They stood together by the fence and listened. There were no guns firing in the Channel. Down in the town, a church bell began to ring, slow and steady across the bright water. Nettie reached out and touched William's sleeve, and then Verrall's, and the three of them stood looking out at the new morning.
