The pit-heaps outside Clayton were black against the evening sky, and the smoke from the potteries hung low over the brick rows. William Leadford walked home with coal dust in the creases of his neck. His boots were worn through at the heel. He stopped at the corner shop and bought a half-penny paper. On the front page it said the German fleet was in the North Sea and the reserves were called up in London.

"It's war by Tuesday," the shopkeeper said.

"Let them have it," Leadford said.

He walked up the hill to his mother's cottage. His mother was boiling cabbage in the scullery. She did not look up when he came in. Leadford went into the front room and took the old six-chambered revolver from behind the loose brick in the chimney. It was heavy and cold. He sat at the deal table and wiped the barrel with an oily rag. Outside, above the tall chimneys, the comet hung in the north. It was bigger than it had been on Monday, a pale green smear against the dark.

He thought of Nettie's small white hands and the way she laughed when she was pleased. She had gone off in Verrall's motor-car on Sunday afternoon. Verrall wore a tweed cap and leather gauntlets and had three hundred pounds a year from his uncle's brewery. Leadford spun the cylinder of the revolver. It clicked six times, sharp and clean. He put the gun in his coat pocket and took his cap from the peg.

"Where are you going, Willie?" his mother called from the scullery.

"Out," he said.

"There's cabbage."

"I'm not hungry."

He shut the door and walked down the cobbles toward the railway station.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool and stale pipe tobacco. Two commercial travelers sat across from him, talking about the price of iron and the trouble in the Balkans. Leadford looked out the window at the dark fields rushing past. Above the hedges, the green star burned steady and bright.

At the coast the air was cold and smelled of salt and kelp. He walked down the promenade past the shuttered bathing machines. There were few people out, and those who walked had their collars turned up, looking out to sea where the warships were said to be steaming without lights.

He went into a public house near the pier and ordered a pint of bitter. The barman was reading a pink evening paper under the gaslight.

"Seen a dark-haired girl with a fellow in a big Napier car?" Leadford asked.

The barman folded the paper. "The gentleman with the yellow scarf? They're up at the Grand. Came in before tea."

Leadford drank the bitter. It was flat and cold. He put the glass down on the zinc counter and felt the weight of the iron in his pocket. His thumb rested on the smooth wood of the grip.

"Is there trouble?" the barman asked.

"No," Leadford said. "No trouble."

He went back out into the wind. The Grand Hotel sat on the cliff, its yellow windows shining over the black water. He walked up the gravel drive and stood in the shadow of the tamarisk bushes. The green light from the sky lay over the wet grass and the white gravel. He waited, his hands deep in his pockets, watching the glass doors of the hotel.

They came out after nine o'clock. Verrall wore a heavy ulster and carried a walking stick. Nettie had a white shawl over her dark hair. She was laughing at something Verrall said, her head tilted back. They did not turn toward the town. They took the cliff path that led out toward the downs.

Leadford followed them at fifty paces. The turf was springy under his worn boots and made no sound. Below them, the sea broke against the chalk in long white lines. The comet was very low now, and its tail stretched halfway across the sky like green smoke.

When they reached the shelter of a gorse bush by the edge of the cliff, Verrall stopped and lit a cigarette. The match flared yellow against the green dark. Nettie leaned against his shoulder.

Leadford stepped out onto the path. "Nettie," he said.

She turned quickly and pulled the shawl tight around her throat. "Willie."

Verrall dropped the match and stepped in front of her. "What are you doing here, Leadford?"

"I've come for her," Leadford said. He took his right hand out of his pocket. The revolver was dull in the green light.

"Put that away," Verrall said. His voice was tight, though he tried to keep it steady. "Don't be a fool."

"You took her," Leadford said. "You had everything else, and you took her too."

"She came of her own will," Verrall said.

"Willie, please," Nettie said. She was crying now, her face pale and small in the strange light. "Go home. Please go home."

Leadford raised the barrel until it pointed at Verrall's chest. His arm was stiff, and his heart hammered against his ribs.

"Stand aside, Nettie," Leadford said.

She did not move. She caught Verrall's sleeve with both hands. "If you shoot him, you shoot me too."

Leadford's finger tightened on the trigger. The iron was cold against his skin. Then the wind stopped.

It did not die down slowly; it simply ceased, as if a door had been shut across the sea. The waves below the cliff grew quiet. A thick, green mist began to fall out of the sky, warm and heavy, smelling faintly of crushed leaves and ozone.

"What is that?" Verrall said. He looked up, his face turning the color of pond water.

Leadford pulled the trigger. The hammer fell with a loud crack, and the flash was bright orange against the green fog. The bullet went wide, whining off into the dark over the sea. Before he could cock the piece again, his knees gave way.

It was not like fainting. The air was sweet in his throat, and a great heaviness pressed down on his shoulders. He dropped the revolver into the gorse. He saw Nettie sink slowly to the grass, her white shawl spreading around her like spilled milk. Verrall went down beside her, one hand still out as if to catch her.

Leadford lay on his side with his cheek against the wet turf. The green fog rolled over them, thick and silent, blotting out the sea and the cliff and the hotel lights. He heard the distant sound of a ship's horn out in the Channel, long and low, and then it stopped. He closed his eyes and slept.

When he woke, the sun was up and the sky was a clear, pale blue. There was no fog. The air was sharper and cleaner than any air he had ever breathed, even in the hills above Staffordshire.

Leadford sat up and looked at his hands. The coal dust was still under his nails, and his cuffs were frayed, but his head felt light and empty. He looked for the rage that had carried him from the potteries to the sea, and it was not there. It felt as if someone had opened a window in a dark, close room.

Ten feet away, Verrall was sitting up, rubbing his eyes with the heels of his hands. Nettie lay beside him, her breathing slow and even. She opened her eyes and looked at the blue sky, then at Verrall, and then at Leadford.

"Willie?" she said. Her voice was quiet. There was no fear in it.

"Are you hurt?" Leadford asked.

"No," she said. She sat up and smoothed her skirt.

The revolver lay in the grass between them. Leadford picked it up. The blued steel looked small and foolish in the bright morning sun. He broke the cylinder, shook the five remaining cartridges into his palm, and threw the gun over the edge of the cliff. He did not hear it hit the water. He dropped the cartridges into the gorse one by one.

Verrall stood up and brushed the dry grass from his ulster. He looked at Leadford for a long time. "I'm sorry," Verrall said.

"It doesn't matter," Leadford said, and knew as he said it that it was true.

They walked back along the cliff path toward the town. Neither of them hurried. Below them, the Channel was flat and silver in the morning sun. Two gray cruisers lay anchored a mile off the shore, their decks quiet, no smoke coming from their funnels.

In the town, people were standing in the streets in their shirtsleeves and dressing gowns. Nobody was shouting. A baker had his shutters down and was handing out loaves of bread to anyone who came by, without asking for pennies. Near the pier, a newsboy sat on an upturned crate, his bundle of papers still tied with twine.

"What's the news?" Verrall asked him.

"The telegraph clerk says the armies have stopped," the boy said. "The men threw their rifles in the ditches. Nobody wants to fight."

Nettie took Leadford's arm, just above the elbow, and then let it go. "Will you come back to Clayton?" she asked.

"Yes," Leadford said. "There is work to be done there. The houses are bad, and the air is bad."

"I am going with Edward," she said.

Leadford looked at her face. She was very pretty in the morning light, but she looked like a person he had known a long time ago, in another country. "I know," he said. "That is right."

They stopped at a small café overlooking the promenade. The proprietor was setting out clean white cups on the iron tables. They sat down together in the sun, the three of them, and waited for the coffee to boil, watching the quiet sea.
