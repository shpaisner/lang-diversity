The letter was on the table beside the cold tea. William read it twice. Her handwriting was small and neat, like the stitches on her Sunday coat. She had gone with Verrall. They were going south to the coast.

Outside, the kilns at Clayton were smoking. The smoke lay low over the slate roofs because the air was damp and heavy. Up above the smoke, in the pale afternoon sky, the green star hung. People in the street were stopping to look at it. They called it the comet.

William folded the letter and put it in his breast pocket. He went to the cupboard and took down the tin box. Inside was the Belgian revolver he had bought from a peddler in Hanley for twelve shillings. It was a heavy, ugly thing with a short barrel and worn grips. He broke it open and checked the cylinder. Five brass cartridges sat in the chambers, dull and greasy. He spun it once and clicked it shut.

His mother came up the stairs. Her boots were heavy on the bare wood.

"You're not going to the pit?" she asked from the doorway.

"No," William said.

"There's talk of the Germans. At the shop they said the fleet's moving."

"Let it move."

"Where are you going, Will?"

"To see a man about a clerk's place," he lied. He put the revolver in his coat pocket. It pulled the wool down on the right side.

"You look white," she said.

"It's the smoke."

He went past her and down the narrow stairs. The street was full of men in caps going toward the works. None of them looked at him. They were all looking up at the green light growing in the east.

William rode third class to the south. The carriage smelled of wet wool and cheap tobacco. Two young men in khaki sat across from him, their rifles resting between their knees. They were drinking stout from dark bottles and laughing too loud.

"They'll have us across the Channel by Friday," one of them said. He had a red neck and small, bright eyes.

"We'll give them a proper hiding," the other said.

William looked out the window. The fields were flat and green, and then it grew dark and the comet showed clearly above the hedgerows. It was no longer a star. It was a great green fan of light, brighter than the moon, washing the grass in a cold, sickly color.

At the junction he got out to change trains. He went into the refreshment room and ordered a bitter. The man behind the bar was reading a paper with thick black headlines.

"War by midnight," the barman said, setting the glass down. "They're shelling the coast already, some say."

"Give me a whisky too," William said.

"Going down to enlist?"

"No."

The whisky burned his throat. He felt the revolver pressing against his hip through the coat. His hand was steady when he put the glass down, though he had not eaten since morning. The green light came through the frosted glass of the pub window, turning the bottles behind the bar a strange, pale jade. Everyone in the room had stopped talking for a moment to look at the glass, and then they began talking again, louder than before, about ships and guns and what the Germans would do.

When he reached the coast, the wind was cold and smelled of salt and kelp. It was much cleaner than the Staffordshire air, though the green light from the sky made the water look like oiled brass.

William walked along the gravel path above the beach. Below him, the waves broke white and flat against the shingle. A mile out, two dark shapes moved slowly without lights. Destroyers, he thought. Searching the water.

He found the boarding house near the cliffs. It had white railings and a small garden with stunted bushes bent by the wind. He stood across the road in the shadow of a brick wall and waited. His feet were cold in his thin boots.

At nine o'clock the front door opened. Verrall came out first, wearing a light tweed coat and a soft cap. He turned to help Nettie down the steps. She was wearing her blue dress, the one she had worn to the fair in Newcastle. She laughed at something Verrall said. Her laugh was clear and bright in the quiet street.

William put his hand in his pocket. His fingers closed around the checkered grip of the Belgian gun. His thumb found the hammer.

They walked down toward the cliffs, away from the town lights. Verrall had his arm around her waist. He walked with the easy stride of a man who had never carried coal or breathed dust.

William stepped out from the wall and followed them. The gravel crunched under his boots, loud in his own ears, but the sea was louder. Above them, the comet filled half the sky, burning silently in green fire.

They stopped near a wooden bench at the edge of the down. Below, the black water slapped against the chalk cliffs. Far off to the east, a searchlight swept the horizon, a thin white finger probing the dark.

William walked up behind them. He did not try to be quiet now.

"Nettie," he said.

She turned quickly. In the green glare of the comet, her face looked pale and hollow, like bone. Verrall stepped in front of her, his hands coming out of his pockets.

"What are you doing here, Leadford?" Verrall asked. His voice was tight, gentlemanly, trying not to sound afraid.

"I came for her," William said. He pulled the revolver out. It looked very small and black in the huge green light.

Nettie gave a short cry. "Will, don't."

"You left me," William said to her. "You took my things and went with him."

"Put that down, you fool," Verrall said, and took a step forward.

William raised the gun and pulled the trigger. The crack was sharp and flat. It did not echo; the sea wind took the sound away instantly. Verrall flinched, untouched, and lunged forward. William cocked the hammer again, his thumb slipping on the cold steel.

Then the wind died. It stopped all at once, as if a heavy door had shut. The air grew thick and warm, smelling suddenly of crushed mint and something sweet, like summer hay.

William tried to aim, but his arms felt made of lead. The green light thickened into a fog that rolled up over the edge of the cliff, fast and silent.

Verrall stopped halfway to him. He put a hand to his forehead and swayed on his heels.

"What is that?" he said. His voice was thick and slow.

Nettie sat down hard on the grass beside the bench. She did not look at the gun. She looked at the green mist pouring over the downs, swallowing the town lights, swallowing the white searchlights out at sea.

William dropped the revolver. It fell into the wet turf without a sound. His knees gave way and he went down onto his hands, then onto his side. The grass was cold against his cheek, but the air he breathed was warm and very sweet.

He tried to remember why he hated Verrall. He could see Verrall lying a few yards away, curled on his side like a sleeping boy, his tweed coat pulled up around his shoulders. William tried to find the anger in his chest, the hot, sour knot he had carried all the way from Staffordshire. It wasn't there. There was only the sweet green fog and a great heaviness in his eyes.

Out on the water, a ship's horn blew once, a low, mournful sound that trailed off into nothing. Then there was no sound at all except the gentle wash of the tide below the cliff.

William closed his eyes. He thought of his mother's kitchen, and the smell of coal smoke, and how small and dark his life had been. Then he did not think of anything at all.

When he woke, the sun was up. The sky was a pale, washed blue, cleaner than any sky he had ever seen. The comet was gone, lost in the bright daylight.

William sat up. His head did not ache. His lungs felt wide and clean, as if he had never breathed coal dust in his life. He looked at his hands. They were still scarred from the pit and stained dark at the knuckles, but they looked like the hands of a stranger, or a good tool he had just been given.

A few yards away, the Belgian revolver lay in the grass. The morning dew was on the barrel, and it looked foolish and rusted, like a toy a child had dropped.

Verrall was sitting up, rubbing his eyes with the backs of his hands. Nettie sat beside him, her knees drawn up, her blue dress dark with dew. She was looking out at the sea. Two warships sat motionless on the flat water, their smoke stacks cold. No flags were flying from their masts.

"Will," Nettie said softly. She did not sound afraid.

William stood up and walked over to them. He looked down at Verrall. There was no rage left in him, not even a memory of it. He wondered how he could ever have wanted to put a piece of lead into this man's chest.

"Are you hurt?" William asked.

"No," Verrall said. He looked up at William, his face puzzled and very clear. "I thought you were going to kill me."

"I thought I was," William said. He sat down on the grass beside them, facing the sea. "It seems a long time ago."

"Look at the ships," Nettie said. "They aren't fighting."

"No," William said. "Nobody is fighting."

They sat together on the wet grass in the bright morning, watching the quiet sea, and none of them spoke of going back.
