The coal dust settled on the windowsill even with the sash down. Leadford sat at the table and looked at the revolver. It was a small Belgian pistol he had bought from a clerk in Burslem for twelve shillings. The barrel was pitted, and the grip was cheap horn, cracked near the screw. He wiped it with an oily rag and put it in his coat pocket.

Outside, the potteries were dark because of the strike. Down the lane, the men stood by the corner of the brick wall with their hands in their pockets and their caps pulled down. They were talking about the Germans and the fleet in the North Sea.

Leadford walked past them without speaking. He went into the Red Lion and ordered a pint of bitter. The barman pulled it slowly.

"They say the Dutch ports are full of transports," the barman said.

"Do they?" Leadford said.

"It's in the Sentinel."

Leadford drank the beer. It was flat and cold. He did not care about the Germans or the fleet. He thought about Nettie's hair when she took the pins out, and he thought about Verrall's big grey motorcar with the brass lamps. Verrall had taken her to the coast. Everyone in the row knew it by noon.

He finished the pint and went out into the street. Above the black chimneys of the kilns, the comet hung in the northeast. It was larger than it had been on Tuesday, and the tail had a pale, greenish cast, like gaslight seen through river fog. It did not look like a star. It looked like something coming down the road with a lantern.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool and shag tobacco. Leadford sat by the window with his knees pressed against the opposite bench. Two reservists in dirty khaki sat across from him, sharing a newspaper. They were young men from Wolverhampton with red necks and big, raw knuckles.

"It's a certainty," the taller one said, tapping the paper. "They've got the guns loaded at Chatham."

"My brother's on the Implacable," the other said. "He wrote my mother from Portsmouth. Said they haven't had shore leave since Sunday."

Leadford looked out at the flat fields of the Midlands. The hedges were dark in the dusk, and the cows stood near the gates. Every time the train stopped at a junction, there were more men on the platforms with kit bags, and the stationmasters looked worried and important.

He reached into his pocket and felt the Belgian pistol. The metal was warm from his thigh. He had five cartridges in the cylinder. He had never shot anything in his life except a tin can on the slag heap behind the foundry, and he had missed it twice before he hit it.

At Oxford, the sky was clear and the comet was bright enough to throw a faint shadow on the platform stones. It was a sickly green shadow, like the light in a chemist's window. A woman on the platform was crying while a young man in a bowler hat tried to kiss her cheek, and the guard blew his whistle.

Leadford leaned his head back against the wood and closed his eyes. He could hear the wheels clicking over the joints in the rail. Verrall, the wheels said. Verrall, Verrall, Verrall. He opened his eyes and watched the dark trees slide past the glass.

Shaphambury was cold and the wind came off the Channel with salt in it. The esplanade was nearly empty except for a few old men in overcoats walking dogs and two policemen standing under a streetlamp. Out beyond the breakwater, the sea was black, and far off to the east a searchlight swept the low clouds, went out, and swept again.

Leadford walked past the boarding houses until he came to the big hotel by the cliff. The dining room windows were lit, and the curtains were only half drawn. He stood across the road by the iron railings above the beach and watched.

They were at a table near the corner. Verrall wore a dark dinner jacket and a white shirt that shone under the electric lights. He was pouring wine from a bottle wrapped in a napkin. Nettie sat across from him in a blue dress Leadford had never seen before. She was laughing at something Verrall said, and she touched the stem of her glass with two fingers.

Leadford stood by the railings for an hour. The wind went through his thin coat, and his teeth began to ache from clamping his jaw shut. He watched them finish their coffee. Verrall signed a slip of paper and helped Nettie with her wrap.

They came out through the revolving doors onto the terrace. Verrall lit a cigarette, cupping the match in his hands, and the flare showed his smooth, brown face. Then they walked down the steps toward the cliff path that led to the rented bungalows above the sand.

Leadford crossed the road behind them. His boots made no noise in the blown sand along the kerb.

The cliff path was narrow, bordered by tamarisk bushes that hissed in the wind. Ahead of him, Verrall's cigarette glowed red and then faded. Nettie was walking close to him, her hand through his arm.

Leadford took the pistol out of his pocket. His fingers were stiff with the cold, and he had to use both hands to thumb the hammer back. The click sounded very loud in the dark, and he stopped, holding his breath, waiting for them to turn. They did not turn. They were talking softly, and the surf below the cliff covered the sound.

Above the sea, the comet filled half the sky. It was no longer a star with a tail; it was a great, pale fan of green fire that seemed to reach down toward the horizon. Out on the water, there was a dull thump, like a door slamming in a cellar, and then another. A yellow flash blinked far out by the point.

"Did you hear that?" Nettie said. Her voice was clear and high.

"Guns," Verrall said. "Practice, probably. Or the real thing."

Leadford stepped out from the shadow of the tamarisk. "Nettie," he said.

They both stopped and turned. Verrall dropped his cigarette and stepped slightly in front of her.

"Who's there?" Verrall said.

"It's Will," Nettie said. She sounded tired, not frightened.

Leadford raised the pistol. He could see Verrall's white shirt front, a clean target against the dark coat. Then a smell came over the cliff edge. It was sweet and sharp, like crushed almonds and fresh rain on hot iron. The green light thickened in the air, turning into a low mist that rolled over the grass. Leadford tried to pull the trigger, but his knees gave way, and he went down onto the damp turf as the green dark closed over his eyes.

When he woke, the sun was up and the sea was very blue. There was no wind. The air tasted clean, like water from a deep well, and his chest did not hurt when he breathed.

Leadford sat up slowly. His coat was damp with dew. Beside his knee, the Belgian pistol lay in the short grass. The brass of the cartridges in the cylinder looked dull and small. He picked the gun up and turned it over in his hand. It felt very heavy and very foolish, like a toy he had outgrown and forgotten in a drawer.

He stood up and walked to the edge of the cliff. Below him, the tide was out, leaving wide flats of wet sand that shone in the morning light. Far out in the Channel, three grey warships sat motionless on the flat water. No smoke came from their funnels. No guns were firing.

Leadford drew his arm back and threw the pistol out over the cliff. It turned over twice in the bright air and splashed into the shallow water near the rocks.

He looked down at his hands. They were still stained with coal dust around the nails, and his cuffs were frayed, but he felt no shame about them. He thought about the potteries, and the strike, and the men standing by the brick wall, and it all seemed like a bad dream he had woken from in a strange room. He brushed the dry grass from his trousers and walked back toward the path where he had seen them last.

They were sitting on a wooden bench near the end of the path, looking out at the sea. Verrall had his dinner jacket off and draped over Nettie's shoulders. His white shirt was rumpled, and his hair was messy from sleeping on the grass.

Leadford walked up to the bench and stopped. Neither of them looked afraid.

"Good morning, Leadford," Verrall said. His voice was quiet, without the edge it usually had.

"Good morning," Leadford said.

Nettie looked up at him. Her eyes were clear, and the puffiness she had the night before was gone.

"Did you sleep out here, Will?"

"Yes," Leadford said. "On the grass."

"We all did," Verrall said. "Something came off the water. A kind of fog."

"I saw it," Leadford said. He sat down on the grass a few feet from the bench, hugging his knees. "I came down from Staffordshire with a gun to shoot you."

Verrall nodded slowly, looking out at the motionless ships. "I thought you might have something like that in your pocket. Last night I would have fought you for it."

"And now?" Leadford asked.

"Now it seems a long way off," Verrall said. "Like something that happened to someone else."

They sat quietly for a while. Down on the esplanade, a baker's cart went by, the horse's hooves clopping steadily on the stone. A gull wheeled over the cliff edge and cried once, sharply, before gliding down toward the sand.

"Are you hungry, Will?" Nettie asked.

"Yes," Leadford said. "I haven't eaten since Birmingham."

Verrall stood up and stretched his arms. "Come on, then," he said. "The hotel should have the fires lit by now. We can get some coffee and eggs."

Leadford stood up and fell into step beside them as they walked back down the path toward the town.
