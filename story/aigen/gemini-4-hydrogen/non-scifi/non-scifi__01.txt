The letter had no south-country postmark, only a smear of coal-dust where the postman’s thumb had pressed it, yet William knew before he broke the flap what it would say. He stood in the narrow scullery of his mother’s house in Clayton, the kettle hissing on the hob, while the paper trembled between his knuckles. Nettie was gone. She had taken her wicker trunk to the junction on Tuesday afternoon, and Edward Verrall—whose father owned half the clay-pits between here and Burslem—had met her in his motor-car.

Beyond the soot-blackened privet hedge, the bottle-ovens belched yellow smoke into the twilight, and above the haze, hanging low over the slag-heaps, a strange green star burned with an indifferent brilliance. His mother came in from the yard with a basin of potatoes, her face hollowed by forty years of washing-days. She looked at the paper, then at his mouth, which had gone white at the corners. She did not ask; in Staffordshire, people rarely asked when a blow fell. She set the basin on the deal table and turned to stir the coals.

William folded the letter into a tight square and slid it into his waistcoat pocket. The kitchen smelled of damp wool and boiled cabbage—the smell of his whole life, of his father’s slow dying, of the twenty-two shillings a week he earned keeping ledgers. Down in the lane, a newsboy shouted something about the German fleet, his shrill voice swallowed by a passing dray. William took his cap from the peg and went out into the cooling dark.

By Thursday, the whole valley was talking of war, though the men at the kilns spoke of it as they spoke of a bad winter or a strike—with a sullen, fatalistic pride. In the Red Lion, the colliers leaned over their pint pots, tracing maps of the North Sea in spilled beer, while the evening papers lay crumpled on the benches, black with headlines about mobilized reserves and cruisers steaming through the Channel.

William sat in the corner by the tap, saying nothing. He had spent his last sovereign that afternoon at a pawnbroker’s in Hanley, where an old, heavy-barrelled revolver had lain for months in a tray of tarnished watches and brass compasses. The weight of it in his coat pocket was the only solid thing left in the world. He thought of Nettie’s small, warm wrist, the way she used to tuck her chin into her collar when the wind came off the moor, and then he thought of Verrall’s smooth, sunburnt hands on the brass steering-wheel. It seemed to him that the whole country was spoiling for a fight because men were tired of being small and cramped and cheated, and if Europe was going to burn, he would not be left behind in the ashes.

When he walked home along the canal towpath, the water was dead calm, reflecting the furnaces on the bank and, higher up, that pale green smudge in the sky. The comet had grown a tail now, a faint, watery plume that stretched halfway toward the zenith, and a group of women stood by the lock-gate, whispering that it meant blood and famine. William walked past them with his head down, his hand closed tight around the iron butt in his pocket.

The third-class carriage was stifling, thick with the smell of cheap tobacco, sweat, and the plush upholstery that had held the dust of twenty summers. William sat wedged between a stout woman with a basket of plums and a young soldier in khaki who kept nervously adjusting his puttees. All the way down from Crewe, the train had been delayed on sidings to let troop-trains roar past, their windows crowded with shouting lads waving caps and handkerchiefs. At every station, the platforms were choked with people buying extra editions, their faces upturned for a moment toward the afternoon sky, where even through the glare of the August sun a faint, sickly greenness seemed to stain the blue.

William stared out at the changing country—the black slag and red brick of the Midlands giving way to fat Warwickshire pastures, then to chalk downs and tall, dusty elms. He felt no kinship with any of it. His serge suit was too thick for the heat, his collar chafed his neck, and the railway ticket he had bought to Shaphambury had left him with four shillings and some coppers. He had found the address on the back of an old envelope in Nettie’s mother’s parlor—a boarding-house near the cliffs, recommended by some cousin of Verrall’s.

All through the long, jolting afternoon, he rehearsed what he would say, though the words turned sour in his mouth. He did not want to argue. He wanted only to see her face when she saw him, to make her understand that she could not simply step into a motor-car and leave him behind in the dirt like a broken pot.

Shaphambury was all whitewash, geraniums, and the sharp, clean smell of salt water, a town built for people who had never seen a coal-pit in their lives. William walked from the station through streets lined with neat villas, his heavy boots ringing loud on the gravel paths, his Staffordshire accent making the landlady at the little lodging-house near the gasworks eye his bundle with suspicion. He did not unpack. As soon as dusk began to gather, he went up toward the cliffs where the great hotels stood behind their tamarisk hedges.

A band was playing on the pier below, a thin, brassy waltz that drifted up on the warm sea-breeze, and couples in light summer clothes strolled along the esplanade. He saw them near the steps of the Grand—Verrall in white flannels, leaning against the stone balustrade with a cigarette between his fingers, and Nettie beside him in a pale muslin dress he had never seen before. She was laughing at something Verrall had said, tilting her head back in that quick, bird-like way that had once made William’s heart turn over in the damp lanes of Clayton. A sudden, blind fury rose in his throat, bitter as gall.

Above the dark line of the sea, the comet hung immense now, its greenish-yellow light washing the white cliffs and the faces of the promenaders in the hue of tarnished silver. People were pointing at it from the pier, their voices rising in an uneasy murmur, but Nettie and Verrall did not look up. They turned away from the lights and began to walk along the footpath that led out onto the dark, open downs. William waited until they were fifty yards ahead, then followed.

The grass on the cliff-top was dry and slippery, smelling of wild thyme and the sea. Half a mile from the town, where the path dipped into a hollow behind a clump of gorse, they stopped to look back at the bay. William came up over the rise, his breath rasping in his chest, and stepped into the path before them.

Nettie gave a small, sharp cry and caught at Verrall’s arm. For a second nobody spoke; the green light from the sky fell across their faces, making Verrall look hollow-eyed and pale, and turning Nettie’s muslin dress the color of sea-foam.

"William," she whispered, her voice trembling. "What are you doing here?"

He drew the revolver from his pocket, though his hand shook so violently the barrel dipped toward the turf. Verrall stepped in front of her, his jaw set, trying to keep the gentlemanly ease in his voice even as his eyes fixed on the iron. "Put that down, Leadford. Don't be a fool."

William tried to speak, to tell them of the ledger-room, of the soot, of the long, empty nights, but his throat was locked. He raised the pistol. Just then, the sea-breeze dropped dead. A strange, heavy stillness settled over the downs, as if a glass bell had been lowered over the world. The air grew suddenly sweet, carrying a faint, sharp scent like bruised laurel leaves or rain on hot slate. William felt his knees loosen. The anger seemed to drain out of his arms like water from a cracked jug. The pistol slipped from his fingers into the grass, and before he could stoop for it, a profound, dreamless sleep took him, and he sank down beside the path.

When William woke, the sun was well up, and a lark was singing high in the washed blue sky. His serge coat was stiff with dew, and his neck ached from the turf. He lay still for a moment, listening to the tide against the chalk below, feeling as though a long fever had finally broken.

He sat up. A few yards away, the revolver lay half-hidden in the clover, looking small and rusty and absurd. Beyond it, Verrall sat with his knees drawn up, rubbing his eyes like a sleepy boy, while Nettie stood near the gorse bush, looking out to sea with her hair blown loose about her shoulders. Neither of them looked like enemies; they looked only bewildered, like people who had survived a shipwreck. William got slowly to his feet and brushed the dry grass from his trousers. When Nettie turned, she did not flinch, nor did Verrall rise.

"Are you hurt, William?" she asked quietly. Her voice sounded smaller in the morning air, and wholly without fear.

He looked at her—not as the prize he had been robbed of, nor the dream that had kept him warm in Clayton, but simply as a young woman who had wanted a wider sky than he could give her. The thought brought a dull, clean ache, yet there was no poison in it. Down in the town, a church bell began to ring, steady and unhurried, and the memory of the shouting crowds and the war headlines seemed as distant as a bad dream.

"No," William said, his voice rough from the night air. "I'm not hurt."

He picked up his cap, looked once more at the two of them in the bright, quiet morning, and turned back toward the station.
