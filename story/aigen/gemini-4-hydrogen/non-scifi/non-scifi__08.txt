The smoke of the Five Towns never truly lifted; it merely thinned at dusk to let the new star show through. William Leadford stood by the cinder-path behind the pottery, his boots grey with marl, and watched that pale, greenish smear hang above the bottle-ovens. In the taprooms down in Clayton, men argued whether it was an omen of the German war, or merely another trick of the astronomers to frighten the poor. William cared nothing for the astronomers, and scarcely more for the Kaiser’s gunboats in the North Sea. His mind was a small, hot room, and in it sat Nettie, smiling at another man.

She had been gone three days. No note for him—only a whispered word to her sister about the Dorset coast, and the sight of young Edward Verrall’s motor-car turning out of the high road with its brass lamps blazing. Verrall, whose father owned half the pits in the valley and took his rent in coughs and crooked backs. William’s fingers tightened on the folded newspaper in his pocket. Its columns were black with mobilization orders and the price of steam-coal, yet all he saw was the curve of Nettie’s throat as she had leaned over the churchyard wall last Sunday, already half-stolen from him.

Inside the cottage, his mother was raking the grate, her breathing a thin, wet rattle. He could not bear her pity, nor the smell of boiled cabbage and damp plaster that clung to his skin. That afternoon he had drawn his savings from the post office, bought a cheap Belgian revolver in Burslem, and packed a single shirt. Tonight he would take the mail train south.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet worsted, stale shag, and the sour sweat of men who worked too many hours for too little bread. William sat wedged by the window, his knees knocking against a traveller’s sample case, while the Midlands slipped past in a blur of black hedges and red furnace-glow. Above it all, even through the soot-filmed glass, the comet kept pace with the train, its tail broadening night by night like spilled luminous paint.

Opposite him, two clerks in celluloid collars were loudly settling the fate of Europe.

"If the fleet doesn't strike by Thursday," said the taller one, tapping his paper, "we’ll have the Prussians in Kent before harvest. The government’s too soft."

"Let them come," muttered a navvy in the corner, spitting between his boots. "A man can’t starve twice."

William kept his hand deep in his pocket, his thumb tracing the checkered grip of the pistol. He felt a cold detachment from their chatter. What did it matter if empires tore each other to pieces? His own small empire had already been sacked. He thought of Verrall’s smooth, sunburnt hands, his easy Cambridge laugh, the way he tossed silver to crossing-sweepers as if money were bright pebbles. Nettie had always loved brightness; she hated the grease on William’s cuffs, the ink-stains on his fingers from his dreary clerkship at the ironworks. She wanted silk, and sea-air, and a man who did not smell of coal-smoke. Well, let her have them for a day or two. William watched the dark fields rush by, nursing his grievance like a banked fire.

By noon the next day, he was walking the esplanade of a white-stuccoed watering-place on the south coast, where the sea lay flat and pewter-colored under a brassy haze. He looked absurd here, and knew it. His serge suit was shiny at the elbows, his collar wilted from the journey, and his boots were still caked with Staffordshire clay. Around him strolled ladies under lace parasols and gentlemen in cream flannel, their voices unhurried, though every newsboard outside the tobacconists screamed of ultimatums and recalled reserves.

He found them at four o’clock, not hiding in some shuttered hotel, but sitting openly on the terrace of the Grand Pavilion, where a string band played a Viennese waltz. Nettie wore a new hat trimmed with cornflowers; she was leaning forward, chin in palm, laughing at something Verrall had said. The sight struck William behind the knees like a blow from a stave. She did not look ruined or penitent. She looked radiant, as though she had shed her old life in the Potteries as easily as a soiled apron.

When they rose and wandered toward the chalk cliffs west of the town, William followed at fifty paces, keeping to the shadow of the tamarisk hedges. The afternoon was growing unnaturally dim. The sun had sunk into a bank of livid, greenish cloud, and the seabirds had stopped crying, circling low over the shingle with a bewildered beat of wings. Down in the town, a bell began to toll, then another, and a faint roar of voices drifted up from the promenade—whether in panic or patriotic fervor, William could not tell.

He overtook them on the high turf above the bay, where the gorse grew stunted from the salt wind. The sky was no longer blue or grey; it was a vast, bruised dome of pale emerald, and the comet hung so low and wide it seemed a man might reach up and touch its cold, fibrous light.

"Nettie!"

She spun round with a gasp, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall stepped instinctively before her, his jaw hardening as he recognized the intruder, though his eyes flickered toward the luminous horror overhead.

"Good God, Leadford," Verrall said, trying for his usual cool authority and missing it. "What are you doing here?"

"Stand aside," William said. His voice sounded thin in the breathless air. He drew the revolver. The metal looked dull and foolish in the green twilight, yet his heart hammered with a fierce joy. At last, they were looking at him. At last, the smallness of his life had grown large enough to frighten them.

Below, along the esplanade, tiny figures were running; a distant bugle blew three sharp notes, broken off abruptly. War had come, or the end of the world, and William did not care which. He raised the barrel toward Verrall’s linen waistcoat.

Then the air changed. It came rolling in from the Channel like a quiet tide—a sweet, sharp vapor, smelling of crushed mint and summer rain after thunder. Nettie gave a soft sigh and sank to her knees. Verrall staggered, clutching his collar, his face slackening into sudden wonder. William tried to squeeze the trigger, to hold fast to his hatred, but his fingers would not obey. A cool heaviness pressed against his eyelids, and he pitched forward into the gorse.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world was terribly still.

He lay on his side in the damp grass, his cheek against a tuft of wild thyme. For a long moment he did not remember where he was, or why he had come; he only knew that the tight, hot knot behind his ribs—woven of poverty, envy, and wounded pride—had come entirely undone. He sat up slowly. The sky was a clear, washed blue, empty of any comet, empty even of smoke. Down in the harbor, the fishing smacks rode at anchor without a soul on their decks, and the white houses of the town gleamed with a sharp distinctness, as if seen through freshly polished glass.

Beside his boot lay the Belgian revolver, beaded with dew. He picked it up, turned it over in his palm, and felt a sudden rush of shame—not the defensive shame of the poor man caught out, but a quiet, grieving pity for the creature he had been yesterday. How small that anger looked in the open morning! He tossed the pistol over the cliff’s edge and listened to it clatter down the chalk into the sea.

Ten paces away, Nettie was stirring. She pushed herself up on one elbow, her cornflower hat crushed beneath her, and looked at him with wide, bewildered eyes. There was no coquetry left in her face, no bright defiance—only the pale, honest features of the girl he had known when they were children gathering blackberries along the canal.

Verrall woke a moment later, sitting up abruptly and staring at his own hands as though he had never seen them before. He looked at William, then at Nettie, and passed a trembling palm over his forehead.

"I had the strangest dream," Verrall murmured, his voice stripped of its patronizing drawl. "That we were all shouting... about ships, and coal, and killing people." He paused, looking down at the silent town. "Leadford. You came to shoot me."

"Yes," William said simply. There seemed no use in lying, and no sting in the truth. "I did."

Verrall nodded slowly, without anger. "I suppose I gave you reason enough. Though I scarcely know why I brought her here, except that I wanted to prove I could take what I liked." He glanced at Nettie, his face reddening with an unaccustomed humility. "I'm sorry, Nettie. That was a poor thing to do."

Nettie drew her knees up, wrapping her arms around them. Tears stood in her eyes, yet she looked steadier than William had ever seen her. "We were all blind," she whispered. "Scrambling after things we didn't even want."

A gull wheeled overhead, its cry clean in the salt air. Down on the promenade, figures were beginning to move—shopkeepers in doorways, soldiers in unbuttoned tunics wandering into the street, all looking up at the empty sky with the dazed expressions of men waking from a long fever. Across the water, the newspapers declaring war would already be blowing into the gutters, their ink meaningless.

William stood, brushed the grass from his trousers, and held out his hand to Nettie, then to Verrall. Together, the three of them turned toward the waking town, uncertain of what waited below, yet breathing the clear air as if for the first time.
