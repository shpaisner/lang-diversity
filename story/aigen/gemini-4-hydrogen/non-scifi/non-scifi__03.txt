William Leadford came home from the counting-house with the taste of coal-smoke in his throat and the ink-stain fresh on his middle finger. All down the terrace, the red brick houses stood shoulder to shoulder against the drizzle, their slate roofs shining like the backs of wet beetles. Inside, his mother had set the lamp on the oilcloth, and the kitchen smelled of damp wool and boiled mutton.

He did not take off his coat. He stood by the dresser, reading the note a third time, though the words had already burned themselves into his eyes. Nettie had gone. Not to her aunt’s in Wolverhampton, as she had told her father, but south, with Edward Verrall. The paper said nothing of Verrall, of course—only that she was sorry, that William must not think harshly of her, that she could not bear the greyness any longer.

"She’s a light thing, Will," his mother said, not looking up from her darning. "You’re better shut of her."

"Hold your tongue," William muttered. He went out into the tiny bricked yard, where the cabbages rotted in the soot. Above the silhouette of the pottery kilns and the great slag-heap beyond the canal, the sky had cleared to a hard, wintry violet. Hanging there, low over the chimney-pots, was the green star. It had grown brighter this past fortnight—no longer a mere smudge of mist, but a pale, trailing plume that made the familiar black ridges of Staffordshire look foreign. William stared at it, his fists clenched in his pockets. He hated Verrall’s white hands, his soft Norfolk jacket, the easy way he tossed coins to the gatekeeper. A man with two hundred a year had no right to steal what a man with twenty shillings a week had built his whole life around.

The next afternoon, Clayton was full of noise. Down by the market square, a crowd of colliers out on strike had gathered outside the newsagent’s window, where the latest placards hung limp in the damp air. ULTIMATUM, the black letters shouted. MOBILIZATION. Men in caps and mufflers argued with a fierce, hungry energy, their faces hollowed by six weeks without wages. Someone spoke of the German fleet in the North Sea; another swore it was all a trick of the masters to break the union.

William pushed past them without a word. What did he care for Belgium or the Kaiser? His own war had been declared in a parlor smelling of cabbage. He turned down the narrow alley behind the church and stopped before old Moss’s window, where trays of tarnished spoons and brass compasses lay behind fly-specked glass.

When he emerged ten minutes later, his pocket hung heavy against his thigh. The revolver was a cheap Belgian thing, its nickel plating worn thin along the barrel, and the six cartridges in his waistcoat felt like smooth, cold pebbles. He walked to the station with his collar turned up, though the rain had stopped. His mother had given him nothing for the journey, so he had taken four sovereigns from the tea-caddy behind the clock—the money saved for his winter boots and the doctor’s bill. He felt a sharp, bitter pang of shame as he slid two of them under the grille for a third-class return to Shaphambury, yet the shame only fed his anger. If Nettie wanted the south, she should see what followed her from the north.

The train south was crowded with reservists, young men in cheap serge suits who laughed too loudly and passed bottles of beer from hand to hand. In the corner of the carriage, an old woman in a black bonnet sat weeping quietly into a cotton handkerchief, while her son stared rigidly at the luggage rack, his jaw set.

William sat by the window, his knees pressed against a kitbag, watching England change. The blackened hedges and stunted thorns of the Midlands gave way to broad, fat pastures, to red-tiled barns and orchards heavy with unripe apples. Everything here looked softer, as if the earth itself had never been scraped raw for coal or iron. It sickened him. He thought of Nettie walking through these gentle lanes on Verrall’s arm, forgetting the soot on the sills, forgetting the Sunday evenings when they had walked by the canal and she had let him hold her gloveless hand.

As twilight thickened, the carriage grew quiet. Even the soldiers fell silent, watching the sky beyond the glass. The comet had risen early, a great greenish-white fan spreading across the eastern heavens, so luminous that it dimmed the first stars. Its light fell across the fields and the distant chalk downs, draining the warmth from the grass and turning the faces of the sleeping men in the compartment to the color of tallow. Someone muttered that it was an ill omen, coming alongside the war. William only pressed his hand against his coat pocket, feeling the hard outline of the iron frame, and waited for the sea.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, tar, and lavender. Even at noon, the whitewashed fronts of the lodging-houses along the esplanade glared in the pale sun, and the shingle beach hissed softly as the tide drew back. William, walking the promenade in his shiny Sunday serge, felt the eyes of the nursemaids and the retired colonels upon him. He was a smudge of coal-dust on a clean linen cloth, and he knew it.

He found them on the second day. He had been loitering near the tea-rooms by the pier, his boots dusty and his stomach hollow from eating nothing but dry bread, when he heard her laugh. It was a light, breathless sound he had not heard since May.

They were coming down the steps from the cliff-gardens. Verrall wore white flannel trousers and a straw hat tilted back from his forehead; he carried a rolled-up newspaper under one arm—no doubt full of the impending slaughter across the Channel, though he looked as untroubled as a boy on holiday. Beside him, Nettie wore a pale muslin dress with blue ribbons at the waist, and a wide hat that shadowed her eyes. She looked up at Verrall as he spoke, her hand resting lightly on his sleeve.

William stepped behind a wooden bathing-machine until they passed. The sight of that blue ribbon—something she had never worn for him, something bought with Verrall’s effortless money—turned his blood to vinegar. He did not rush out. A strange, cold patience had settled over him. He followed them at a distance as they turned away from the town, taking the narrow chalk path that climbed toward the lonely downs above the bay.

On the headland, the sun had dropped behind the hills, and the wind had died away. A curious stillness lay over the coast. The sea below was flat as a slate, and the air had grown thick and heavy, carrying a faint, sweetish scent like crushed almonds or wild thyme after rain.

Above the dark rim of the Channel, the comet filled half the sky. It was no longer a distant star, but a vast, glowing veil of pale emerald mist that seemed to brush the very tops of the cliffs, turning the white chalk to bone.

William stepped out from behind a stone wall. Nettie saw him first. She stopped dead, her hand flying to her throat, and the color drained from her face.

"Will!" she whispered.

Verrall turned sharply, stepping in front of her. "Leadford? What in God’s name are you doing here?" His voice held that crisp, quiet authority William had hated all his life—the master’s son speaking to a trespassing clerk.

"Stand aside, Verrall," William said, drawing the revolver. His fingers were damp with sweat.

"Put that down, you fool," Verrall said, not flinching. "Have you gone mad?"

"You take everything—the land, the pits, the very air—and now you take her." William raised his arm, the barrel wavering. "You think I’ll crawl back to the dirt and let you laugh?"

As he drew breath to steady his aim, the sweet vapor in the air rushed into his lungs, heavy and warm. A sudden dizziness washed over him, loosening the tight iron band around his chest. His knees buckled. He heard Nettie cry out, saw Verrall stagger sideways as if struck by a wave, and then the chalk path tilted upward to meet his cheek, and the green sky closed over his eyes.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up. A lark was singing somewhere high above the downs, a clear, insistent thread of sound against the quiet wash of the sea. The green mist was gone. The sky had never looked so vast or so cleanly washed—a pale, delicate blue from the horizon to the zenith, with only a faint smear of white where the comet had passed.

He sat up slowly, his hand brushing the damp grass. Beside him lay the Belgian revolver, its barrel spotted with dew. He looked at it, feeling neither shame nor triumph, only a quiet bewilderment, as if he had woken from a long illness and found a stranger’s clothes upon his chair. All the tight, bitter knots inside him—the counting-house humiliations, the soot, the fierce, possessive ache for Nettie—had dissolved like salt in water.

A few paces away, Nettie was sitting up on the turf, her hat crushed beneath her, her dark hair falling loose about her shoulders. She looked out over the Channel, her eyes wide and clear. Beside her, Verrall sat with his arms wrapped around his knees, his white flannels stained with grass and chalk.

None of them spoke. There was no need for the old words, nor for the pride that had armed them yesterday. When Verrall finally turned his head, the mask of easy superiority was gone from his face; he looked simply like a young man, bewildered and gentle in the morning light.

"Are you hurt, Leadford?" he asked quietly.

"No," William said. He rose, brushing the chalk from his coat, and walked over to them. Nettie looked up at him without terror, only a deep, quiet pity for them all. Down in the town, a church bell began to toll, slow and peaceful across the sunlit roofs, as if the world itself had woken from a dark and feverish dream.
