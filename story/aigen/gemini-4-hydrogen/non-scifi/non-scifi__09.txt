William Leadford sat on the edge of his narrow iron bedstead, listening to his mother’s dry, persistent cough in the kitchen below. Through the small casement window, the Staffordshire dusk was settling over the pot-banks, thick with coal-smoke and the sour smell of wet clay. Far down the valley, the bottle kilns of Burslem glowed like banked furnaces, throwing a dull, coppery stain against the low clouds. Above that stain, in a rift of pale sky, hung the comet—a smudge of greenish light, like verdigris on an old penny.

On the chest of drawers lay Nettie’s note, folded twice, its cheap blue paper already greased by the sweat of his thumbs. Do not look for me, Willie. I am going with Edward. Three days had passed since he found it, and the words had ceased to sting; instead, they had hardened inside him into a cold, heavy knot beneath his ribs. He was nineteen shillings a week at Rawdon’s counting-house, a narrow-chested clerk with ink on his cuffs and coal-dust in the creases of his neck. Edward Verrall was the young gentleman from Checkshill Towers, who had horses to ride and nothing to do all summer but loiter by the lodge gates until a gardener’s daughter looked up from her sewing.

Willie reached into his coat pocket and closed his fingers around the cold, ridged grip of the Belgian revolver he had bought that afternoon in Hanley. It had cost him his savings, every shilling he had put by for a parlor suite and a wedding. He stood up, his boots scraping on the bare boards, and went down the steep stairs without saying goodbye.

The third-class carriage smelled of damp wool, stale shag, and the soot that blew in whenever the train slowed for a junction. Willie sat in the corner, his knees pressed against the opposite bench, watching the Midlands slip away into the gathering night. Across from him, two commercial travelers in bowler hats were rattling a folded newspaper between them, their voices raised over the clatter of the wheels.

"The ultimatum’s expired," the stout one declared, tapping the print with a thick forefinger. "They’ve got the North Sea Fleet steaming out of Portland before midnight. Mark my words, we’ll have the Germans in the Channel by Friday."

"Let them come," the other muttered, peering out at the dark hedgerows. "We’ve waited long enough for a reckoning."

Willie turned his face to the dirty glass. What did their fleet or their Germans matter to him? Let the whole Empire burn down to ash and clinker; his own small world had already foundered in the ditch behind Checkshill Park. He thought of Nettie’s dark hair, the quick, nervous way she tucked a stray lock behind her ear when she was pleased, and the thought made his throat ache with a dry, murderous bitterness.

As the train rattled south-east through the sleeping shires, the green star seemed to follow them. It had grown brighter since the evening before, its faint, trailing mist stretching halfway across the constellations. Even the travelers fell silent for a moment, glancing sideways at the strange pallor it cast over the passing fields, before returning to their papers and their war.

By noon the next day, Willie was walking the white, chalk-dusted promenade of Shaphambury. The sea air was sharp with salt and weed, cutting cleanly through his thin serge coat, and the houses here were washed in cream and pale yellow, untouched by the grime of the Potteries. Yet even in this quiet watering-place, the fever of the mainland had arrived. Newsboys ran along the esplanade crying special editions, and clusters of holidaymakers in straw boaters stood beside the railings, looking out toward the horizon as if they expected to see ironclads rising through the haze.

Willie kept his right hand buried in his pocket, his fingers never leaving the iron of the pistol. He had walked four miles from the station, and his cheap boots were grey with dust. He felt conspicuous and shabby among the summer dresses and flannel suits, a creature crawled up from the marl-pits into the sun.

Then, near the steps leading down to the cliff-path, he saw them. Verrall was wearing a loose suit of cream flannel, a panama tilted back on his fair head; Nettie walked beside him in a pale blue muslin frock Willie had never seen before. She was laughing at something Verrall had said, looking up at him with that quick, breathless tilt of her chin—the very look she used to give Willie under the hawthorns at home. The sight turned his stomach to vinegar. He stepped back behind a clump of tamarisk, his heart hammering against his ribs, and watched them turn toward the quieter lanes above the bay.

Dusk did not bring darkness that evening, only a deeper, more unnatural greenness. The comet now filled a vast wedge of the sky, a silent fan of cold emerald fire that turned the whitewashed villas the color of river-mud and made the sea look like oiled slate. People had come out onto their verandas to stare at it, speaking in hushed, uncertain voices.

Willie waited in the shadow of a brick garden wall along the lane behind their boarding-house. His feet were blistered, and he had eaten nothing since morning, yet his mind was remarkably clear. When he heard the crunch of gravel and the easy, clipped cadence of Verrall’s voice, he stepped out into the middle of the path.

"That’s far enough," Willie said.

Nettie gave a sharp little cry and caught at Verrall’s sleeve. Verrall stopped, squinting through the eerie twilight, before recognition hardened his face.

"Leadford," he said, his voice dropping an octave, cool and measured. "What in God’s name are you doing down here? Have you lost your wits?"

Willie drew the revolver from his coat. His hand was shaking, and he hated himself for it, hated the thin, Potteries whine in his own voice as he answered. "You’ve taken what wasn’t yours, Mr. Verrall. You’ve plenty of everything up at the Towers—horses, land, money to burn. You couldn’t leave me the one thing I had."

Verrall gently disengaged Nettie’s fingers and stepped half a pace in front of her, though his face had gone the color of chalk in the green glare. "Put that down, you fool," he said. "Nettie came of her own free will."

"Willie, please—" Nettie’s voice broke, thin and terrified.

Before Willie could answer, a dull, heavy thudding vibrated through the soles of their boots. Far out beyond the headland, muffled by miles of water, the heavy guns had begun to speak. A second rumble followed, and a third, shaking the leaves of the tamarisk bushes along the wall. Somewhere out in that green-lit dark, men were drowning in iron hulls.

Willie’s finger tightened on the trigger. He wanted the sharp, honest crack of the shot to tear through Verrall’s calm, to end this miserable tangle of pride and humiliation once and for all.

Instead, a sudden, inexplicable languor seemed to drop out of the sky. A faint scent drifted up from the sea—not of salt or coal-smoke, but something sweet and sharp, like bruised sweetbriar and rain-wet earth. The green light thickened around them into a palpable, luminous mist that clung to their eyelashes and cooled their throats as they breathed it in.

Willie tried to raise his arm, to steady his aim, but his shoulder felt as heavy as lead. Across from him, Verrall swayed slightly, reaching out a hand to steady himself against the brick wall. Nettie did not scream; she simply sank down onto her knees on the gravel, her head drooping forward as if overcome by an immense, gentle fatigue. Willie’s own knees gave way. The revolver slipped from his slack fingers, clattering harmlessly against a flint, and he rolled onto the damp verge beside the path as a soft, dreamless dark closed over his eyes.

A lark was singing somewhere high above the downs, its notes falling through the morning air like clear water. Willie woke on the damp grass beside the lane. For a long moment he lay still, staring up into a sky of vast, pale blue, washed clean as linen after rain. There was no trace of the green star, nor any smear of smoke on the horizon.

He sat up, brushing a dry leaf from his sleeve, and felt an astonishing lightness in his chest, as though a lifelong congestion of soot and bile had been washed away while he slept. Beside his boot lay the little black revolver, its barrel already dulled with a film of morning dew. He looked at it with a quiet, distant wonder, unable to fathom the dark, cramped fever that had driven him to buy it, or to carry it across England in his pocket.

A few yards away, Verrall was sitting against the garden wall, rubbing his eyes with the back of his wrist, his cream flannels creased and stained with earth. Nettie stirred beside him, her straw hat crushed beneath her elbow. When she looked up and saw Willie, there was no terror in her face, and no anger—only a bewildered, solemn stillness, as though they were three strangers who had been washed ashore together after a long storm.

Down in the bay, the sea lay empty and silver under the sun; no guns sounded from the Channel, and no flags flew from the harbor masts. Willie stood up, shook the chalk dust from his trousers, and walked slowly across the gravel toward them, not to claim or to accuse, but simply to ask if she was cold.
