The smoke of Clayton hung low over the red-brick terraces, tasting of sulfur and damp coal, as William Leadford sat at the oilcloth table and read Nettie’s letter for the fourth time. It was written on thick, cream-colored paper—paper she could never have bought in Staffordshire—and the ink was neat and unhurried. She was sorry, she wrote. She had not meant to deceive him, but Mr. Verrall had asked her to go away with him to the coast, and she had said yes. She hoped William would find some peace.

Peace. William folded the sheet until its edges cut into his thumb. Downstairs, the kitchen clock ticked with a dry rattle, and through the window came the cry of a newsboy trotting along the cobbles, shouting about the German fleet and the Balkans. Nobody in the street cared much about the Balkans, yet men stood at their garden gates in their shirtsleeves, staring upward. Above the squat bottle-kilns, the evening sky had taken on a bruised pallor. The comet, which the newspapers had mocked all spring, now hung over the Midlands like a smear of greenish phosphorus, dimming the stars.

William stood up, his boots scraping the bare boards. He was twenty-four, narrow-shouldered, with coal dust lodged in the creases of his knuckles, and for three years his only wealth had been the belief that Nettie Stuart belonged to him. That afternoon he had drawn his savings from the post office—four pounds and eleven shillings—and walked to a pawnshop in Burslem, trading half of it for a heavy revolver and six cartridges in greasy paper. He slipped the pistol into his coat pocket, feeling its cold weight against his hip, and went out into the soot-laden dusk.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool, stale shag tobacco, and the sour breath of tired men. William sat wedged in the corner, his knees pressed tight together so as not to touch the stout commercial traveler opposite him, who was slapping a folded newspaper against his thigh.

"Mark my words," the traveler said to a clerk beside him, his voice thick with port and certainty. "The Kaiser’s only waiting for the harvest. By Friday the Channel will be thick with cruisers, and we’ll be calling up the reserves."

"It’s that star," the clerk muttered, glancing sideways at the blackened glass. "My wife says the parsons are praying against it in Manchester."

"Nonsense," the traveler barked. "Gases and ice, nothing more. It’s the Germans we’ve got to watch."

William said nothing. He kept his hand in his coat pocket, his thumb tracing the cylinder of the revolver. Outside, the dark hedgerows of Warwickshire gave way to the Thames valley, and then to the pale chalk of the southern downs. Every station they passed was crowded with young men in caps, laughing too loudly as they bought late editions, their faces washed in the eerie, sea-green light of the gas lamps and the thing overhead. By the time the train shuddered to a halt at the seaside terminus, it was past midnight. William stepped down onto the platform, his cheap boots pinching, and walked toward the front. The air here was salt-sharp, yet strangely heavy, as if a thunderstorm were waiting to break. Out over the dark water, the comet’s tail spread like a luminous fan, turning the whitewashed lodging houses the color of old bone.

He slept for two hours in a dry ditch behind a railway embankment, waking with dew soaking his trouser knees and a dull ache behind his eyes. When he walked back into the town, the morning sun was already high, though it looked oddly pale, like a lemon floating in milk.

Shaphambury was built for people who did not work with their hands. Along the promenade, gentlemen in light flannel suits strolled with silver-topped canes, and ladies beneath lace parasols paused to admire the scarlet geraniums. Even here, the talk was all of war; knots of men gathered outside the tobacconists’ shops, pointing at the chalked placards, their voices brisk and proprietary. William, unshaven and gray-faced in his stained Staffordshire tweed, felt their eyes slide over him with a mild, automatic distaste. He bought a penny bun from a cart and ate it sitting on a cast-iron bench, watching the bathing machines wheeled into the surf.

Past noon, he found them. He had wandered up the steep lane behind the grand hotel, where the tidy villas gave way to open turf and gorse, and there they were, walking along the cliff path. Edward Verrall was taller than William had remembered, with a careless swing to his shoulders and a straw boater tilted back from his forehead. Beside him, Nettie wore a dress of pale blue muslin. She was laughing at something Verrall had said, her hand resting lightly on his arm. The sight of her white wrist against his dark sleeve made William’s throat close with a bitter, metallic taste. He stepped back behind a clump of tamarisk and followed them at a distance.

By dusk, the wind had died altogether. The sea below the cliffs lay flat and oily, reflecting the monstrous green glare that filled half the northern sky. A peculiar stillness had settled over the downs; no gulls cried, and the brass band on the pier had stopped playing an hour ago.

William caught up with them where the path narrowed between a stone wall and a drop of chalk. Nettie heard his boots on the flint and turned first. The color drained from her face, leaving her eyes dark in the green twilight.

"Will," she breathed, her hand flying to her throat.

Verrall stepped smoothly in front of her, his jaw tightening with the annoyance of a man interrupted in his leisure. "What is the meaning of this, Leadford? You’ve no business following us here."

William did not answer. He pulled the revolver from his pocket. His arm felt heavy as lead, and his hand trembled—not from fear, but from twenty-four years of small wages, cold grates, and watching men like Verrall take whatever they fancied by birthright.

"Stand aside, Mr. Verrall," William said, his voice cracking. "I’ve come for Nettie."

"Don’t be a fool, man," Verrall snapped, taking a half-step back, his eyes fixed on the barrel. "If it’s money you’re after—"

"Will, please!" Nettie cried, trying to push past Verrall’s arm.

Before William could pull back the hammer, a strange breath of air rolled up from the sea, cool and sweet, smelling of bruised mint and wet clover. The green light thickened until the path vanished. A sudden drowsiness struck William behind the knees; his anger slipped away like a dropped coat, and as he slumped onto the grass, he heard Verrall fall beside him.

Light woke him—not the harsh glare of midday, nor the feverish emerald of the night before, but a pale, washed morning that smelled of rain-wet grass and salt. William lay still for a long time, his cheek pressed against a tuft of wild thyme. The first thing he noticed was the silence inside his own head. For years, his thoughts had been like a cramped, smoky kitchen where a kettle was always boiling over—full of grievances, calculations, and the sour ache of wanting what he could not reach. Now, the room was empty, and the windows stood wide open.

He pushed himself up on one elbow. Five paces away, Edward Verrall was sitting up on the turf, blinking at the horizon with the bewildered, unguarded face of a boy woken too early. His straw hat lay crushed beneath his hip, and a smudge of chalk dust streaked his cheek. Between them, Nettie knelt in the damp clover, her hair unpinned and falling over her shoulders, looking down at her own open hands as if she had never seen them before.

No one spoke. Below the cliff, the tide was coming in with a slow, rhythmic hiss over the shingle. Farther along the coast, a church bell began to ring—not an alarm, nor a summons to pray against the sky, but a plain, unhurried tolling that carried clearly through the motionless air. William looked down at the nickel-plated revolver lying near his boot. In the clean morning light, it looked absurd, like a rusted toy dropped by a child. He picked it up by the barrel and tossed it gently over the edge of the chalk into the nettles below.

Verrall watched the pistol disappear, then looked across the grass at William. The easy, careless superiority had gone out of his face; he looked merely tired, and very young, stripped of the invisible armor his money had always lent him.

"I shouldn’t have spoken to you like that last night," Verrall said quietly, his voice lower, without the sharp edge of command. "I didn't think of you at all, Leadford. Only of what I wanted."

William nodded slowly. "I wanted to kill you," he said, and there was no heat in the words, only a quiet wonder that such a thing had been inside him hours ago. "I thought if I couldn't have her, nobody should. As if she were a piece of land."

Nettie looked up at them both, her eyes clear and unafraid. "I ran away because I was frightened of being poor all my life, Will," she said, her voice steady in the sea breeze. "I saw my mother's hands, and I couldn't bear it. With Edward, everything seemed easy. I didn't love him as he thought, and I didn't love you as you wanted me to."

William looked at her—really looked at her—not as a prize to be guarded, nor as an escape from Clayton, but as a woman sitting on the grass in a crumpled blue dress, trying to tell the truth. The old, possessive agony did not return; in its place was an aching tenderness that asked for nothing back. Below them, the town was stirring, and a fishing smack put out into the silver water. Whatever madness had driven the nations toward the guns yesterday seemed, in this clear morning, as distant as a bad dream. William stood and held out his hand to Nettie, and the three of them walked together down the path toward the town.
