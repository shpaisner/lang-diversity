The kitchen smelled of boiled cabbage and his mother’s damp woollens, as it always did on Thursdays, though the grate had gone cold an hour since. William sat with his elbows on the oilcloth, staring at the torn envelope beside his teacup. She had written it in pencil, on a sheet of cheap ruled paper, the loops of her g’s and y’s slanting hurriedly toward the margin as if she were already listening for the trap on the lane. Do not think too badly of me, Will. As if a man could choose what to think when the blood was hammering behind his eyes like a steam-stamp in the foundry.

Through the small, soot-speckled pane, the bottle kilns of Swathinglea stood black against a bruised twilight. Above them, where the smoke thinned toward the zenith, hung the new star—no larger than a sixpence, yet shedding a sickly, verdigris light that made the slate roofs look as though they had been washed in brine. His mother had crossed herself when she first saw it from the scullery door, muttering of plagues and the Kaiser’s ships. William had merely cursed it. What did a smudge in the sky matter beside the fact that Nettie was gone, and gone with Edward Verrall?

Verrall, with his soft hands and his Cambridge drawl, his Norfolk jackets that smelled of heather and expensive tobacco. A gentleman’s son who had come down to the estate for the shooting and lingered for the sport of ruining a clerk’s girl. William pushed back his chair, the legs scraping harshly on the flagstones. He had four pounds ten in his tin box under the floorboards, and tomorrow was Friday.

By noon the next day, the whole High Street was clotted with men in caps and greasy waistcoats, clustered like flies around the placards outside the Sentinel office. ULTIMATUM, the black letters shouted. RESERVISTS CALLED TO THE COLOURS. A collier with coal-dust still lodged in the creases of his neck was reading aloud to a circle of pottery girls, his voice thick with beer and bellicose pride. They were going to thrash the Germans before Michaelmas; the fleet was already steaming into the North Sea.

William shouldered through them without a word. Their war seemed to him a noisy, theatrical thing, a drum-beating distraction for men who had nothing real to lose. His own war was quiet and sat heavy in his coat pocket. He had spent two of his sovereigns in a cramped pawnbroker’s behind the canal, where an old man with rheumy eyes had slid a dull-nickel Belgian revolver across the counter without asking a single question, only wiping his spectacles and remarking that the world had gone mad for iron.

At the railway booking-office, the clerk was too flustered by the telegraph bells to notice William’s white face or the tremor in his fingers as he counted out the silver for a third-class single to Shaphambury. Everyone was travelling, or talking of travelling; the platform was choked with soldiers’ kits and weeping mothers. Yet when William leaned out of the carriage window as the train pulled past the slag heaps, it was not the troop trains on the siding that held his eye. It was the sky above the pit-head, already tinged at three in the afternoon with that pale, unnatural green, as though a vast copper lamp had been lit behind the clouds.

The journey south took all the remaining daylight, and with every mile the country changed its coat. The blackened brick and sour, treeless pastures of Staffordshire gave way to fat Warwickshire elms, then to the pale chalk downs and pinewoods of the coast, all of it swimming in a strange, aquatic dusk. Even before the sun had properly set, the thing in the sky had swollen to a great fan of watery emerald, spilling across half the southern heavens.

In William’s compartment sat a commercial traveller in boots and a thin, anxious clergyman who kept consulting his watch. Neither of them looked out of the window for long. They talked of the bank rate, of the mobilization orders posted in Winchester, of the rumour that a German cruiser had been sighted off the Nore.

"It is the judgment of Providence," the clergyman said once, his fingers tightening on his umbrella handle. "A sign in the firmament, and the nations rushing to slaughter."

"It’s bad for trade, is what it is," the traveller muttered, wiping his brow with a spotted handkerchief. "And the heat—there’s no air in this carriage. It’s like breathing warm flannel."

William said nothing. He sat in the corner with his hand in his pocket, his thumb tracing the knurled grip of the revolver. He thought of Nettie’s throat, white against the dark green of her Sunday serge, and of how she had laughed last summer when they walked by the canal at Clayton, before Verrall had ever set eyes on her. The jealousy in him was no longer a hot fire; it had cooled into something hard and angular, like a flint in his chest. When the train finally hissed into Shaphambury station, he stepped onto the platform before the carriages had fairly stopped.

Shaphambury was a town built for idleness, all white stucco terraces and clipped tamarisk hedges facing the English Channel, and tonight it wore an air of brittle, feverish gaiety. Along the esplanade, the hotels blazed with electric light, as if their guests meant to drown out the green glare overhead. Ladies in silk wraps strolled beneath the palms, their laughter a pitch too high, while gentlemen in evening dress stood in tight knots by the balustrade, their cigars glowing red as they argued about the Belgian frontier.

William found them within the hour. He had no need of cunning; he simply walked the length of the seafront until he saw Verrall’s yellow motorcar parked outside the Grand Marine. From the shadow of a privet hedge across the gravel sweep, he watched the lighted terrace.

They came out after nine—Verrall tall and indolent in his dinner jacket, Nettie beside him in a gown of pale muslin that William had never seen before. Her hair was dressed high, and a shawl of silver gauze hung from her bare shoulders. She looked like one of the fine ladies from the illustrated papers, yet as she paused by the stone urns, William saw her glance upward at the great green smear in the sky, and saw her hand tighten nervously on Verrall’s sleeve. Verrall bent his head and said something, smiling with that easy, proprietary tenderness that made William’s teeth grind together.

They did not stay among the crowd. Turning away from the hotel music, they descended the steps toward the lower promenade and took the narrow chalk path that climbed the headland toward the downs. William waited until the darkness swallowed the gleam of her white dress, then slipped across the gravel and followed them.

Out on the headland, the wind from the Channel had died away altogether. The sea lay below like dark oiled glass, catching the eerie reflection of the heavens, and the only sound was the dry rustle of the gorse as Nettie’s skirt brushed past it. They walked slowly fifty paces ahead of him, their shadows scarcely distinguishable from the turf.

At the bench near the cliff’s edge, Verrall stopped and drew her closer. William stepped squarely onto the white chalk track, ten yards away, and drew the revolver.

"Nettie," he said, his voice small and cracked in the stillness.

She gave a sharp cry and shrank back. Verrall stepped instantly before her, his face pale in the greenish gloom, though he did not flinch. "Leadford? Put that thing down. Are you out of your senses?"

"Stand aside," William said, raising his arm. "I’ve come to settle with you."

Before Verrall could answer, a curious heaviness dropped upon the cliff. A faint, sweetish odour—like crushed almond leaves and wet chalk—rose from the grass. The green light overhead thickened into a luminous mist rolling silently in from the sea, wrapping the headland in a soft hush. William tried to press the trigger, but his arm felt leaden, his knees inexplicably weak. Beside the bench, Nettie sank gently onto the turf without a sound, and Verrall crumpled forward beside her. William watched them fall as if from a great distance, then felt the chalk rush up to meet his cheek, and knew nothing more.

He woke to the singing of a lark, high and clear in a sky of washed, pale blue. For a long while he lay still on his side, his cheek pressed against the damp turf, wondering why the air tasted so clean—cleaner than any air he had ever breathed in Staffordshire, without the faintest taint of coal smoke or sea-fog.

When he sat up, his head was wonderfully light. The Belgian revolver lay a few inches from his hand, its nickel barrel beaded with dew. He looked at it with a kind of quiet curiosity, as if it were an odd, ugly stone he had picked up on a walk and forgotten to throw away. Then he looked across the chalk path.

Verrall was sitting up by the bench, rubbing his eyes with the back of his hand, his evening shirt crumpled and stained with grass. Beside him, Nettie stirred, pushing a loose strand of dark hair from her forehead. When she saw William, there was no terror in her face—only a deep, bewildered stillness, as though they had all three woken from the same long, feverish dream.

William stood up, leaving the pistol where it lay in the wet grass, and walked slowly toward them. The hot, knotted rage that had driven him across half of England was gone, vanished as completely as the green mist from the sky. Looking at Nettie’s pale, tear-stained cheeks, and at Verrall’s bewildered eyes, he felt only an immense, lucid pity—for her, for himself, for the foolish pride that had nearly made a murderer of him.

"Are you hurt, Nettie?" he asked gently.

She shook her head, looking up at him with steady, wondering eyes. Far below them, out in the sunlit Channel, two grey warships lay motionless upon the water, their funnels smokeless, as quiet as the sleeping town behind them.
