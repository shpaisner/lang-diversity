The rain in Staffordshire never seemed to fall from the sky so much as it seeped out of the soot-blackened brick. William Leadford stood by the narrow window of Rawdon’s counting-house, his knuckles white against the sill, staring down at the slick cobblestones of the yard. Behind him, the gas-jet hissed above his ledger, where columns of figures waited in neat, miserable rows—shillings and pence he would never own, tallying crates of cheap earthenware bound for London.

In his coat pocket lay a crumpled sheet of cheap notepaper. Nettie had not even had the courage to tell him to his face. Do not look for me, Will, she had written in her round, schoolgirl hand. I cannot bear the smoke any longer, nor the waiting. Edward has promised to take care of me.

Edward Verrall. The name tasted of copper and bile. William knew Verrall’s sort—the gentry from the big house on the ridge, with their soft palms, their tweed shooting-jackets, and their easy assumption that whatever grew in the valley belonged to them for the plucking. Outside, a newsboy’s cry cut through the clatter of a passing dray, shrill with evening panic: Ultimatum! Fleet mobilizes in the North Sea! The men in the yard barely looked up. War was coming, everyone said so, an iron storm gathering over Europe, yet to William it felt like nothing more than a larger version of the cruelty that had already emptied his life. He turned back to the desk, blew out the gas, and reached for his hat. There was a train to the coast at dawn, and he had five pounds saved beneath his floorboards.

His mother was asleep in the kitchen chair when he let himself into the cottage, her chin resting on the collar of her faded shawl. A single candle guttered on the table beside a cold plate of bread and dripping. William stood in the doorway, his boots dripping mud onto the scrubbed flagstones, and felt a sudden, suffocating pity for her—for the forty years she had spent scrubbing coal-dust out of his father’s shirts, and now his own, with nothing to show for it but crooked fingers and a worsening cough.

He crept upstairs without waking her. In the tiny room beneath the eaves, the air was damp and smelled of plaster. He knelt by the hearth, pried up the loose pine board, and drew out the tin box. Beneath the folded banknotes lay the little Belgian revolver he had bought off a drunken foundryman in Hanley three months ago, back when he had still believed a man might need to defend his rights in a strike. The metal was cold and greasy in his palm.

Crossing to the dormer window, he pushed the casement open. Above the jagged silhouettes of the bottle-kilns and the black mounds of slag, the sky had cleared to a bruised violet. There it hung, just above the eastern horizon: the green star the papers had been prattling about for weeks. It was larger tonight, no longer a mere speck, but a pale, luminous smear trailing a milky plume across the constellations. It looked cold and indifferent, like a drop of verdigris on velvet. William slipped the pistol into his pocket and sat on his narrow bed to wait for morning.

The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool, shag tobacco, and fear. All the way down from the Midlands, the men opposite William argued over their folded newspapers, their voices rising above the clack of the rails. A commercial traveler in a bowler hat tapped a map of Belgium with a chewed pencil, declaring that the Kaiser’s armies would be in Brussels before Sunday. Beside him, a young clerk kept repeating that the banks would close, that the price of bread would double by Tuesday.

William said nothing. He kept his gaze fixed on the flat, green pastures sliding past the glass, so different from the scarred clay-pits of home, yet he felt no relief. His right hand remained buried in his pocket, his thumb tracing the knurled grip of the revolver. Every mile south tightened a wire inside his chest. He pictured Nettie—her dark hair pinned beneath a straw hat, her quick, nervous laugh—walking beside Verrall along some sunlit promenade while the world slid toward slaughter.

By the time the train shuddered to a halt at Shaphambury, dusk was pooling in the hollows of the sand-dunes. The sea-air struck William’s face, sharp with salt and rotting kelp, almost dizzying after the sulphur of the Potteries. Along the station approach, holidaymakers hurried toward the London trains with strapped trunks, their faces pale beneath the newly lit streetlamps. A telegraph boy pedaled past, shouting to a porter about the Channel Fleet. William turned his back on the crowd and walked toward the cliffs, where the summer villas overlooked the dark, swelling water.

Night fell strangely, without true darkness. The green comet now spanned half the heavens, its pale, sea-colored radiance washing over the chalk cliffs and turning the breaking surf into ribbons of ghostly froth. William found them near midnight, walking along the low stone wall that bordered the gardens of the Royal Hotel. He knew Nettie at once by the pale shawl draped over her shoulders, and Verrall by the tall, careless slope of his silhouette and the red tip of his cigarette.

They stopped by a gap in the tamarisk hedge, looking out at the sea. Nettie’s voice drifted back to William on the warm wind, low and troubled. “It frightens me, Edward. Look at the sky. Everyone in the dining-room was talking of war.”

“Nonsense, my darling,” Verrall replied, his tone light, almost amused, as he slipped an arm around her waist. “The politicians always rattle their sabers in August. Come inside; you’ll catch a chill.”

A red mist seemed to rise behind William’s eyes. He stepped out from the shadow of the bathing-huts, his boots crunching on the shingle. “Nettie,” he called, his voice cracking like dry wood.

She gasped, pulling away from Verrall’s arm. Verrall stiffened, stepping between them. “Leadford? What the devil are you doing here?”

William drew the pistol, his hand shaking violently. “You stole her,” he choked out, though the words sounded thin against the roar of the tide. Verrall lunged, catching William’s wrist. There was a sharp crack, a smell of burnt powder, and Nettie’s scream—cut short by a sudden, heavy sighing of the wind. From across the water rolled a dense, luminous fog, sweet as crushed clover and heavy as chloroform. William’s knees buckled, and the pistol slipped from his numb fingers as the green mist closed over his eyes.

He woke to the sound of gulls wheeling overhead and the cool tickle of marram grass against his cheek. For a long moment, William lay still, staring up at a sky of washed, pale blue. The oppressive weight that had sat behind his ribs for years had vanished, as though a window had been thrown open in a long-sealed room.

He sat up slowly. A few yards away, the little revolver lay half-buried in the sand, its barrel speckled with salt-dew. Looking at it, William felt no surge of shame, nor any lingering spark of the murderous heat that had driven him south. He felt only a quiet, wondering sorrow for the bruised, frightened creature he had been the night before, clutching at a woman as if she were property to be hoarded against the dark.

Near the tamarisk hedge, Edward Verrall was rising to his elbows, blinking in the clear morning light. His linen collar was torn, and a smear of dirt darkened his cheek. Beside him, Nettie stirred, pushing her tangled hair back from her forehead. She looked at William, and for the first time since he had known her, there was no guilt in her eyes, nor fear—only a wide, startled recognition.

“Will?” she murmured, her voice gentle.

“Are you hurt?” William asked, getting to his feet and brushing sand from his trousers.

Verrall stood up too, his hand going to his bruised wrist, then dropping to his side. He looked at William with a stripped-down honesty, his gentry polish washed away by the night’s sleep. “It went wide,” Verrall said quietly. “You missed me, Leadford. Though I think I deserved it.”

“No,” William said, and meant it. “No one deserves that.”

They walked back into Shaphambury together, three rumpled figures moving through a town that seemed to have woken from a collective fever. The streets were unusually quiet. Along the promenade, shopkeepers were unbarring their shutters without hurry, pausing to speak to one another across the cobblestones with an unaccustomed gentleness. At the corner by the post office, a small crowd had gathered around the telegraph board, where a pale-faced clerk was pinning up a fresh sheet of paper.

William stopped to read it over a fisherman’s shoulder. The ultimatum had been withdrawn; the fleets in the North Sea had turned back toward their harbors, their crews reportedly standing on the decks in the dawn light, staring in bewilderment at the guns they had been ordered to load. A murmur of quiet relief passed through the crowd—no cheering, no flags, only the sound of men and women exhaling after a long terror.

Nettie stood beside him, her fingers lightly touching the sleeve of his worn coat. “What will you do now, Will?” she asked softly.

He looked down at her, seeing her clearly at last—not as a prize he had lost, nor as an escape from the soot of Staffordshire, but simply as a young woman trying to find her way in a world that had suddenly grown vast and unfamiliar. “I’ll go back to my mother,” he said, a faint smile touching his mouth. “She’ll be worried. And there is work enough to be done at home.”

He shook Verrall’s hand, feeling the warmth of the other man’s grip without a trace of rancor, and turned toward the railway station. Above the red-tiled roofs and the calm, glittering sea, the morning sun climbed higher, shining down on a world that looked no different than it had yesterday, yet felt, for the first time, like a place where men might learn to live.
