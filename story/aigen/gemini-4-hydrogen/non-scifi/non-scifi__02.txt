William Leadford knew the worst before he even reached the end of the terraced row. The kilns of Clayton were belching their usual greasy smoke into the dusk, and his boots were soaked through from the black slush of the lane, yet it was not the damp that chilled him. It was the sight of Mrs. Burleigh standing at her gate without a shawl, watching him come up the hill with the terrible, flat pity of a neighbour who has already told the whole street. Nettie had gone. She had taken her wicker trunk to the station at noon, and young Mr. Verrall, with his motor-car and his father’s money, had met her on the platform.

Inside his mother’s kitchen, the oil lamp smelled of paraffin and the air was thick with the steam of boiled cabbage. Leadford sat at the oilcloth table without taking off his cap. He earned twenty-two shillings a week as a clerk at the earthenware works, a sum that had seemed sufficient only when Nettie’s hand was in his along the canal towpath. Now, the chipped mug before him, the dampness creeping up the wallpaper, and the endless cough of his mother in the scullery all pressed in upon him like a prison wall.

Through the narrow window, above the jagged silhouettes of the bottle-ovens, a pale green star hung low in the sky. Men at the public house had been arguing about it for a fortnight, just as they argued about the newspapers from London—full of mobilisation, ultimata, and the German fleet. Leadford did not care about the Kaiser or the skies. He only knew that a gentleman had reached down into the soot and taken the one clean thing he possessed.

He did not go to the counting-house the next morning. Instead, he put on his Sunday collar, though it was frayed at the fold, and walked down into Hanbridge with his savings—three sovereigns and some loose silver—tied in a handkerchief. The streets were unusually loud. Newsboys were shouting special editions at every corner, their voices hoarse with the promise of a European war, and little knots of potters stood outside the tobacconists, reading the placards with grim, excited faces.

Leadford pushed open the door of a pawnbroker’s shop in a narrow alley behind the market. The bell jangled, and an old man with spectacles pushed up onto his forehead looked over a tray of tarnished watches. When Leadford asked for a revolver, the pawnbroker scarcely blinked; the whole country seemed to be reaching for iron that week. He produced a heavy, dull-metal pistol and a cardboard box of cartridges, laying them on the baize counter between them. Leadford paid without bargaining. The weight of the thing in his coat pocket made his shoulder droop, yet it gave him a hard, quiet centre to lean upon.

By midday he was in a third-class carriage rattling southward. Through the grimy glass, the black slag-heaps of Staffordshire gradually gave way to green pastures, hedgerows, and the red roofs of quiet shires. The other passengers talked incessantly of troops moving to the ports and of the strange greenish glare that had lingered even after sunrise. Leadford sat in the corner with his arms folded, feeling the cold cylinder press against his ribs, nursing his grievance until it was the only warm thing inside him.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, tar, and expensive leisure. Coming out of the railway station into the late afternoon sun, Leadford felt like a smear of coal-dust on a white tablecloth. Men in cream flannels and women carrying lace parasols strolled along the esplanade, though even here the war-fever had touched them; gentlemen stopped by the kiosk to buy the evening papers, and clusters of young clerks in straw boaters argued loudly near the bandstand.

He found them near the western end of the parade, where the stuccoed boarding-houses gave way to neat brick villas overlooking the shingle. They were walking slowly toward the cliffs. Verrall wore a light tweed jacket and carried a walking-stick, his head bent attentively toward Nettie. She was wearing the blue cotton dress Leadford had always admired, the one she had sewn herself on winter evenings in Clayton, and as Verrall said something, she tilted her head back and laughed.

That laugh cut through Leadford’s chest like a jagged shard of pot. All the long nights he had spent reading cheap books to better himself for her, all the humiliation of his leaky boots and his mother’s washing-tubs, rose in his throat. He kept fifty paces behind them, his hand buried in his coat pocket, his fingers curled around the checkered grip of the pistol. Above the sea, the sun was sinking into a bank of copper cloud, and already the great green star was visible in the eastern sky—no longer a mere point of light, but a bloated, luminous disc that cast a faint, sickly sheen across the darkening water.

The path wound upward from the promenade onto the open chalk downs, where the turf was short and springy and patches of yellow gorse caught the dying light. Here, away from the clatter of carriages and the shouting of the newsboys below, the evening was unnaturally still. Not a gull cried; not a breath of wind stirred the grasses. A curious, sweetish haze was creeping inland from the horizon, tinted a faint, unearthly emerald by the swollen star overhead.

Leadford quickened his stride on the soft turf until he was within ten yards of them, just where the path dipped behind a clump of hawthorn. When he stepped out, blocking their way, Nettie gave a sharp little cry and caught at Verrall’s sleeve.

"Willie!" she gasped, her face draining of colour. "What are you doing here?"

Verrall stiffened, stepping half in front of her with the instinctive, infuriating composure of his class. "Look here, Leadford," he said, his voice tight. "There’s no sense making a scene."

Leadford pulled the revolver from his pocket. His hand was shaking, not from fear, but from the sudden, blinding rush of his own inadequacy. "You stole her," he said, his voice cracking in the quiet air. "You’ve everything, and you came down into the dirt and took the one thing that was mine."

He raised the heavy barrel. Verrall’s eyes widened, and Nettie threw up her hands. Just as Leadford’s finger tightened on the trigger, a thick, cool wave of green-white vapour rolled over the crest of the hill, smelling strangely of bruised poppies and rain. His lungs filled with it; his knees turned to water. The pistol dropped from his fingers into the grass, and the world dissolved into a soft, silent dark.

When Leadford opened his eyes, the dawn was breaking over a world that looked as though it had been washed in clear spring water. The heavy, suffocating haze was gone, leaving the sky a pale, luminous violet above the sea. He lay on his side in the damp grass, his cheek pressed against a tuft of wild thyme. For a long moment he did not move, marvelling at the extraordinary lightness in his chest. The hot, jagged knot of hatred that he had carried all the way from Staffordshire had vanished, leaving behind only a quiet, aching clarity, like the stillness after a long fever has broken.

He pushed himself up on one elbow. Beside his knee lay the pawnbroker’s revolver, its metal dulled by the heavy dew. Looking at it now, he felt no anger, only a profound, bewildering shame—not the mean shame of poverty, but the larger shame of a man who had meant to kill another creature over his own bruised pride.

A few yards away, Verrall was sitting up on the turf, rubbing his forehead with the heel of his hand as if waking from a deep, dreamless sleep. Beside him, Nettie stirred, pushing back the stray hair from her eyes. When she saw Leadford, she did not flinch or cry out. She simply looked at him with a quiet, bewildered recognition, as though they were three strangers who had survived the same shipwreck and been cast up together on an unfamiliar shore.

Verrall was the first to stand, brushing the chalk dust from his tweed trousers. He looked at the pistol lying in the grass, then up at Leadford’s face, and whatever fear or arrogance had stood between them the night before seemed to have melted away in the cool morning air.

"You’re all right, Leadford?" Verrall asked gently.

"I’m all right," Leadford said. He bent down, picked up the revolver by the barrel, and walked to the edge of the steep chalk cliff. Without ceremony, he tossed it out over the void; it spun once in the pale light and vanished into the deep, quiet water far below.

They walked back down the path toward Shaphambury together, saying very little. Nettie walked between them, her blue dress damp with dew, her face pale and serious in the morning light. Leadford looked at her—really looked at her, without the desperate hunger of possession—and saw for the first time how young and frightened she was, and how little either of them had understood what they were doing.

Below, the town was waking, yet the frantic clamour of the previous evening had ceased. Along the esplanade, small groups of townspeople stood leaning against the iron railings, gazing silently out at the calm sea. A newsboy sat on a stone bollard near the pier, his bundle of war-editions lying unopened at his feet, as if the fierce quarrel of the nations had suddenly become as meaningless as Leadford’s own. At the corner of the parade, Leadford stopped and held out his hand. Verrall shook it firmly, and Nettie pressed his fingers with a quiet, tearless gratitude. Then Leadford turned alone toward the railway station, his boots still worn, his pockets empty, yet walking steadily into a world that had been given back its breath.
