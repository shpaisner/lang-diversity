The smoke of the Five Towns hung low over the wet slates, tasting of sulphur and wet ash. William Leadford stood by the canal bridge, his boots caked in yellow clay, reading the folded note a second time until the cheap ink blurred in the drizzle. Nettie had gone to the coast. Not alone—the whole street knew by noon that young Mr. Verrall’s motor-car had been seen waiting by the tollgate at dawn, its brass lamps muffled in mist. Leadford folded the paper and thrust it into his serge pocket, his fingers brushing the cold brass of his watch-chain. Around him, the ironworks thumped with a dull, indifferent pulse. On the corner by the public house, a cluster of colliers stood arguing over an evening edition, their faces lit by the flare of a naphtha lamp. There was talk of the North Sea, of troop trains rattling south through Crewe, of ultimata and broken treaties. Leadford scarcely heard them. When he looked up past the black mouths of the bottle kilns, the sky was not its usual soot-brown; above the ragged clouds hung a pale, sickly smear of greenish light, like phosphorus rubbed on wet slate. People called it the comet, though to Leadford it looked merely like a bruise on the evening. He turned toward his mother’s terrace, his jaw set so hard his teeth ached. Let the Germans come, let the fleet sink; a man could only bear so much, and his own small world had already been torn open.

His mother’s kitchen smelled of boiled cabbage and damp wool. She sat by the grate, darning a grey stocking by the light of a single tallow candle, watching him with the quiet, helpless dread of women who have spent their lives waiting for bad news. Leadford did not sit down. He took his best coat from the peg behind the door and counted the shillings in his tin box—fourteen and sixpence, the sum of three weeks’ clerking at Rawdon’s pottery works before the strike had shut the gates. By morning, he would be in London, and by evening, on the shingle at Shaphambury. He walked out again into the narrow street, past the shuttered draper’s, to old Moss’s shop by the railway arch. Moss did not ask questions of men with Leadford’s look; he laid a blunt-nosed Belgian revolver on the baize counter alongside six dull cartridges, and took twelve shillings without blinking. Leadford slipped the iron weight into his coat pocket. It pulled the lining down against his thigh, solid and comforting, an answer to every sneer he had swallowed since boyhood. Verrall had his father’s money, his smooth Cambridge vowels, his fine leather boots; Leadford had this. Outside, a boy ran past shouting a special edition—war declared at midnight, or near enough—and the green star overhead seemed brighter now, casting a faint, unhealthy sheen on the wet cobbles. Leadford kept his head down and walked toward the station.

The third-class carriage was stifling with wet wool and cheap tobacco. Across from Leadford, two reservists in ill-fitting khaki sat with their kitbags between their knees, passing a flat bottle of gin and singing scraps of music-hall songs that fell flat against the rattle of the wheels. At every junction—Rugby, Bletchley, the sprawling sidings outside London—the train was shunted aside to let long, dark troop-trains thunder past, their windows crowded with pale, shouting faces. An elderly clerk in the corner kept tapping his newspaper with a trembling finger, muttering about the gold standard and the price of bread, while a woman with a sleeping child pressed her forehead to the glass and wept silently. Leadford sat with his hand in his pocket, his thumb tracing the knurled grip of the revolver. He felt no kinship with their panic. Their war was a distant, noisy thing of maps and admirals; his was intimate, a tight knot behind his breastbone that would only loosen when he saw Verrall’s smooth face go slack with terror. When they changed at last for the branch line to the sea, night had fallen entirely. Through the soot-streaked window, the eastern sky glowed with an unnatural, emerald luminance, bright enough to turn the chalk cuttings and the sleeping hedgerows the colour of verdigris. Nobody in the carriage spoke of it anymore. They were too tired, and the air through the ventilator tasted faintly of lightning and crushed mint.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, rotting kelp, and coal-smoke from the coastguard station. Leadford knew the town only from a picture postcard Nettie had once shown him, yet he found the seafront easily enough, following the gas-lamps down a steep lane of flint cottages. The promenade was nearly deserted; a few townsfolk stood by the seawall, staring out toward the Channel where searchlights swept the low cloud-bank like nervous white fingers. Leadford kept to the shadow of the tamarisk bushes above the shingle. He waited an hour, his boots soaking through with dew, before he heard voices coming up from the steps of the Grand Hotel. It was Verrall’s laugh first—light, unhurried, the laugh of a man who had never counted pennies in his life—and then Nettie’s, softer, slightly breathless. They paused under a flickering lamp. Nettie wore a pale shawl over her shoulders, and she was looking up at Verrall with an expression Leadford had never once seen her wear for him: an easy, unguarded tenderness, free of all the pinching anxieties of the Five Towns. Leadford stepped out from the bushes, the gravel crunching loud beneath his heel. He drew the revolver, his arm stiff as a fence-post. Verrall turned, his eyes widening in the greenish gloom, and Nettie gave a sharp, small cry. Leadford’s finger tightened on the cold iron, taking up the slack of the trigger, ready to end the whole miserable business in a single clap of noise.

Before the hammer could fall, the wind died. It did not merely drop; it ceased as though a heavy door had been shut across the mouth of the Channel. A vast, cool hush settled over the promenade, swallowing the hiss of the shingle and the distant throb of a steamer’s engines. Out of the sea-mist rolled a bank of vapour, pale green and luminous, drifting through the tamarisk leaves without stirring a twig. Leadford tried to shout, to curse them both and pull the trigger, but his throat closed around a sudden, overwhelming sweetness, like the scent of clover fields after rain. The revolver grew impossibly heavy in his hand, dragging his arm down until the barrel pointed at his own wet boots. Across the path, Verrall had dropped to one knee, his hand pressed to his brow, while Nettie swayed against the iron railings, her eyes fluttering shut as gently as a child’s. A strange lightness rose behind Leadford’s eyes, washing away the hot, cramped fury that had sustained him for three days. He thought, with a sudden, bewildering clarity, of his mother’s hands in the candle-light, and of how small and foolish his anger looked beneath that immense, silent sky. His knees gave way on the damp turf. As he sank down, the green mist folded over his face, cool and merciful, and he heard the revolver clatter harmlessly somewhere among the stones before the dark took him.

When Leadford woke, the sun was already high, and the world looked as though it had been washed in clear water. There was no green glare in the sky—only a vast, pale blue vault stretching over a quiet sea. He sat up slowly, stiff from the damp grass, his coat soaked with dew. A few yards away, the Belgian revolver lay half-buried in a clump of sea-thrift, dull and rusted already by the salt air. He looked at it for a long moment, feeling neither shame nor pride, only a quiet wonder that he had carried such a clumsy piece of iron two hundred miles to mend a broken heart. Down on the promenade, people were stirring—fishermen, a policeman without his helmet, two old ladies sitting on a bench as if waking from a long afternoon nap. Nobody was running; no newsboys were shouting. Near the hotel steps, Nettie and Verrall were standing together by the low stone wall. Leadford got to his feet, brushed the dry grass from his trousers, and walked toward them. Nettie saw him first, and she did not shrink back or cry out. Her face was pale, her hair damp with mist, but her eyes were clear and steady. Verrall looked at him too, without his usual guarded arrogance, only a quiet, tired decency. Leadford stopped a few paces off, his hands hanging empty at his sides. There was no need for grand speeches, nor any sudden forgiveness; the old bitterness had simply thinned out and blown away like coal-smoke. "You're cold, Nettie," he said, his voice sounding strange and mild in his own ears, and she nodded, wrapping her shawl tighter, as the three of them turned to look out over the empty, peaceful water.
