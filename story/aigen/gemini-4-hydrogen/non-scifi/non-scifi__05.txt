The kitchen in Clayton smelled always of boiled cabbage, damp wool, and the sour coal-smoke that drifted down from the potbanks whenever the wind turned east. William Leadford sat by the fender with Nettie’s letter flattened against his knee. He had read the six lines until the ink seemed to lift off the cheap, ruled paper and float before his eyes. She was sorry. She had not meant to be cruel. She had gone away with Mr. Verrall because she could not bear the greyness any longer.

His mother rattled the kettle at the range, her small, swollen hands moving with a stubborn, rhythmic patience that made him want to strike the table. She did not know about the letter, though she must have guessed from the way he sat—his shoulders hunched, his boots still caked with the yellow clay of the canal path, his tea cooling untouched on the oilcloth.

"There’s a queer light out by the kilns," she murmured, peering through the misted pane. "Folks at the shop say it’s larger tonight."

William did not look up. He knew what she meant; for three weeks the green star had hung above the slag heaps at dusk, a pale, cold smear in the smoky sky that the colliers pointed at with their pipe-stems. To William it was nothing—a trick of the air, no more to him than the talk in the Red Lion about the German cruisers and the ultimatum in the morning papers. His world had shrunk to the narrow parlor of his own humiliation, to the memory of Nettie’s small, gloved hand resting on Verrall’s tweed sleeve.

Two days later, William took the six-thirty train out of Stoke with four pounds in his pocket and a nickel-plated revolver wrapped in a clean handkerchief at the bottom of his Gladstone bag. He had bought the pistol from a pawnbroker in Hanley who had barely glanced at his face, being too busy arguing with a customer about the mobilization of the Reserve. Everywhere William went, men were shouting about war. They crowded the newsagents’ windows, red-faced and thirsty for trouble, as though the whole country had caught the same fever that burned behind his own ribs.

He sat in the corner of the third-class carriage, watching the black, scarred valleys of Staffordshire slip away into green hedgerows and quiet orchards. A commercial traveler opposite him rattled a newspaper, reading aloud about dreadnoughts steaming out of Portsmouth.

"We’ll teach them a lesson before Michaelmas," the man said, looking around for agreement.

William stared out at the passing elms and said nothing. He understood the man’s appetite for ruin; he felt it in his own jaw, tight and aching. Verrall was of that breed—the young gentlemen with their motorcars and their easy, apologetic smiles, who took what they wanted from the daughters of clerks and weavers and thought a five-pound note or a week at the seaside settled the account. When the train reached the coast at dusk, the sea air tasted sharp and foreign against William’s tongue, and the green star, grown huge now, cast a faint, sickly sheen across the slate roofs of Shaphambury.

Shaphambury was all white stucco and brisk salt wind, full of people who had nothing better to do than stroll along the esplanade and watch the tide come in. William found a back room in a lodging house behind the gasworks, where the landlady eyed his shabby serge suit and demanded three shillings in advance. He paid without a word and went out into the streets to hunt.

It took him a day and a half to find them. He saw Nettie first, coming out of a draper’s shop near the pier. She was wearing a new coat of pale cream cloth, and a straw hat tilted forward over her brow in a way she had never worn in Clayton. For a moment William’s breath stopped in his throat; she looked so small, so fragile, and so terribly far beyond his reach. Then Verrall stepped out behind her, carrying two parcels, his fair hair ruffled by the wind, laughing at something she had said.

William stood in the doorway of a tobacconist’s until they turned the corner toward the cliffs. His hand, thrust deep into his coat pocket, closed around the cold, oiled grip of the revolver. He did not hate Nettie—that was the worst of it. He loved her with a fierce, bruised tenderness that turned instantly to poison whenever he pictured Verrall’s hand at her waist.

That night, he could not sleep. He sat by the attic window watching the comet. Its tail now stretched halfway across the heavens, a ghostly, luminous veil of emerald mist that made the gaslamps below look yellow and dying.

He caught them at dawn on the chalk downs above the bay, where the short turf was wet with dew and the sea lay flat and grey beneath a strange, greenish twilight. They had walked out early, perhaps to see the comet before the sun rose, or perhaps because they, too, had felt the restless heaviness in the air.

William stepped out from behind a clump of gorse twenty yards ahead of them. He did not shout or wave his arms; he simply raised the pistol and walked forward, his boots squelching in the damp grass.

Nettie gave a sharp, small cry and caught at Verrall’s arm. Verrall stopped dead, the color draining from his cheeks, though he shifted slightly to put his shoulder between her and the barrel.

"Leadford," Verrall said, his voice tight but steady. "Put that down. Don’t be a fool."

"You’ve ruined her," William said. The words sounded thin and theatrical in the vast, quiet air, and that made him angrier. His thumb drew back the hammer. "You’ve had your sport, and now you’ll pay for it."

Nettie’s face was white as the chalk path. "Willie, please—it wasn’t him, I came of my own—"

Before William could squeeze the trigger, a sudden stillness fell over the downs. The sea-breeze died. A pale green vapor, sweet and faintly pungent like crushed mint, drifted up from the horizon, thickening the air until the white cliffs vanished. William’s arm grew heavy as lead. His knees buckled, the pistol slipped from his numb fingers into the grass, and a deep, dreamless sleep folded over all three of them.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was well above the sea, shining with an extraordinary, washed clarity. Every blade of grass beside his cheek stood out distinct and bright, beaded with clear water. He lay still for a long moment, waiting for the familiar sourness in his stomach, the hot, throbbing knot behind his eyes that had been his constant companion for months.

Nothing came. His head was as light and quiet as an empty room with the windows thrown open.

He pushed himself up on one elbow. Ten paces away, Verrall was sitting in the turf, staring down at his own open hands with a look of mild, bewildered wonder. Beside him, Nettie stirred, tucked her skirt beneath her knees, and sat up, brushing a strand of hair from her forehead.

William looked down at the nickel-plated revolver lying near his boot. In the clean morning light it looked absurd—a clumsy, ugly lump of metal, like a dead beetle. He nudged it away with his toe, feeling a sudden, hot flush of shame, not at his failure, but at the smallness of the passion that had brought him there.

Nettie looked across the grass at him. There was no terror in her eyes now, only a quiet, searching recognition.

"Are you hurt, Willie?" she asked softly.

"No," he said, and his own voice surprised him; it sounded calm, almost gentle. "No, Nettie. I’m not hurt at all."

He stood up and brushed the damp chalk from his trousers, feeling the breeze off the Channel cool against his face, as if he had just woken from a long, feverish illness.

They walked back down into Shaphambury together, following the narrow white path above the sea. Nobody spoke much. There was no urge in William to demand what she owed him or to measure his pride against Verrall’s ease. Looking at Nettie’s profile against the bright water, he saw her clearly for the first time—not as a prize lost, nor a possession stolen, but simply as a young woman who had wanted air and light, and had taken the only road she knew to find them.

The town below was strangely quiet. People stood in doorways talking in low, unhurried voices, looking up at the clear blue sky where the comet had vanished with the dawn. At the corner by the harbour, a postman stopped Verrall to say that word had just come from London: the fleets were returning to port, and the mobilization orders had been cancelled in the night. Nobody knew why, yet nobody looked surprised.

They stopped at a small stall near the station, where a woman gave them warm rolls and mugs of tea, waving away Verrall’s silver with a quiet smile. Sitting on the low stone wall above the shingle, William broke his bread and watched the gulls wheeling over the tide.

"I’ll take the afternoon train back to Clayton," he said, looking at Nettie without bitterness. "My mother will be wondering."

Nettie nodded slowly, her fingers resting on the warm mug. "Tell her I’m well, Willie."

"I will," he said. When he rose to go, he shook Verrall’s hand—not as a rival defeated, nor as a friend, but simply as one man parting from another in the broad, honest light of day.
