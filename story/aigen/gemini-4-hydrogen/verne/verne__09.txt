In the murky, soot-laden basin of Staffordshire, where the great blast furnaces of the Potteries belch perpetual pillars of ochre smoke against the heavens, William Leadford walked with a heart heavier than the iron ore of the local mines. A clerk of modest means, earning scarcely twenty-five shillings a week, he had dared to anchor his entire existence to the affections of Nettie Stuart. Yet Nettie, dazzled by the prospects of wealth and leisure, had fled that very morning in the company of Edward Verrall, the heir to the dominant colliery interests of the district. To Leadford, the betrayal was not merely a wound to his pride; it was the total collapse of his moral universe. Driven by a cold, calculating fury, he entered a gunsmith’s shop in the High Street and purchased a six-chambered revolver of Belgian manufacture, together with fifty cartridges of smokeless powder. As he stepped back onto the cobblestones, wrapping the cold steel in his handkerchief, he paused to look upward. There, hanging above the jagged silhouette of the chimney stacks, shone the Great Comet. Discovered scarcely four months prior by astronomers at the Cape Observatory, this celestial wanderer had grown from a faint telescopic speck into a formidable luminary of a singular, pale emerald hue. Its nucleus rivaled Venus in brilliance, while its vast gaseous tail stretched upward toward the zenith like a luminous banner unfurled across the void. Men spoke of it in hushed, anxious tones in the taverns, yet Leadford paid it little heed; his eyes were fixed only upon the southern railway line.

While Leadford boarded the third-class carriage of the south-bound express, the scientific academies of Paris, Greenwich, and Berlin were gripped by an unprecedented agitation. Spectroscopic analysis of the comet’s light had revealed astonishing anomalies: alongside the familiar bands of carbon and cyanogen, there appeared a brilliant, unknown green line in the spectrum, indicating a gaseous element hitherto unrecorded by terrestrial chemistry. Worse still, calculations of the comet’s parabolic orbit confirmed that on the night of the seventeenth of September, the Earth would pass directly through the dense extremity of its tail. Yet the populace scarcely had time to contemplate astronomical peril, for Europe was rushing headlong toward a cataclysm of its own making. Across the Continent, telegraphic wires hummed with ultimatums. Mobilization orders were posted in Vienna, St. Petersburg, and Berlin; the great ironclads of the British Home Fleet had already slipped their moorings at Portsmouth, steaming eastward into the grey waters of the North Sea. Inside the rattling compartment, as the locomotive devoured the miles between Birmingham and London at fifty miles an hour, Leadford remained oblivious to the mobilization of empires. He sat rigid upon the wooden bench, his hand resting inside his coat pocket upon the checkered walnut grip of his revolver. Through the soot-streaked window, the English countryside blurred past—hedgerows, quiet hamlets, and fields of ripening wheat—all bathed in the eerie, shifting twilight of an autumn afternoon that seemed already tinged with the unnatural, greenish luminescence of the approaching star.

By nightfall, Leadford reached the coastal resort of Shaphambury, a town of whitewashed terraces and windswept esplanades perched above the English Channel. Under ordinary circumstances, the promenade would have been crowded with holidaymakers; tonight, however, an oppressive silence hung over the shingle beach. Far out upon the dark expanse of the Channel, the long, sweeping beams of naval searchlights pierced the mist, accompanied at intervals by the dull, unmistakable thunder of heavy artillery. War had commenced. Somewhere beyond the horizon, steel-clad cruisers were hurling shells of lyddite across the waves, while on shore, anxious citizens gathered in small knots upon the parade, gazing skyward with faces upturned to the heavens. For the comet was now a spectacle of terrifying magnificence. It spanned nearly a third of the firmament, casting a vivid, phosphorescent emerald glow over the chalk cliffs and turning the breaking surf into liquid jade. Leadford, his eyes bloodshot from lack of sleep and his mind sharpened to a single, lethal point, inquired at the post office and the principal hotels until he traced Verrall’s sleek, French-built motor-car to a secluded bungalow perched upon the western cliffs. He set out along the coastal path, the damp sea breeze whipping his threadbare coat against his knees. Above him, the green radiance intensified with every passing minute, illuminating the rugged path with the clarity of a theatrical limelight, while the distant cannonade rolled steadily across the restless waters like the beating of a colossal drum.

He found them shortly before three o’clock in the morning, standing upon a grassy ledge overlooking the sea, where the chalk precipice dropped eighty feet to the jagged rocks below. Unable to sleep beneath the uncanny brilliance of the heavens and alarmed by the gunfire echoing from the Channel, Nettie and Verrall had stepped outside to watch the horizon. Leadford approached silently across the springy turf until scarcely ten paces separated them. At the crunch of his boots upon the gravel path, Nettie turned, and a sharp cry escaped her lips as she recognized the gaunt, pale figure emerging from the emerald shadows. Verrall stepped instinctively before her, his handsome features hardening as he perceived the desperate resolution in his rival’s posture. "Leadford!" Verrall exclaimed, his voice tight with alarm. "What madness brings you here? Put away your folly—do you not hear the guns at sea? The whole world is going to ruin!" Leadford made no reply. A terrible, icy calm had settled over his faculties. He drew the Belgian revolver from his pocket, the metallic click of the hammer sounding extraordinarily loud in the brief lull between the distant salvoes. Raising his arm until the foresight aligned squarely with the center of Verrall’s tweed waistcoat, he felt his index finger take up the slack of the trigger. Nettie threw up her hands in a gesture of helpless terror, her eyes wide and luminous in the green starlight, as the chasm between love and murder narrowed to the breadth of a single heartbeat.

Yet the fatal shot was never fired. At that precise instant, a profound and inexplicable change overtook the terrestrial atmosphere. The booming of the naval guns out in the Channel ceased abruptly, as though a giant hand had muffled the throats of the cannon. Simultaneously, the night breeze died away into an absolute, breathless stillness. Looking upward, Leadford saw the vast emerald tail of the comet appear to descend, dissolving into a dense, luminous fog that engulfed the stars and swept rapidly down toward the surface of the sea. Within seconds, the strange vapor enveloped the cliffs. It was neither cold nor suffocating; rather, it possessed a faint, aromatic sweetness, reminiscent of crushed laurel and ozone, which seemed to invigorate the lungs even as it weighed heavily upon the eyelids. The barometric pressure dropped with dizzying rapidity. Leadford felt the revolver grow unbearably heavy in his grasp; his knees buckled beneath him, and the weapon slipped harmlessly from his nerveless fingers onto the damp grass. Across the narrow space of turf, he saw Verrall stagger and sink gently to the earth, his arm still outstretched toward Nettie, who was already collapsing softly beside him like a flower bowed by a summer rain. No agony accompanied this universal swoon. Across the entire hemisphere—aboard the darkened battleships in the Channel, within the crowded trenches of the Continent, and throughout the soot-blackened streets of Staffordshire—millions of men and women succumbed simultaneously to the gentle, irresistible chemistry of the celestial gas, plunging together into a deep and dreamless slumber.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun had already risen above the eastern horizon, flooding the English Channel with a radiance so pure and crystalline that it seemed the world had been washed anew. The green fog had vanished entirely, leaving behind an atmosphere of astonishing transparency, through which the distant coastline of France was faintly discernible. Leadford sat up slowly, his mind remarkably clear, utterly devoid of the feverish torment that had driven him south. Beside him in the dew-drenched grass lay the Belgian revolver; he regarded it with the detached curiosity one might bestow upon a relic of some barbarous, forgotten age, and tossed it idly over the cliff into the surf below. A few paces away, Nettie and Verrall were stirring from their slumber. As they rose to their feet and their eyes met his, there was neither fear in Nettie’s gaze nor arrogance in Verrall’s, and in Leadford’s own breast, the old, poisonous thorns of jealousy and class resentment had dissolved into nothingness. They spoke together quietly, marveling at the extraordinary lucidity of their thoughts, for the unknown element of the comet had purged the human brain of its ancient, animalistic fogs of hatred and greed. Far out upon the tranquil blue waters, the great warships lay motionless, their signal halyards fluttering with flags of truce rather than defiance, while their crews stood bewildered and reconciled upon the decks. Walking side by side back toward the waking town, Leadford, Nettie, and Verrall looked out upon a transformed Earth, ready at last to build an existence governed not by passion and strife, but by reason and universal brotherhood.
