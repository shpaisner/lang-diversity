In the blackened heart of Staffordshire, where the blast furnaces of the Potteries belched perpetual soot against a leaden firmament, William Leadford sat in his narrow garret, staring at a folded sheet of note-paper. To an observer acquainted with the harsh realities of industrial England, Leadford presented the familiar aspect of the thwarted clerk—thin-shouldered, pale from insufficient nourishment, and consumed by a fierce, silent pride. For three years his modest wages had scarcely sufficed for bread and tallow, yet he had nourished a singular passion for Nettie Stuart, the daughter of a neighboring artisan. Now, the letter informed him in hurried, tremulous characters that she had fled southward with Edward Verrall, the idle heir to the local colliery fortunes.

Above the jagged silhouettes of the chimney stacks, however, a far grander spectacle was unfolding in the nocturnal heavens. For six weeks, the observatories of Greenwich, Paris, and Pulkovo had tracked an erratic wanderer from the outer reaches of the solar system. Having passed its perihelion, the comet now rushed toward the Earth's orbit at a velocity exceeding forty miles per second. Its nucleus burned with an unprecedented emerald brilliance, and spectroscopic analysis had revealed an anomalous band in the green—a hitherto unknown gaseous element trailing behind it in a luminous plume millions of leagues in length.

Leadford cared nothing for celestial mechanics. As the greenish radiance seeped through his grimy windowpane, illuminating the poverty of his chamber, a cold, methodical fury crystallized within his breast. He rose, reached into a locked drawer, and drew forth a heavy six-chambered revolver, purchased that very afternoon with his final sovereign.

By dawn, William Leadford was aboard the southbound express, hurtling across the English midlands at fifty miles an hour. The railway carriage was crowded with anxious passengers, their faces buried in the morning editions of the London journals. While the celestial visitor commanded the inner columns with sober calculations of its impending grazing of the terrestrial atmosphere, the front pages screamed of imminent catastrophe closer to home. Across the continent, the great European powers had reached the brink of armed collision. Ultimata had been exchanged between the chancelleries; millions of conscripts were even now boarding troop trains along the Rhine, and the ironclad squadrons of the North Sea were steaming under sealed orders toward their battle stations.

Yet Leadford sat in stony silence, his hand resting upon the hard metallic contour of the weapon in his coat pocket. Through the soot-streaked window, the verdant fields of Warwickshire and Oxfordshire flashed past, bathed in a strange, sickly twilight, though the sun stood high. Already the outer fringes of the comet’s vast tail were brushing the upper strata of the terrestrial atmosphere, diffusing a faint, opalescent haze across the noon sky.

At each junction, the platforms swarmed with reservists bidding farewell to weeping families, and the air vibrated with the hoarse clamor of patriotic anthems. Leadford observed this collective madness with bitter detachment. To his mind, the impending collision of empires was no more terrible than the private treachery that had robbed him of his sole solace. When the train finally hissed to a halt at the coastal terminus of Shaphambury, the evening tide was rising, and the eastern horizon glowed with an unnatural, emerald phosphorescence.

Shaphambury was a tranquil watering-place perched upon the chalk cliffs of the southern coast, far removed from the clatter of the Staffordshire foundries. Under ordinary circumstances, its promenade would have been thronged with holidaymakers enjoying the bracing maritime air; tonight, however, a profound and ominous quiet reigned along the esplanade. Far out upon the slate-colored waters of the Channel, the sweeping beams of naval searchlights pierced the gathering dusk, silently signaling the presence of the Home Fleet, poised to intercept the rival armada before midnight.

Leadford walked briskly along the shingle, his boots crunching upon the wet pebbles as he consulted the address he had wrested from a reluctant railway porter. A mile beyond the town, nestled in a sheltered hollow above the cliffs, stood a whitewashed cottage surrounded by tamarisk hedges. Through the parted curtains of its ground-floor parlor, a warm lamplight spilled onto the manicured lawn.

Drawing nearer, Leadford concealed himself behind a weathered stone wall and peered through the foliage. There, seated beside a table laid with silver and crystal, was Edward Verrall, immaculate in evening attire, leaning forward with an expression of careless amusement. Opposite him sat Nettie, her dark hair catching the amber glow of the lamp, though her countenance betrayed a restless melancholy. At the sight of them—so insulated by wealth and indifference from the misery they had wrought—the blood hammered in Leadford’s temples. He drew the revolver from his pocket, verified the alignment of the brass cartridges in the cylinder, and stepped silently over the low wall onto the dew-soaked turf.

The glass doors of the parlor stood ajar to admit the sea breeze, which had abruptly fallen to a dead calm. Leadford crossed the terrace with noiseless precision and halted upon the threshold, his silhouette framed against the luminous green vault of the heavens.

"Nettie," he said, his voice flat and devoid of tremor.

At the sound, Nettie uttered a sharp cry and sprang from her chair, upsetting a goblet of wine across the damask cloth. Verrall rose more slowly, his initial astonishment giving way to aristocratic disdain as his eyes fell upon the leveled barrel of the revolver.

"Leadford!" Verrall exclaimed, stepping instinctively before the trembling girl. "Have you lost your senses? Put down that theatrical toy before you do yourself an injury."

"Stand aside, Verrall," Leadford replied, his finger tightening upon the cold steel trigger. "You have stolen everything a man may call his own. Tonight, accounts are settled."

Before Verrall could utter another syllable, a phenomenon of breathtaking magnitude arrested all three figures. Above the cliffs, the zenith seemed to split asunder in a silent cascade of emerald fire. Thousands of meteors—fragments of the comet’s disintegrating nucleus—streaked across the firmament without a sound, illuminating the chalk headlands and the motionless sea in blinding verdigris. Instantly, a strange, subtle perfume, resembling neither ozone nor any earthly blossom, permeated the stagnant air. It was a dense, luminous vapor, heavier than oxygen, descending rapidly from the upper atmosphere like an invisible tide. Leadford felt a sudden, unaccountable heaviness in his limbs; the barrel of his pistol wavered, dipping toward the floorboards as a profound, irresistible languor crept over his senses.

Across the entire breadth of the Northern Hemisphere, from the chalk cliffs of England to the fortified plains of Central Europe, the green vapor was accomplishing its silent, irresistible work. Had a dispassionate observer been stationed high above the terrestrial globe in a dirigible balloon, he would have witnessed a spectacle unparalleled in the annals of human history. Beneath that soft, emerald shroud, the roaring furnaces of the Midlands banked their fires; the thundering printing presses of London and Paris ground to a halt; and along the contested frontiers, the artillerymen slumped peacefully beside their unlimbered cannons, overcome by a sudden and beneficent lethargy.

In the coastal cottage at Shaphambury, the lamp flickered and died, leaving the room bathed solely in the serene, phosphorescent glow of the descending mist. Leadford’s knees buckled beneath him. The revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers, striking the carpeted floor with a dull thud that provoked no alarm. He sank gently beside the threshold, his head resting against the wooden frame, while a few paces away, Verrall collapsed into an armchair and Nettie subsided gracefully upon the hearthrug.

There was no struggle for breath, no sensation of suffocation. On the contrary, as the unknown celestial gas—perhaps an allotropic form of nitrogen, as the surviving physicists would later conjecture—entered their lungs and coursed through their veins, it acted as a marvelous chemical solvent upon the brain. Throughout the long, quiet hours of the night, while the Earth swept cleanly through the comet’s tail, the fevered anxieties, the ancestral hatreds, and the petty, consuming jealousies of mankind were gently oxidized and dissolved, leaving the human spirit washed clean as a slate after rain.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun had already cleared the eastern horizon, pouring a flood of extraordinarily pure, crystalline light through the open glass doors. The green mist had vanished entirely, leaving the sky a deep, transparent azure such as he had never beheld in the smoke-stained valleys of Staffordshire. He sat up slowly, marveling at the lightness of his frame and the astonishing lucidity of his mind. Every familiar ache of poverty, every jagged memory of humiliation and rage, had evaporated like a bad dream at daybreak.

He looked down at the heavy revolver lying beside his hand. It appeared to him now as an absurd, barbarous relic of a vanished age—an incomprehensible piece of ironmongery designed to shatter the miracle of human life. Picking it up by the barrel, he walked out across the dewy lawn to the edge of the cliff and cast it far out over the sparkling waters of the Channel.

Returning to the terrace, he found Nettie and Verrall standing together in the morning sunlight. There was no fear in Nettie’s eyes, nor any trace of arrogance in Verrall’s bearing; both looked upon him with a calm, luminous wonder, as though recognizing a brother long separated by a dark sea.

"Will," Nettie said softly, extending her hand—not in supplication, but in simple, profound fellowship.

Leadford took her hand, and then clasped Verrall’s, feeling no pang of bitterness, only an expansive, quiet joy. Far out upon the sea, the great ironclads were turning their prows peacefully toward their home ports, their guns forever silent. The world had awakened from its long, feverish night, and before the three of them lay the boundless, uncharted morning of a wiser humanity.
