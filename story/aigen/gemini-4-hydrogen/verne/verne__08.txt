In the autumn of that memorable year, the industrial district of Staffordshire presented a spectacle of unremitting gloom. Above the countless chimneys of Swathinglea, thick columns of bituminous smoke rose into a heavy sky, mingling with the perpetual haze of the blast furnaces. Amidst this labyrinth of brick and slag lived William Leadford, a young man of twenty-four years, whose modest employment as a junior clerk barely sufficed to sustain his widowed mother and himself. Yet it was not the rigors of poverty that weighed most heavily upon Leadford’s brow; rather, it was the bitter certainty of betrayal. Nettie, the sole object of his ardent affections, had abandoned the modest cottage of her father to elope with Edward Verrall, the scion of the wealthiest ironmaster in the county.

Night had scarcely fallen when Leadford stepped into the cobbled lane, his eyes drawn involuntarily toward the eastern horizon. There, suspended above the silhouette of the colliery wheels, hung the celestial visitor that had preoccupied the observatories of Greenwich, Paris, and Pulkovo for three consecutive months. To the naked eye, the comet appeared as an immense scimitar of pale, emerald luminescence, spanning nearly thirty degrees of the nocturnal arc. Astronomers, armed with the spectroscope, had already determined that its coma contained an anomalous nitrogenous gas, unknown to terrestrial chemistry. As Leadford gazed upon its cold, green radiance, he felt no scientific wonder, only a profound, suffocating despair that seemed to mirror the unnatural stillness of the atmosphere.

The following morning brought intelligence of a far more terrestrial, yet scarcely less terrifying, nature. Through the narrow streets of the town, newsboys ran with damp broadsheets, crying out the collapse of European diplomacy. From the chancelleries of Berlin and Vienna to the Admiralty in Whitehall, the vast machinery of modern warfare had been set in motion. Dreadnoughts were already slipping their moorings at Portsmouth, their coal bunkers filled to capacity, while across the continent, millions of conscripts were boarding trains toward fortified frontiers. The world, it seemed, was determined to consummate its long-standing rivalries in a tempest of steel and picric acid.

Leadford, however, remained entirely indifferent to the fate of empires. His universe had contracted to a single, burning point of personal injury. Having learned through the indiscretion of a domestic servant that Verrall had conveyed Nettie to the quiet coastal village of Shaphambury, Leadford entered a dim pawnbroker’s establishment on the High Street. Without a tremor of hesitation, he laid down his meager savings upon the scarred wooden counter and purchased a six-chambered revolver of thirty-two caliber, together with a small pasteboard box of brass cartridges. He examined the cylinder with the cold precision of an engineer, noting the alignment of the chambers and the tension of the spring. The instrument was compact, reliable, and deadly. Slipping the weapon into the pocket of his heavy woollen coat, he strode toward the railway station, resolved to intercept the fugitives before the night closed in.

The express train to the eastern coast devoured the miles with mechanical fury, its locomotive trailing a dense plume of steam across the English Midlands. Seated in the corner of a third-class carriage, Leadford stared out at the passing hedgerows, his fingers resting continually upon the cold butt of the revolver. The landscape itself appeared subtly altered beneath the noon sky. Though no clouds obscured the sun, the light possessed a peculiar, coppery tint, as if filtered through a vast pane of smoked glass. Within the compartment, three commercial travelers conversed in hushed, anxious tones, debating both the trajectory of the approaching comet—which was due to reach its nearest proximity to the Earth’s orbit within twenty-four hours—and the mobilization of the North Sea Fleet.

By four o'clock in the afternoon, the train reached the coastal terminus. Stepping onto the wooden platform, Leadford was immediately struck by the oppressive, stagnant quality of the maritime air. Not a breath of wind stirred the surface of the salt marshes; the sea gulls crouched silently upon the shingle, refusing to take wing. Passing the stationmaster’s office, Leadford chanced to glance at the mercurial barometer mounted beside the door: the column had plummeted to twenty-eight inches, an atmospheric depression of extraordinary magnitude, yet the sky remained ominously clear. Undeterred by these portents of nature, he inquired the direction of the Shaphambury cliffs and set off along the chalky road at a rapid, determined pace.

Twilight was descending rapidly when Leadford reached the summit of the white cliffs overlooking the German Ocean. Below him, the sea lay motionless, resembling an immense sheet of polished lead. Far out upon the eastern horizon, three dark silhouettes broke the line of water and sky—heavy ironclad cruisers steaming in line ahead. Even as Leadford watched, a brilliant flash of orange light erupted from the forward turret of the leading vessel, followed several seconds later by the dull, rolling concussion of a twelve-inch naval gun. The great maritime conflict had commenced.

Yet Leadford scarcely heeded the distant thunder of the artillery. Ahead of him, winding along the narrow footpath that skirted the precipice, walked two figures. He recognized them instantaneously: Verrall, attired in a light tweed traveling suit, and Nettie, her hand resting lightly upon his arm. A surge of hot, blinding rage flooded Leadford’s veins, sweeping away every vestige of caution. Stepping briskly from behind a clump of gorse, he planted himself squarely in their path and drew the revolver from his pocket.

"Halt!" Leadford cried, his voice harsh and unfamiliar in the heavy air.

Nettie uttered a sharp gasp of terror and recoiled, clutching at Verrall’s sleeve. Verrall himself stood motionless, his face pale yet remarkably composed as he measured the distance between them and the gleaming barrel of the pistol. Before a single word of explanation or defiance could be uttered, however, an extraordinary phenomenon arrested all three in their tracks.

From the zenith of the darkening sky, the comet had suddenly expanded until its luminous green tail occupied more than half of the celestial vault. At that precise instant, the outermost envelope of the celestial body entered the upper strata of the Earth’s atmosphere. A silent, majestic cataract of emerald vapor appeared to pour downward from the heavens, extinguishing the first stars and swallowing the distant flashes of the naval battle in a luminous fog.

Within seconds, the green mist enveloped the cliffs of Shaphambury. It was neither cold nor hot, yet it possessed a distinct, sweetish odor, faintly reminiscent of ozone and crushed almonds. Leadford attempted to steady his aim, determined to execute his vengeance before the darkness became absolute, but an inexplicable lethargy seized his muscles. His arm grew heavy as lead; the revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers and fell noiselessly into the turf. Beside him, Nettie swayed like a reed in a gentle current and sank softly to the ground, her eyes closing as if in tranquil slumber. Verrall staggered one pace forward, reached out blindly toward the falling girl, and collapsed beside her without a groan. Leadford felt his pulse slow to a gentle, rhythmic cadence; his eyelids drooped, and he too yielded to the irresistible slumber. Across the entire hemisphere, from the trenches of the continent to the decks of the ironclads, the green gas performed its silent work, plunging every living creature into a profound, dreamless suspension of the vital faculties.

When consciousness returned to William Leadford, the world had undergone a transformation more profound than any cataclysm recorded in the annals of geology. He opened his eyes to a dawn of indescribable purity. The green vapor had entirely dissipated, absorbed into the terrestrial atmosphere, leaving the air extraordinarily brisk, transparent, and invigorating to the lungs. Sitting up upon the dew-drenched grass, Leadford experienced a sensation of absolute mental lucidity, as though a thick, distorting veil had been torn forever from his intellect.

Beside his knee lay the thirty-two caliber revolver. He picked it up, examining the steel cylinder with mild curiosity, utterly unable to comprehend the savage, irrational fury that had driven him to purchase such an instrument of destruction. With a calm, deliberate motion, he tossed the weapon over the edge of the cliff, watching it vanish into the clear, rippling waters far below. Out upon the horizon, the three ironclad cruisers lay motionless upon the sunlit sea; their guns were silent, and from the masthead of each vessel fluttered a white flag of parley.

Turning back to the path, Leadford saw Nettie and Verrall stirring into wakefulness. As their eyes met his, there was neither fear in their countenances nor resentment in his heart. He knelt beside them, extending a hand to help them rise. In that quiet morning hour, beneath a sky purged of both storm and malice, the three stood together in silent accord, ready to descend into the valley and begin the reconstruction of a newly awakened world.
