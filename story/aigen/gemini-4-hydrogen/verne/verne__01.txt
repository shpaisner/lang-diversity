In the heart of Staffordshire, where the earth is scarred by deep coal-shafts and the horizon is perpetually obscured by the dense, carbonaceous exhalations of a hundred blast-furnaces, the town of Clayton lay beneath a canopy of soot. It was the autumn of that most extraordinary year, and two phenomena simultaneously occupied the minds of the populace: the imminent rupture of European peace and the terrifying apparition in the northern heavens. William Leadford, a young clerk of meager means whose weekly stipend scarcely exceeded twenty shillings, walked briskly along the cobbled thoroughfare toward his modest lodgings. His brow was furrowed, not by the telegraphic dispatches from Berlin and Vienna that shouted of mobilization from every newsstand, but by a feverish, personal agitation concerning Nettie Stuart, the sole bright light in his otherwise dreary existence. Yet even William could not entirely ignore the celestial visitor. High above the glowing chimneys of the Potteries, the Great Comet—first observed by astronomers at the Paris Observatory three months prior—now blazed with an intensity that rivaled the full moon. Its nucleus, a dazzling point of emerald fire, trailed a luminous tail spanning thirty degrees of the celestial vault. Scientific journals had already calculated that the Earth, traveling along its orbital path at eighteen miles per second, would soon intersect this gaseous appendage. To the ironworkers and potters of Clayton, however, the green star was merely an omen of the continental war whose ironclads were already steaming toward the North Sea.

When William reached the small, brick-built cottage on the outskirts of Clayton, he found not the warm welcome he had anticipated, but a silence as cold as the granite flagstones beneath his boots. Upon the deal table lay a single sheet of paper, folded with cruel precision. In trembling, hurried strokes, Nettie had written that she could no longer endure the narrow, smoky confines of Staffordshire. She had departed with Edward Verrall, the young heir to the Checkley Collieries, whose vast fortune had procured for him one of the newest mechanical marvels of the age: a French-built Panhard-Levassor motor-carriage, capable of achieving forty miles an hour upon the macadamized highroads. A terrible, blinding rage seized William Leadford's heart. Methodical even in his despair, he did not waste a moment in idle lamentation. Breaking open a locked drawer, he withdrew a small, six-chambered Belgian revolver and a box of cartridges, thrusting them into the pocket of his threadbare overcoat. Next, he consulted a soiled copy of Bradshaw’s Railway Guide. Verrall possessed a summer villa on the East Coast, at the quiet watering-place of Shaphambury. By calculating the gradients and junctions, William determined that the 6:42 express from Crewe would carry him across the breadth of England before midnight. Ten minutes later, he was seated in a jolting third-class carriage, his eyes fixed upon the darkening landscape as the locomotive devoured the miles at a steady fifty miles per hour, its iron flanks bathed in the eerie, verdant glow of the ascending comet.

The coastal town of Shaphambury presented a spectacle of feverish commotion when William descended onto the railway platform. Though the hour was late, the streets were thronged with anxious citizens, and the telegraph office blazed with gaslight. England was mobilizing. Out in the dark waters of the North Sea, barely three leagues from the shore, lay a formidable squadron of the Channel Fleet—massive steel leviathans displacing fifteen thousand tons apiece, their searchlights sweeping the horizon like giant luminous fingers. From the promenade, one could already hear the distant, ominous rumble of heavy ordnance as coastal batteries tested their range. Newsboys raced through the crowds, crying out the latest dispatches: an ultimatum had expired; an enemy cruiser had been sighted off the Dogger Bank; war was no longer a dread possibility, but an immediate, mechanical certainty. William Leadford, however, remained entirely impervious to the fate of empires. His universe had shrunk to the dimensions of a single vendetta. Moving swiftly through the bustling thoroughfares, he scanned the facades of the seaside hotels until, outside the Grand Hotel, his eyes fell upon a mud-splashed Panhard-Levassor motor-carriage, its brass lamps still warm. A brief inquiry of a distracted porter confirmed his suspicions: Mr. Verrall and a young lady had dined there an hour before and had walked out toward the chalk cliffs to observe the celestial phenomenon. Gripping the cold butt of his revolver, William turned toward the sea, where the green comet now hung directly overhead, illuminating the foaming breakers with a ghastly, phosphorescent pallor.

The wind blew fresh from the northeast, carrying the sharp tang of brine and the faint, sulfurous odor of coal-smoke from the warships anchored in the roadstead. William ascended the winding path that led to the summit of the cliffs, his footsteps muffled by the thick turf. Ahead of him, silhouetted against the luminescent sky, walked two figures. He recognized them instantly: the tall, broad-shouldered frame of Verrall, clad in a heavy motoring coat, and beside him, Nettie, her shawl drawn tightly around her shoulders. They paused near the edge of the escarpment, looking out toward the black hulls of the ironclads. William crept closer, sheltering behind a weathered timber breakwater. He heard Verrall’s confident, careless laughter, followed by Nettie’s soft, anxious reply regarding the distant thunder of the fleet. At the sound of her voice, a paroxysm of jealousy convulsed William’s frame. He stepped boldly from his concealment, his right hand extended, the steel barrel of the Belgian revolver gleaming with a pale green reflection. "Verrall!" he cried, his voice hoarse and strained above the roaring surf. Both figures spun around in alarm, Nettie uttering a sharp gasp of terror. William leveled the weapon at his rival’s chest, his finger tightening upon the trigger. At that precise instant, the barometer at the Shaphambury observatory must have plunged a full inch. A tremendous, silent flash of emerald light split the zenith, and a strange, whistling wind, entirely unlike any terrestrial gale, swept down from the upper strata of the atmosphere.

The Earth had plunged directly into the gaseous envelope of the Great Comet. What had appeared from a distance as a mere luminous vapor now revealed itself as a dense, palpable fog of an exquisite emerald hue, descending swiftly from the stratosphere to envelop the globe. Out in the North Sea, the distant booming of the naval artillery abruptly ceased, as if a giant hand had muffled the mouths of the cannons. The powerful electric searchlights of the battleships flickered, dimmed, and were swallowed entirely by the verdant mist. Upon the cliffside, William Leadford found himself paralyzed. The revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers, falling silently onto the grass. He attempted to draw a breath to shout his defiance, yet his lungs filled not with the familiar mixture of oxygen and nitrogen, but with a cool, sweet-scented miasma—a subtle, unknown gas that seemed to permeate every fiber of his being. A profound, irresistible lethargy stole over his limbs. Looking through the swirling green haze, he saw Nettie sink gently to her knees, her head resting against Verrall’s shoulder, while the young millionaire himself swayed and collapsed softly beside her upon the turf. There was no pain, no sensation of suffocation; rather, it was as though the entire turbulent mechanism of human existence were being gently wound down to a halt. Within minutes, the great cities of Europe, the marching armies, the steaming fleets, and the three silent figures upon the English coast were alike submerged beneath a luminous, tranquil sea of green sleep.

Dawn broke over Shaphambury, not with the accustomed crimson of an English morning, but with a crystalline, silver clarity never before witnessed by mortal eyes. The green vapor had vanished, absorbed entirely into the soil and the waters of the ocean, leaving behind an atmosphere of astonishing purity. William Leadford opened his eyes. For several moments he lay motionless upon the turf, gazing upward into a sky of unfathomable azure. Then, slowly, he sat up. A miraculous transformation had occurred within the secret chambers of his brain. The blinding, murderous obsession that had driven him across England like a madman had utterly evaporated, leaving behind an immense, luminous lucidity. It was not a sudden conversion of the soul, but a physiological cleansing, as though the comet’s strange gas had purged the human nervous system of its ancient, savage fevers. Beside him, the Belgian revolver lay forgotten in the dew; William regarded it with the detached curiosity one might bestow upon a relic of the Stone Age. A few paces away, Nettie and Verrall were stirring, rising slowly to their feet and looking about them with the bewildered, clear-eyed wonder of awakened children. Out in the bay, the great ironclads lay motionless upon a glassy sea, their battle flags lowered, their crews crowding the rails in silent, peaceful contemplation. William stood up and walked steadily toward his former rival and his lost love—not as an avenger, nor even as a suppliant, but as a fellow citizen of a newly awakened world, extending his hand in the quiet light of a transformed dawn.
