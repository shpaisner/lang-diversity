The kilns of Staffordshire burned with their customary, sullen defiance against the heavens, yet even the dense pall of bituminous smoke could not obscure the portentous spectacle suspended above the Midlands. For three weeks, the Great Comet had dominated the nocturnal firmament, a celestial wanderer whose parabolic trajectory was carrying it directly across the path of the terrestrial globe. Astronomers at Greenwich and Paris, peering through their equatorial refractors, had measured its coma at forty thousand miles in diameter, its sweeping tail glowing with a peculiar, monochromatic emerald light that baffled the spectroscope.

Below this silent marvel, Europe was descending into chaos. In the narrow, soot-stained streets of Clayton, William Leadford purchased an evening gazette, his rough fingers trembling with a fury that owed little to the international crisis. The headlines shrieked of ultimatums delivered in Vienna and Berlin, of railway lines requisitioned for troop movements, and of mighty ironclad fleets steaming toward the North Sea. Yet Leadford scarcely registered the impending catastrophe of nations. His entire universe had contracted to a single, burning point of betrayal: Nettie, the woman he had loved with all the fierce, narrow devotion of his impoverished youth, had fled with Edward Verrall, the son of a wealthy colliery owner.

In the cramped parlor of his mother’s cottage, Leadford completed his preparations. He possessed four pounds in silver, a third-class railway timetable, and a six-chambered revolver of Belgian manufacture, which he oiled with mechanical precision. While monarchs and ministers prepared to hurl millions of men into the furnace of war, Leadford was determined to execute his own private, inexorable justice upon the shores of the eastern coast.

At nine o'clock that evening, the express locomotive thundered out of the junction, its boiler roaring under a pressure of one hundred and eighty pounds to the square inch. Seated in the corner of a dimly lit third-class compartment, William Leadford watched the dark silhouettes of hedgerows and telegraph poles rush past in a dizzying blur. Along those copper wires, he knew, urgent dispatches were flashing at the speed of light, carrying orders that would mobilize the reserve battalions of three empires before dawn.

Above the speeding train, the comet had risen higher, occupying nearly thirty degrees of the celestial vault. Its greenish luminosity bathed the English countryside in an unearthly, theatrical twilight, transforming the rivers into ribbons of pale jade and casting sharp, unfamiliar shadows across the carriage floor. Inside the compartment, two commercial travelers debated the naval situation with grim authority, calculating the displacement and armament of the rival battleships currently maneuvering off the Dogger Bank.

Leadford said nothing. He kept his right hand buried deep within the pocket of his heavy wool coat, his thumb resting against the checkered walnut grip of the revolver. In his fevered imagination, the rhythmic clatter of the iron wheels upon the steel rails seemed to repeat a single, maddening refrain: Nettie and Verrall, Nettie and Verrall. He pictured them laughing together in some fashionable seaside promenade, utterly indifferent to the misery he endured in the smoky valleys of Staffordshire, and indifferent, too, to the vast, iron machinery of war grinding into motion across the continent. By the time the locomotive hissed to a halt at the coastal terminus of Sheringham, the sky had begun to turn a strange, sulphurous green.

The salt air of the North Sea struck Leadford’s face with bracing intensity as he stepped onto the deserted platform. Sheringham was a quiet town of flint cottages and steep chalk cliffs, yet tonight it wore the aspect of a fortress under siege. Down upon the esplanade, clusters of anxious townsfolk had gathered, their faces upturned toward the horizon. Far out upon the dark expanse of waters, three miles from the shore, the pale beams of electric searchlights swept nervously across the waves, revealing the formidable, low-lying silhouettes of British cruisers patrolling the approaches to the coast.

Ignoring the murmured speculations of the crowd, Leadford set out with purposeful strides along the cliffside road. Through a series of terse, persistent inquiries at the railway inn and the local cab stand, he had traced the fugitives to a secluded boarding-house perched above the western shingle. The wind was rising from the northeast, driving low scuds of cloud across the face of the comet, whose brilliance had now intensified to such a degree that one could easily read the finest newsprint by its light alone.

As Leadford approached the whitewashed villa, he paused beside a low stone parapet to inspect his weapon. The six brass cartridges gleamed faintly in the emerald gloom. Somewhere out to sea, a dull, concussive thud vibrated through the sole of his boots—a distant warning shot, perhaps, or the first exchange of heavy naval artillery. The madness of the world seemed to have reached its zenith, and Leadford felt himself a mere instrument of that universal violence. Adjusting his collar against the chill breeze, he descended the steep, winding path toward the beach, where two figures could be dimly seen walking beside the breaking surf.

The tide was receding across the wet pebbles, leaving behind glistening pools that mirrored the immense, spectral fan of the comet’s tail. Nettie and Verrall walked slowly, unaware of the solitary figure advancing upon them from the shadow of the chalk cliffs. Leadford quickened his pace, his boots crunching loudly upon the shingle. At the sound, Nettie turned, and even in that strange, verdant light, Leadford saw the sudden, unmistakable pallor of terror overspread her features.

"William!" she cried, instinctively clutching Verrall’s arm. "How did you find us here?"

Verrall, to his credit, did not recoil. Drawing himself up to his full height, the young gentleman stepped slightly forward, placing himself between Nettie and the intruder. "This is an intolerable intrusion, Leadford," he said, his voice trembling slightly despite his efforts to maintain an aristocratic composure. "You have no claim upon her. Leave us in peace, or I shall summon the coastguard."

"Your coastguard has weightier matters to attend to tonight, Mr. Verrall," Leadford replied, his voice dropping to a cold, metallic register. He drew the revolver from his pocket and leveled the blued steel barrel directly at Verrall’s chest. "You took what belonged to me, just as your class has always taken the bread, the air, and the very lives of men like me. Tonight, the accounts shall be balanced."

Nettie let out a piercing shriek, throwing herself before Verrall with outstretched hands. "No, William, for God's sake! Do not do this dreadful thing!"

Leadford’s jaw tightened. A roaring filled his ears, drowning out the crash of the waves and the frantic pleadings of the woman he had loved. He shifted his aim slightly, his forefinger applying a steady, inexorable pressure to the trigger.

At that precise instant, a blinding flash of orange light tore across the eastern horizon, followed by the heavy thunder of twelve-inch naval guns. The war had begun. Yet even as the echoes of that cannonade rolled across the cliffs of Norfolk, an extraordinary phenomenon arrested Leadford’s hand.

The northeast wind ceased abruptly, as though a colossal glass dome had been lowered over the island. The restless sea flattened into an oily expanse. Looking upward in awe, the three figures beheld the zenith split open in a cascade of dazzling, emerald fire. The Earth had entered the outer envelope of the comet’s tail.

A dense, luminous vapor, resembling neither fog nor smoke, descended rapidly through the upper atmosphere. Within moments, it enveloped the cliffs, the town, and the distant ironclads in a glowing, translucent mist. Leadford braced himself for suffocation or instant incineration, yet as he drew a breath, he experienced only an exquisite coolness. The vapor carried a faint, indescribable aroma, reminiscent of ozone after a summer storm and crushed laurel leaves.

Suddenly, an irresistible lethargy seeped into Leadford’s marrow. The murderous tension in his forearm dissolved. His fingers uncurled, and the heavy Belgian revolver dropped onto the wet pebbles with a dull clatter. Looking sideways through heavy eyelids, he saw Verrall sink slowly to his knees, his face devoid of anger, while Nettie gently crumpled beside him. A profound, dreamless slumber, sweet and absolute, claimed them all, even as the guns out upon the North Sea fell silent.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the emerald mist had vanished, and the rising sun poured a flood of pure, golden light across the eastern sea. He sat up slowly, expecting the familiar ache of despair. Instead, he drew a breath of air so crisp and exhilarating that it seemed the very chemical composition of the atmosphere had been perfected during the night.

His mind felt like a dusty lens wiped clean. Across the shingle, Nettie and Verrall were stirring from their slumber. They sat up, blinking in the morning light, and looked directly at him. There was no hatred in Leadford’s heart—not a single ember of the dark, possessive fury that had driven him across England. He understood, with sudden and breathtaking clarity, how foolish his jealousy had been, how absurd it was to seek to own another human soul through violence.

"William," Nettie murmured, her voice filled not with terror, but with a gentle, wondering pity. "What has happened to the world?"

They all turned their gaze toward the horizon. The great grey cruisers still floated three miles out upon a tranquil, sapphire sea, yet no black smoke poured from their funnels, and their searchlights were extinguished. From the direction of the town, the church bells began to ring—not with the frantic clanging of an alarm, but in a slow, harmonious peal of peace. Leadford picked up the revolver from the pebbles and emptied the six brass cartridges into his palm. With an unhurried motion, he cast both the weapon and the shells far out into the receding tide, then rose to greet his companions as equals in the dawn of a newly awakened earth.
