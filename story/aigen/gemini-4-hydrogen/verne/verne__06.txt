In the autumn of that memorable year, the industrial district of Staffordshire presented a spectacle of unrelieved gloom. Beneath a canopy of bituminous smoke belched forth by a hundred blast furnaces, the town of Clayton lay crouched amidst its slag heaps and brick kilns. Here dwelt William Leadford, a young man of twenty-two years, employed as a clerk at a modest stipend of eighteen shillings a week. His life, circumscribed by the ledger and the soot-blackened walls of his mother’s cottage, was one of bitter privation. Yet, even in this dismal corner of England, men were compelled to lift their eyes toward the heavens, for a celestial visitor of unprecedented magnitude was rushing toward the Earth. First observed through the great equatorial refractor at Greenwich as a faint nebulosity in the constellation of Orion, the comet had, within six weeks, attained a brilliance that outshone Venus. Spectroscopic analysis conducted by the foremost astronomers of Europe revealed a troubling anomaly: the comet’s coma and its immense, bifurcated tail were composed not merely of incandescent carbon and hydrogen, but of an unknown gaseous element emitting a vivid, emerald-green line in the spectrum. Meanwhile, upon the terrestrial globe, human passions burned with a feverish intensity no less ominous. Despatches from Berlin, Paris, and London spoke of mobilized army corps, of ironclads coaling in haste, and of diplomatic ruptures that threatened to plunge the entire continent into a mechanized war of annihilation. Leadford, however, cared little for the maneuvers of cabinets or the fate of empires; his mind was consumed by a more immediate, personal despair.

Nettie Stuart, the daughter of a local gardener, had been the solitary light in Leadford’s bleak existence. For two years they had walked together along the cinder paths beside the canal, exchanging vows of eternal fidelity. Then had come Edward Verrall, the son of a wealthy colliery proprietor, whose polished manners and motor-car represented a world utterly inaccessible to a humble clerk. When Nettie vanished from Clayton without a word of farewell, leaving only a brief, trembling note confessing her flight with Verrall to the southern coast, Leadford’s grief crystallized into a cold, homicidal resolve. Utilizing his meager savings—money painfully accumulated for their modest wedding—he purchased a five-chambered revolver of Belgian manufacture and a box of central-fire cartridges. On a Tuesday afternoon, he boarded the southward-bound express at the junction, taking his place in a third-class carriage crowded with silent, anxious passengers. As the locomotive thundered across the Midlands at fifty miles an hour, Leadford stared blindly out the window at the flying telegraph poles. At every station, newsboys hawked special editions announcing that the British Channel Fleet had steamed out of Portsmouth under sealed orders, and that artillery exchanges had already commenced along the Belgian frontier. Europe was tearing itself asunder, yet Leadford felt only the hard, metallic weight of the revolver resting against his ribs. By nightfall, he had reached the coast of Kent, and after a tedious journey on a branch line, he arrived at the quiet seaside hamlet of Shere, where his inquiries at the railway inn quickly confirmed that a gentleman matching Verrall’s description had leased a secluded bungalow upon the cliffs.

Night had fallen, yet there was no darkness. Above the English Channel, the great comet now spanned an arc of nearly forty-five degrees across the celestial vault, bathing the chalk headlands and the restless sea in a cold, phosphorescent, emerald radiance. It was a light so intense that Leadford could easily consult his pocket watch without striking a match, though it lent a spectral, cadaverous hue to his pale features. He concealed himself amidst a thicket of gorse some two hundred yards from the bungalow, a modest wooden structure overlooking the sea. Through the uncurtained parlor windows, he could discern the silhouettes of Nettie and Verrall seated beside a lamp-lit table. The sight ignited a fresh paroxysm of jealousy within his breast, yet he forced himself to wait for the dawn, determined that his vengeance should be executed in the full light of day. Far out upon the southern horizon, the night was punctuated by sudden, sharp flashes of orange flame, followed seconds later by the dull, heavy reverberation of twelve-inch naval guns. Searchlights from unseen cruisers swept the misty expanse of the Channel like luminous blades, probing the darkness for torpedo boats. Even as the two greatest maritime powers on Earth locked in mortal combat upon the waves, the atmospheric barometer in the coastal stations was plunging with alarming rapidity. A strange, oppressive stillness settled over the cliffs, broken only by the distant thunder of the naval engagement and the soft, rhythmic wash of the tide below. Leadford drew the Belgian revolver from his coat pocket, carefully inspected the cylinder, and waited for the morning.

At four o'clock in the morning, the eastern sky did not redden with the accustomed hues of dawn; instead, the green luminescence of the comet swelled until it seemed to fill the entire firmament. According to the calculations of the observatories, the Earth was now passing directly through the outer envelope of the cometary tail, a vast, tenuous cloud of gas extending millions of miles into space. Suddenly, the doors of the bungalow were thrown open. Verrall and Nettie emerged onto the cliff path, carrying valises, evidently alarmed by the escalating cannonade at sea and intending to seek the railway station inland. Leadford stepped out from the gorse, blocking their narrow path, the revolver held steady in his right hand. Nettie uttered a piercing cry, clutching Verrall’s arm, while the young gentleman froze, his face blanched in the eerie emerald glare. Leadford raised the barrel, aiming directly at Verrall’s chest, his finger tightening upon the trigger. At that precise instant, a phenomenon of unparalleled grandeur arrested his hand. A rushing sound, resembling the sudden escape of steam from a thousand colossal boilers, swept down from the zenith. The air grew thick, heavy, and violently agitated, carrying a faint, sweetish odor not unlike that of bruised almonds and ozone. Before Leadford could discharge his weapon, a dense, luminous green vapor descended upon the cliffs like a solid curtain. His lungs labored for oxygen; a profound vertigo seized his brain; the revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers, and he sank unconscious into the damp grass beside his intended victims.

During the three hours that followed, the planet Earth experienced a suspension of its turbulent history. The cometary gas, mingling with the terrestrial atmosphere, acted as a swift, universal anesthetic upon every living creature. Across the entire hemisphere, from the soot-stained potteries of Staffordshire to the crowded boulevards of Paris and the fortified plains of Prussia, human activity ceased instantaneously. In the North Sea, the dreadnoughts of the opposing fleets drifted helplessly upon the swell, their gun crews slumped over the breeches of their hot cannons, their stokers unconscious before the dying fires of the boilers. Express trains hurtled onward until their steam pressure failed, coming to gentle, silent halts in the midst of quiet pastures. In London, the roar of traffic vanished, replaced by an eerie, emerald-tinted silence as millions slumbered upon the pavements and within their chambers. Yet this strange asphyxiation was neither lethal nor destructive; rather, by some mysterious chemical affinity, the cometary element was rapidly absorbed into the bloodstreams of all sentient beings, oxidizing the toxins of fatigue and altering the very equilibrium of the nervous system. Upon the Kentish cliffs, William Leadford, Nettie Stuart, and Edward Verrall lay motionless within ten paces of one another, the green mist swirling gently over their still forms. Above them, the great comet passed its perihelion and began its long, parabolic retreat into the frigid depths of outer space, leaving behind a world bathed in a transformed and purified atmosphere.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the green vapor had entirely vanished. Above him arched a sky of an extraordinary, crystalline azure, purged of every trace of smoke or haze. He sat up slowly, expecting the familiar constriction of hatred in his chest, the bitter ache of his wounded pride. To his profound astonishment, he felt nothing of the sort. His mind possessed an unprecedented lucidity, as though a dark, suffocating veil had been lifted from his intellect. He looked down at the Belgian revolver lying amidst the dew-soaked clover; the instrument of murder appeared to him now as an absurd, barbarous relic of a vanished madness. A few yards away, Nettie and Verrall were also stirring, rising to their knees and gazing about them with expressions of bewildered wonder. Leadford rose to his feet and walked toward them, not with the stride of an avenger, but with the calm bearing of a fellow traveler. When Verrall saw him, there was no flinching, no posture of defense; instead, he extended his hand, and Leadford clasped it firmly. Nettie looked from one to the other, tears of quiet comprehension glistening in her eyes, and spoke softly of the foolishness that had driven them all. Together, the three walked to the edge of the chalk cliff and looked out across the Channel. Far below, the mighty ironclads floated peacefully upon the sparkling blue water, their signal flags hanging limp, their guns forever silenced. The morning sun rose warmly over a quiet, rational, and newly awakened Earth.
