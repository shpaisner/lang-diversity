In the autumn of that memorable year, the industrial district of Staffordshire presented a spectacle of gloomy labor beneath a sky doubly troubled. Throughout the Potteries, tall brick chimneys belched their ceaseless columns of bituminous smoke, obscuring the sun by day and reflecting the dull crimson of the blast furnaces by night. Among the thousands of clerks and artisans who toiled within this soot-stained labyrinth was William Leadford, a young man of twenty-four years, whose meager salary scarcely sufficed to keep body and soul together in his mother’s damp cottage. Yet Leadford possessed an ardent, restless intellect, sharpened by evening lectures and radical pamphlets, which rendered his poverty all the more bitter.

Two portentous events then occupied the public mind. Across the English Channel, the great powers of Europe were sliding inexorably toward the abyss of war; every morning’s telegraph brought fresh dispatches of mobilized regiments and ironclad squadrons steaming into the North Sea. Simultaneously, astronomers at the Greenwich and Paris observatories had confirmed the rapid approach of an extraordinary comet. First observed as a faint nebulous speck in the constellation of Aquarius, it now blazed nightly with an eerie, emerald-green luminescence, its spectroscopic analysis revealing vast quantities of an unknown, volatile gas.

Upon Leadford, however, these cosmic and political convulsions made little impression compared to a domestic catastrophe. Returning one evening from the counting-house, he found upon his table a brief note written in a trembling hand. Nettie Stuart—the young woman to whom he had pledged his future—had fled. She had abandoned the bleak monotony of Staffordshire for the wealth and refinement of Edward Verrall, the scion of a prominent local family. Blinded by humiliation and a fierce, possessive rage, Leadford swore a terrible oath of vengeance.

The following morning, Leadford expended the greater part of his slender savings at a gunsmith’s shop in Birmingham, procuring a cheap, six-chambered revolver and a box of cartridges. Concealing the weapon within the pocket of his frayed greatcoat, he purchased a third-class railway ticket for the Lincolnshire coast, having learned through a careless servant that Verrall had conveyed Nettie to a secluded seaside bungalow near Shaphambury.

The journey eastward was fraught with an atmosphere of feverish agitation. At every junction, the iron rails hummed beneath the weight of troop trains rushing toward the southern ports. Through the soot-streaked carriage windows, Leadford observed artillery batteries being loaded onto flatcars, while newsboys ran along the platforms shouting the latest bulletins of diplomatic rupture. The British fleet had been ordered to clear for action; a naval engagement was hourly expected.

Yet, as twilight descended over the flat fens of Lincolnshire, the terrestrial tumult seemed to pale before the majesty of the heavens. The comet had grown to terrifying proportions. Its incandescent nucleus hung high in the eastern sky, trailing a sweeping, fan-shaped tail that spanned nearly forty degrees of the celestial vault. This appendage did not emit the pale, silvery light of ordinary comets, but rather a vivid, phosphorescent green that bathed the marshlands and solitary farmsteads in an uncanny glow. Inside the rattling railway carriage, Leadford’s fellow passengers stared upward in superstitious dread, whispering of ancient prophecies and the end of days. Leadford alone remained indifferent to the celestial prodigy; his mind, fixed upon a single, murderous purpose, burned with a dark and solitary fire.

Upon alighting at the coastal terminus, Leadford struck out on foot along the wind-swept dunes, guided by the salty tang of the North Sea and the distant roar of the surf. The hour was late, yet the darkness was dispelled by the extraordinary radiance of the comet, which now occupied nearly half the firmament. A strange stillness hung over the earth, broken only by the occasional rattle of pebbles beneath his boots. In his pocket, he carried a small aneroid barometer he had once won as a school prize; glancing at its dial by the green starlight, he noted with passing curiosity that the needle had dropped precipitously, indicating an atmospheric disturbance of unprecedented magnitude.

Far out upon the dark waters, a line of faint, winking lights betrayed the presence of the Channel Fleet. Through the crisp night air came the faint, rhythmic throb of marine engines and the occasional sweep of electric searchlights cutting across the horizon. At any moment, the heavy guns of those floating fortresses might open fire, inaugurating a conflict that would drench the continent in blood.

Leadford turned his back upon the sea and approached a cluster of timbered bungalows nestled behind a sandy bluff. There, walking slowly along the cliff path, he beheld the two figures he sought. Verrall, attired in a light tweed suit, had his arm cast protectively about Nettie’s shoulders, while she looked up at the luminous green sky with an expression of mingled wonder and apprehension. A surge of volcanic fury seized Leadford’s heart. Drawing the revolver from his pocket, he stepped from the shadow of the marram grass, blocking their path along the narrow ridge.

"Stop!" Leadford cried, his voice harsh and unnatural in the breathless quiet of the night.

Nettie uttered a sharp exclamation of terror, clinging to Verrall’s arm, while the young gentleman stepped forward instinctively to shield her. "Leadford!" Verrall exclaimed, his eyes widening as they fell upon the gleaming barrel of the pistol. "Put that weapon down, man. Have you lost your senses?"

"You have stolen all that made life endurable to me," Leadford replied, his finger tightening upon the trigger as he advanced a pace. "Now, you shall both answer for it."

Before he could fire, however, a phenomenon of sublime and terrifying grandeur intervened. From the upper strata of the atmosphere came a sudden, rushing sound, like the roar of a mighty cataract. The earth had entered the gaseous envelope of the comet. A dense, luminous green vapor, smelling faintly of sweet almonds and ozone, swept down from the zenith, extinguishing the stars and enveloping the coastline in an impenetrable emerald fog.

Leadford gasped, his lungs filling with the strange, cool gas. He attempted to raise his arm, to squeeze the trigger, to consummate his vengeance—but an irresistible languor seized his limbs. A pistol shot rang out, wild and harmless, its report muffled by the thickening air. Verrall staggered backward, clutching at his throat, while Nettie sank gently onto the turf as if overcome by a sudden slumber. Leadford’s knees buckled beneath him; the revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers and vanished into the sand. Within thirty seconds, all three lay motionless upon the cliff, wrapped in a profound, dreamless insensibility, while across the globe, the armies of Europe and the crews of the great ironclads succumbed to the same mysterious, universal sleep.

How many hours elapsed during that celestial visitation, Leadford could never precisely ascertain. When he at length opened his eyes, the emerald mist had entirely dissipated, and the eastern horizon was aglow with the purest, most crystalline dawn he had ever beheld. The sun had not yet risen, yet the sky was a vault of immaculate sapphire, devoid of the slightest trace of haze or smoke.

Leadford sat up, drawing a deep breath. To his astonishment, his head felt remarkably clear; the dull ache that had plagued him for months in the smoky air of Staffordshire had vanished, replaced by a sensation of extraordinary physical vigor. More astonishing still was the transformation within his mind. He looked back upon his actions of the previous night—his bitter jealousy, his murderous pursuit, the revolver lying a few paces away in the sand—and regarded them with the detached wonder of a man waking from a fevered delirium. The dark miasma of hatred had been purged from his soul, as cleanly as the comet’s breath had cleansed the terrestrial atmosphere.

A few yards distant, Verrall was stirring, raising himself upon one elbow and gazing about with an expression of profound bewilderment. Beside him, Nettie sat up, brushing the sand from her skirts, her face illuminated by the gentle roseate light of the approaching sun. Leadford rose to his feet and walked toward them, his step light and unhurried. There was no menace in his bearing, no tremor in his voice, as he spoke.

"Are you unharmed?" he asked quietly.

Verrall stared at him for a long moment, his own features softening as the clarity of the new morning took possession of his faculties. "Quite unharmed, Leadford," he replied, extending a hand. "Good heavens... what has happened to us?"

"The comet has passed," Leadford said, looking up at the fading, silvery-green trail that still lingered faintly in the western sky. "And I believe we have survived the storm."

He walked back to where the revolver lay half-buried in the turf, picked it up by the barrel, and cast it far over the cliff edge into the receding tide below. Neither Nettie nor Verrall flinched at the gesture; they watched the weapon disappear into the foam with a quiet, mutual understanding. For several minutes, the three stood together upon the headland, conversing without rancor or embarrassment. The petty barriers of class, the fierce, possessive instincts that had driven Leadford to madness and Verrall to arrogance, seemed utterly trivial in the light of this serene awakening. Nettie wept softly, not from fear, but from a sudden, overwhelming realization of how narrow and blind their former lives had been.

Presently, their attention was drawn toward the sea. The sun had now cleared the horizon, flooding the North Sea with golden light. Out upon the tranquil waters, the great ironclad cruisers of the fleet lay motionless at anchor. No black smoke poured from their funnels; no thunder of artillery shattered the morning peace. As Leadford watched, a string of brightly colored signal flags rose slowly to the masthead of the flagship—the universal maritime signal of peace and recall.

Far across the continent, Leadford knew with an unerring intuition, millions of men were waking in the trenches and the cities to the same luminous sanity. Turning away from the sea, William Leadford walked down the cliff path beside Nettie and Verrall, ready to take his place in the construction of a rational, united world.
