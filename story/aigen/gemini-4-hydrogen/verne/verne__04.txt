In the smoky heart of Staffordshire, where the blast furnaces of the Black Country belch their perpetual pillars of fire against the firmament, William Leadford walked with a heavy heart and an empty purse. He was a young man of twenty-two, a clerk in a pottery manufactory, condemned by the rigorous hierarchy of English industry to a meager wage of eighteen shillings a week. Yet his gaze was not fixed upon the slag heaps or the soot-stained brick cottages of Clayton; rather, it was drawn upward to the zenith, where a celestial phenomenon of unprecedented magnitude was unfolding. For three weeks, astronomers at Greenwich and Paris had tracked the luminous stranger through their equatorial telescopes. It was a comet of parabolic orbit, rushing toward its perihelion with a velocity exceeding forty leagues a second. To the naked eye, it appeared as a blazing emerald star, trailing a luminous tail that spanned thirty degrees of the nocturnal arc. Spectral analysis had revealed the presence of carbon and an unknown, volatile gas within its coma, yet the populace paid scant attention to the warnings of the observatories. Their minds were consumed by a far more terrestrial conflagration. Every telegraph wire across Europe hummed with the feverish dispatches of mobilization. Great Britain and the Continental Powers stood upon the precipice of war, their ironclad fleets steaming into the North Sea, their arsenals working day and night to forge engines of destruction. Amidst this twin shadow of cosmic mystery and human folly, William Leadford nursed a private agony that eclipsed the fate of empires.

The cause of his torment lay folded within his waistcoat pocket: a brief, tear-stained note from Nettie Stuart, the daughter of a humble gardener, whom William had loved with all the fierce, possessive ardor of his impoverished youth. She had fled. Not alone, alas, but in the company of Edward Verrall, the idle, handsome scion of a wealthy colliery proprietor, whose motorcar had so often raised the dust along the Clayton lanes. To William, this betrayal was the ultimate outrage of a cruel social order—a world where gold purchased even the affections of the heart. Driven by a feverish delirium, he sought out a pawnshop in the neighboring town of Swadlincote. There, for the sum of twelve shillings—his final reserve of coin—he purchased a small, six-chambered revolver of Belgian manufacture, together with a box of brass-rimmed cartridges. He examined the rifled barrel and the crisp action of the hammer with a cold, mechanical precision that belied the tempest in his breast. He knew where the fugitives had gone; a careless word dropped by a stableboy had revealed their destination to be Shaphambury, a quiet watering-place upon the southern coast. By ten o'clock that evening, William had secured a third-class ticket upon the southward express. As the locomotive thundered out of the station, its boiler hissing with superheated steam, he sat in the corner of the dimly lit compartment, his fingers gripping the butt of the weapon in his coat, vowing that neither the laws of man nor the vast distances of England would shield Verrall from his vengeance.

Throughout the long nocturnal journey, the railway carriage became a microcosm of the nation's madness. At every junction, newsboys ran along the platforms, hawking special editions whose ink was scarcely dry. The headlines screamed of naval engagements in the North Sea: armored cruisers exchanging broadsides of high-explosive shells, torpedo boats darting through the fog like steel sharks, and great dreadnoughts sinking beneath the freezing waves with all hands aboard. The passengers—merchants, soldiers, and laborers alike—argued fiercely, their faces flushed with patriotic fury and irrational hatred. William spoke to no one. Pressing his forehead against the vibrating glass of the window, he watched the countryside glide past beneath an increasingly unnatural illumination. The comet had grown monstrous. Its nucleus now rivaled the full moon in brilliance, and its vast, gaseous plume stretched across half the heavens, bathing the sleeping fields of Warwickshire and Oxfordshire in a pale, spectral green. The very atmosphere seemed charged with an electric tension; the barometer, as a fellow passenger nervously remarked, had plummeted to an unprecedented low, yet not a breath of wind stirred the leaves of the wayside oaks. By the time the train reached the southern terminus and William transferred to the coastal branch line, the eastern horizon was glowing not with the accustomed rose of dawn, but with a sickly, phosphorescent radiance. It was apparent even to the most unobservant eye that the Earth was rushing directly into the path of that luminous, extraterrestrial vapor, whose precise composition remained a subject of anxious debate among the savants of Europe.

Shaphambury lay nestled beneath chalk cliffs that plunged steeply into the English Channel. When William stepped out upon the gravel platform, the hour was just past midnight, though the darkness was strangely mitigated by the emerald glare overhead. He wasted no time in seeking an inn. Guided by the directions he had extracted from a drowsy porter, he struck out along the cliff road toward a secluded cluster of seaside villas known as the Bungalows. As he walked, a dull, rhythmic concussion vibrated through the soles of his boots and rattled the pebbles along the shore. Out beyond the horizon, invisible in the nocturnal haze, the great fleets were locked in mortal combat; the heavy naval ordnance, firing projectiles weighing half a ton, was tearing the night air apart. Yet William scarcely heeded the distant thunder of war. His entire being was concentrated upon the yellow lamp glowing behind the curtained windows of the third villa. Reaching the low stone wall that bordered the garden, he vaulted over it with silent agility. Through a gap in the heavy draperies, he beheld a scene that sent the blood rushing to his temples: a modestly furnished parlor, a table set with remnants of supper, and there, standing beside the hearth, was Nettie, her dark hair falling over her shoulders, listening with a troubled expression as Edward Verrall spoke to her in low, earnest tones. William drew the revolver from his pocket, cocked the hammer with a sharp metallic click, and stepped toward the open French doors.

"Verrall!" William cried, his voice hoarse and trembling as he burst into the room. Nettie uttered a piercing shriek, recoiling toward the mantelpiece, while Verrall spun around, his handsome face blanching at the sight of the leveled firearm. For three seconds, the tableau remained frozen—a stark monument to human jealousy and class hatred. William's finger tightened upon the trigger. Yet, at that precise instant, the heavens above Shaphambury seemed to ignite. A blinding flash of emerald light engulfed the villa, accompanied by a sharp, hissing sound like the sudden escape of steam from a thousand boilers. The Earth had plunged squarely into the dense, gaseous tail of the comet. Through the open doors poured a thick, luminous green mist, heavy and sweet to the nostrils, carrying an aroma not unlike that of crushed almonds and ozone. William gasped, his lungs involuntarily drawing in the strange, extraterrestrial element. Instantly, a profound lassitude seized his limbs. The revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers, clattering harmlessly upon the floorboards. Across the room, Verrall swayed like a felled sapling and sank gracefully onto the hearthrug, while Nettie collapsed into an armchair, her eyes fluttering shut without a trace of fear. William stumbled backward onto the veranda, his vision swimming in a sea of vivid, soothing green. There was no pain, no suffocation—only an overwhelming, irresistible sensation of release, as if a burning fever were being gently washed from his brain. Looking up one final time at the blazing, emerald sky, William Leadford succumbed to a deep, dreamless slumber.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was high above the Channel, yet the world upon which it shone seemed newly created. The emerald mist had vanished, leaving the atmosphere of an extraordinary, crystalline purity, as though every particle of soot, dust, and miasma had been precipitated by some cosmic alchemy. He sat up upon the wooden veranda, amazed by the clarity of his own thoughts. The fierce, blinding rage that had driven him across England had evaporated like morning dew, replaced by a calm, rational comprehension of his own folly. Rising to his feet, he stepped back into the parlor. Near the hearth, Verrall was stirring, rubbing his eyes with the bewilderment of a man waking from a century of sleep, while Nettie sat upright, gazing at the sunlit sea with an expression of serene wonder. William stooped, picked up the fallen revolver, and looked at it not with passion, but with the mild curiosity of an antiquarian examining a barbarous relic. Placing the weapon carefully upon the table, he approached his former rival and his lost love. There were no reproaches, no cries of alarm; when their eyes met, a profound, unspoken sympathy passed between the three of them, as if the cometary nitrogen had cleansed the very springs of human selfishness. Stepping out onto the cliff path together, they looked toward the horizon. Far out upon the tranquil, azure waters of the Channel, the great ironclad warships lay motionless, their towering funnels devoid of smoke, their terrible guns silent forevermore—silent witnesses to the dawn of a wiser, gentler age for all mankind.
