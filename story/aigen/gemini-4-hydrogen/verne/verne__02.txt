The district of Staffordshire, known not unjustly as the Black Country, presented on that September evening a spectacle of industrial gloom. From the tall chimneys of the ironworks and collieries, dense columns of sulfurous smoke ascended, mingling with the damp vapors of the Midlands. Along a cinder-strewn path skirting the great slag heaps of Burslem, William Leadford walked with a rapid, agitated stride. He was a young man of twenty-two, slight of frame, wearing the threadbare coat of an under-clerk, whose pale countenance bore the unmistakable stamp of both poverty and acute mental distress. In his pocket lay a crumpled sheet of notepaper, the contents of which had shattered his modest existence. Nettie Stuart, the object of his sole and ardent affection, had fled the smoky precincts of her home in the company of Edward Verrall, the wealthy and idle son of a local proprietor. Yet, had Leadford raised his eyes from the blackened earth, he would have beheld a phenomenon far surpassing his private grief. High in the eastern firmament hung the Great Comet, an astronomical visitor whose rapid approach had occupied the observatories of Greenwich and Paris for weeks. Its nucleus burned with a fierce, greenish-white radiance, and its luminous tail stretched across the constellations like a scimitar of emerald fire. The spectroscope had revealed within it an unknown gas, of a nature wholly foreign to terrestrial chemistry, and calculations indicated that within forty-eight hours, the Earth must inevitably pass through its outer envelope.

Leadford, however, cared little for the speculations of astronomers. Consumed by a bitter sense of injustice, he had resolved upon a desperate course. In a narrow shop in Birmingham, he had expended the last of his meager savings upon a six-chambered revolver and a box of cartridges. By midnight, he had boarded the southbound express train, securing a corner in a dimly lit third-class carriage. The powerful locomotive, driven by a high-pressure boiler, hurled itself through the darkness at fifty miles an hour, its iron wheels clattering rhythmically over the steel rails. At every station where the train paused to take on water, the platform was crowded with anxious citizens clutching the latest editions of the evening journals. The telegraph wires were humming with ominous dispatches: diplomatic relations between London and Berlin had been severed; the Channel Fleet had weighed anchor under sealed orders; and unconfirmed rumors spoke of a naval engagement already begun amidst the fogs of the North Sea. Europe, armed to the teeth with Krupp cannon and Whitehead torpedoes, was sliding inexorably into the abyss of a continental war. Inside the carriage, two commercial travelers debated the trajectory of the comet and the price of Admiralty coal, their voices rising in irritable contention. Leadford sat silent in his corner, his hand resting upon the cold metal in his pocket, his thoughts fixed solely upon the coastal town of Shaphambury, whither the fugitives had fled.

Upon the following afternoon, Leadford alighted at the Shaphambury terminus. The seaside resort, ordinarily a tranquil haven of whitewashed villas and manicured esplanades, was gripped by a peculiar paralysis. Many of the wealthier visitors had departed inland, terrified alike by the prospect of coastal bombardment and the portentous aspect of the heavens. As twilight descended, the Great Comet dominated the entire eastern sky, its coma spanning thirty degrees of arc. It bathed the chalk cliffs, the shingled beach, and the restless waters of the English Channel in a ghostly, phosphorescent light. Far out upon the horizon, the long, slate-gray hulls of British ironclads could be dimly discerned, their electric searchlights sweeping the dark expanse of the sea in ceaseless vigilance. Leadford, indifferent to the martial display, pursued his inquiries with methodical determination. At the Grand Marine Hotel, a distracted porter informed him that a Mr. Verrall and a young lady had engaged rooms the previous day, and had recently been seen walking eastward along the cliff path toward the headland. Leadford set out at once. The air had grown remarkably still; the barometers in the harbor master’s office had been falling steadily since noon, yet no wind stirred the surface of the deep. Only the distant, rhythmic throb of a cruiser’s engines broke the oppressive silence of the approaching night. He climbed the steep incline of the downs, his boots slipping occasionally upon the dew-dampened turf, until he saw two silhouettes standing near the edge of the precipice, outlined sharply against the emerald glow of the sea.

"Verrall!" cried Leadford, his voice echoing harshly across the silent headland. The pair turned abruptly. Edward Verrall, clad in a well-tailored suit of coastal tweed, stepped instinctively before Nettie, whose face showed a sudden, pale alarm beneath the eerie green illumination. "Stand aside," Leadford commanded, drawing the revolver from his coat. The weapon gleamed faintly in the celestial light. "I have not come to bandy words with a thief who buys what he cannot earn." Verrall, though unarmed, held his ground with the cool composure of his class, while Nettie uttered a sharp cry of entreaty. Yet before Leadford could steady his trembling arm, a singular atmospheric transformation occurred. A faint, sweetish odor, reminiscent of crushed bitter almonds and fresh ozone, drifted across the cliffs. Looking upward, they perceived that the sky had lost its crystalline clarity; a luminous, verdant mist was descending from the zenith, enveloping the stars, the sea, and the distant warships. The Earth had entered the comet’s tail. Out over the Channel, a sudden flash of orange fire split the green haze, followed by the heavy, reverberating boom of a twelve-inch naval gun. Then, an absolute silence fell. A profound, irresistible drowsiness seized Leadford’s limbs. His eyelids grew heavy; the revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers and fell into the grass. Across the turf, he saw Verrall sink to his knees, and Nettie collapse gently beside him, before darkness claimed his own senses.

How many hours elapsed during that universal swoon, none could precisely determine. When Leadford at length opened his eyes, the sun had already risen above the eastern horizon, flooding the English Channel with a brilliance such as he had never before witnessed. The green vapor had entirely vanished, leaving behind an atmosphere of astonishing purity and transparency. Every blade of grass, every distant sail, stood out with the sharp definition of a finely engraved plate. Leadford sat up, expecting the familiar, gnawing ache of his jealousy and the dull fatigue of his journey. Instead, he experienced a sensation of boundless physical well-being and an extraordinary lucidity of mind. It was as though a dense, suffocating fog had been swept from the chambers of his brain by a bracing sea breeze. Beside him lay the revolver. He picked it up, examined its blued steel barrel and revolving cylinder with detached curiosity, and wondered what madness could have induced him to carry so senseless an instrument of destruction. With a calm, unhurried gesture, he rose and hurled the weapon far over the cliff, watching it vanish into the glittering surf below. Turning, he saw that Verrall and Nettie were also stirring. They sat up upon the turf, looking first at the radiant sky and then at Leadford, their faces entirely devoid of fear, anger, or embarrassment. A quiet, rational serenity seemed to possess them all, stripping away the artificial barriers of pride and resentment that had divided them only a few hours before.

"You are unharmed, William?" Nettie asked, her voice possessing a clear, tranquil cadence that matched the morning air. "Entirely, Nettie," Leadford replied, offering his hand to help both her and Verrall to their feet. "I have been acting like a man in a fever. The fever has broken." Verrall nodded gravely, brushing the damp turf from his sleeve. "We have all been walking in darkness," he observed, gesturing toward the vast expanse of the sea. "Look yonder." Together, the three walked to the edge of the headland and gazed down upon the harbor of Shaphambury and the waters beyond. A miraculous transformation had overtaken the instruments of war. The great gray ironclads lay motionless at anchor, their battle flags lowered, while small white boats plied peacefully between the ships and the shore. Through the clear air, the distant sound of cheering could be heard—not the fierce roar of combat, but the joyful acclamation of men liberated from an ancient nightmare. Along the esplanade below, the townsfolk were emerging from their dwellings, conversing in animated, friendly groups, their petty anxieties and national hatreds dissolved by the chemical alchemy of the comet’s breath. Leadford looked at Nettie and Verrall, feeling no pang of loss, only a vast, quiet wonder at the boundless possibilities of the new day. Side by side, the three began the descent toward the town, ready to take their places in a world reborn.
