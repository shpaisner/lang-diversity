In the blackened heart of Staffordshire, where the kilns of the Potteries belched their perpetual tribute of soot into the overcast firmament, William Leadford walked with a heart as heavy as the iron slag beneath his boots. A clerk of modest means and fierce, solitary pride, he had been robbed of the sole radiance in his narrow existence. Nettie—she whose laughter had once sweetened the sulfurous air of Burslem—had vanished southward with Edward Verrall, the heir to a considerable fortune and a name spoken with deference in the county halls. In Leadford’s coat pocket rested a newly purchased six-chambered revolver, its steel cylinder cold against his trembling fingers. Above the chimneys, however, a far grander drama was unfolding in the depths of space. For weeks, the observatories of Greenwich, Paris, and Pulkovo had tracked an extraordinary celestial body rushing toward the inner solar system. Already, its nucleus shone with a peculiar, livid emerald hue, outstripping the calculations of the most eminent astronomers. Yet the populace paid scant heed to the heavens, for the journals of London and Berlin were filled with graver terrestrial alarms. Across the North Sea, the ironclad squadrons of two mighty empires steamed toward one another, their bunkers gorged with Welsh coal and their magazines brimming with lyddite. Leadford, blind to everything save his own humiliation, folded his newspaper, stepped onto the station platform, and awaited the southbound express that would bear him toward vengeance.

The locomotive thundered across the Midland plain, its pistons driving the great coupled wheels at sixty miles an hour through the gathering dusk. From his third-class compartment, Leadford watched the telegraph wires rise and fall beside the embankment, humming with the frantic dispatches of ministers and admirals. At every junction, the railway sidings were choked with rolling stock painted in the drab olive of the War Office—flatcars bearing field artillery, ammunition caissons, and regiments of infantry bound for the eastern ports. Europe, driven by a century of mutual suspicion, was sliding irresistibly down the inclined plane of catastrophe. Yet as night fell, the passengers in the dimly lit carriage pressed their faces not to the passing troop trains, but to the eastern horizon. There, rising above the dark silhouette of the Cambridgeshire fens, hung the Great Comet. It was no longer a mere telescopic curiosity, nor even a faint smudge upon the ecliptic; it had become a stupendous luminous fan, spanning thirty degrees of the celestial vault. Its head burned with the brilliance of Jupiter, while its vast tail—a luminous plume of rarefied gas—swept across the constellations like the beam of a cosmic lighthouse. Astronomers at the Royal Society had detected anomalous spectral lines within its radiance, indicative of an unknown element akin to nitrogen, yet infinitely more volatile. Leadford, staring into that eerie green luminescence, felt only a cold intensification of his resolve, as though the heavens themselves had lit the stage for his retribution.

Midnight found him upon the desolate shingle of Shaphambury, a quiet seaside hamlet on the Suffolk coast where the North Sea broke in long, phosphorescent rollers against the cliffs. Here, among a cluster of modest timber bungalows, Verrall had sought refuge with his prize. Leadford had traced them with the methodical persistence of a man who had nothing left to lose; a bribed porter in Ipswich and a garrulous cabman had supplied the final coordinates. Now, he crouched behind a wind-bent tamarisk hedge, watching the lamplit windows of the furthest dwelling. Out upon the black expanse of the German Ocean, long white fingers of electric searchlights swept the horizon, probing the mist for the silhouettes of hostile torpedo boats. Twice, the distant horizon pulsed with a brief, silent flash, though whether of lightning or of naval artillery, the wind carried no report to the shore. Overhead, the comet had attained a terrifying majesty. It occupied nearly half the visible sky, bathing the chalk cliffs, the wet pebbles, and the restless sea in a cold, copper-tinted twilight that cast sharp, unnatural shadows across the sand. The air itself seemed curiously still, charged with an oppressive, metallic tension, as if the atmosphere were waiting for some tremendous electrical discharge. Leadford checked the chambers of his revolver by the ghastly celestial glow. Inside the bungalow, a shadow moved across the drawn blind—a slender silhouette he knew too well. He settled down upon the damp shingle to wait for the dawn.

Daybreak arrived not with the rosy warmth of the sun, but with a sickly, emerald suffusion that turned the eastern sky the color of oxidized bronze. Suddenly, the morning silence was shattered by a deep, subterranean tremor that vibrated through the shingle beneath Leadford’s knees. Far out beyond the sandbanks, veiled in sea-fog, the armored battleships had opened fire; the dull, rhythmic booming of twelve-inch naval guns rolled across the water like distant thunder. At that moment, the door of the bungalow opened, and Nettie stepped out onto the veranda, followed closely by Verrall, who carried a pair of field glasses to observe the maritime engagement. Leadford rose from his concealment and strode across the pebbles, his weapon drawn and leveled at Verrall’s chest. Nettie uttered a sharp cry, stepping instinctively between the two men, her eyes wide with recognition and terror. Verrall paled, yet stood his ground, raising a hand in useless protest. Leadford’s finger tightened upon the trigger—when an extraordinary phenomenon arrested his very breath. From the zenith, the vast, luminous tail of the comet seemed to stoop toward the globe. A dense, translucent mist of pale green vapor descended swiftly through the upper atmosphere, swallowing the distant flashes of the warships and muffling the roar of their cannon. An overpowering odor of ozone and sweet, unfamiliar ethers filled the air. Leadford’s limbs grew heavy as lead; his eyelids drooped; the revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers onto the stones, and side by side with his rivals, he sank into a profound, dreamless insensibility.

How many hours elapsed while the Earth drifted through that celestial cloud, no chronometer could immediately record, for all human industry had ceased in the same instant. When William Leadford at length unclosed his eyes, the emerald haze had entirely vanished, leaving behind an atmosphere of crystalline purity such as he had never beheld in his life. The sun stood high in a vault of dazzling, cloudless azure, and a gentle breeze off the sea carried the crisp, invigorating scent of salt and wet kelp. He sat up slowly, expecting the familiar, gnawing ache of wounded pride and murderous fury. Instead, his brain felt astonishingly clear, as if a thick curtain of soot had been scrubbed from the windows of his perception. Beside him upon the shingle lay the revolver, its blued steel glistening in the pure light; he regarded it with the mild bewilderment of a man examining a grotesque toy left behind by a forgotten nightmare. A few paces away, Nettie and Verrall were also stirring from their stupor. Verrall sat up, passing a hand across his forehead, and looked at Leadford without fear or arrogance. Nettie rose to her knees, gazing first at the quiet sea, then at the two men, her face pale yet extraordinarily serene. When Leadford stood up, he felt no impulse to strike, no bitterness in his throat. It was as though the green vapor of the comet, penetrating every lung upon the planet, had catalyzed some subtle chemical transformation within the human blood, purging the petty ferocities of the animal brain.

They stood together by the low stone sea-wall, three ordinary mortals blinking in the radiance of a renovated world. To Leadford’s newly awakened eyes, Verrall was no longer a hated aristocrat who had plundered his happiness, but simply a young man, somewhat bewildered and earnest, who had followed an impulse of youth. Nor did Nettie appear as a faithless betrayer; he saw her clearly now, with all her modest virtues and limitations, and realized with a quiet pang of wonder that love could neither be compelled by anger nor defended by lead. They spoke together in low, unhurried tones, without recrimination or theatricality, marveling at the extraordinary lightness of their spirits. Presently, a coastguard came cycling along the cliff path, his face beaming with an almost childlike astonishment. He called out to them that the telegraph office in the village had resumed its clicking, and that astonishing dispatches were pouring in from every capital of Europe. Out upon the North Sea, the rival fleets had ceased their bombardment; through the coastguard’s telescope, one could discern the gray leviathans resting motionless upon the swell, their signal flags fluttering in peaceful parley, while launches passed amicably between British and German hulls. Leadford bent down, retrieved the revolver from the pebbles, and cast it far out into the incoming tide, watching the white foam close over it forever. Bidding Nettie and Verrall a calm, sincere farewell, he turned his face inland toward the railway station, eager to return to Staffordshire and begin, alongside his fellow men, the rational reconstruction of the Earth.
