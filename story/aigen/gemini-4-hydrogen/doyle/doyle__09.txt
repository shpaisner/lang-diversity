A man who has been bred among the blast-furnaces and pot-banks of Staffordshire learns early to take his knocks without whimpering, yet there is a limit to what flesh and pride can endure. In that grim autumn, the whole of the Five Towns seemed to lie under a pall of soot and despair. I was twenty-two, a clerk in a raw-material office at Clayton, earning a guinea a week and living in a damp terrace house where the plaster peeled from the walls like scabs. Trade was stagnant, strikes were brewing in the collieries, and every evening the London papers brought darker news from the Continent. Germany was massing her cruisers in the North Sea, and our own fleet had vanished into the grey mists off the Dogger Bank. Men talked of war in the public-houses with a fierce, thirsty eagerness, as though bloodshed might somehow cure their poverty.

Yet it was not the Kaiser’s battleships that drew men’s eyes upward when the factory whistles blew at dusk. High in the eastern sky hung the green comet. At first it had been a mere smudge of sickly light beside the Pleiades, visible only to those who knew where to look, but week by week it swelled into a blazing portent. By October its luminous tail swept halfway across the zenith, casting a faint, uncanny emerald sheen upon the slate roofs and the black canal water. The astronomers at Greenwich wrote learnedly of its gaseous composition, though they could not agree upon its course. For my own part, I cared little for their calculations. I had a sharper poison in my blood, and her name was Nettie Stuart.

We had been promised to one another since the previous spring, though her father, a head-gardener at Checkley Hall, thought little enough of a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a cough. Still, Nettie had sworn she loved me, and I had believed her with the blind, desperate faith of a man who possesses nothing else in the world. Then came Edward Verrall. He was the son of the great pottery owner—a sleek, fair-haired young gentleman from Cambridge with a motor-car, a crisp laugh, and pockets lined with sovereigns. He had seen Nettie by the lodge gates, and from that hour my doom was sealed.

I found the truth on a Tuesday evening, scrawled in a hurried, blotted note that Nettie had left with my mother. She was sorry, she wrote; she had gone away with Mr. Verrall to the sea, and I must forget her. Forget her! As I stood in the narrow kitchen under the sputtering gas-jet, a red mist swam before my eyes. All the bitterness of my cramped, hungry life seemed to gather into a single hard knot in my chest. Without a word to my mother, I put on my cap and walked straight down to the High Street, where a Jewish pawnbroker kept a tray of rusty pistols in his window. Five shillings bought me a squat, five-chambered revolver and a handful of cartridges. They were tarnished with verdigris, yet when I spun the cylinder in my palm, the click of the ratchet was as sharp and decisive as a judge’s sentence. I knew where Verrall’s aunt kept a cottage—at Shaphambury, on the Essex coast—and I swore before God that neither of them should live to laugh at me.

The journey south was like a passage through a madhouse. Every railway station was choked with reservists in ill-fitting khaki, their sweethearts weeping on the platforms while drunken crowds cheered them on. At Birmingham, a newsboy thrust a special edition through the carriage window, screaming that shots had been fired in the Heligoland Bight and that war had been declared at noon. My fellow-passengers—two commercial travellers and a red-faced grazier—fell into a furious argument over our naval gunnery, waving their fists and cursing the Germans until the compartment smelled of stale tobacco and sweat. I sat in the corner with my chin sunk into my collar and my right hand wrapped round the butt of the revolver in my ulster pocket. Their war meant nothing to me; I had a private campaign of my own.

I reached Shaphambury after midnight, having walked the last six miles along a flinty coast road from the junction. The little seaside town lay hushed and deserted, its shutters bolted against the night air, though the sky above was brighter than any noon I had ever seen in Staffordshire. The comet had grown monstrous. It filled the heavens with a cold, emerald radiance, so vivid that I could read the lettering on the signposts and see the white foam curling along the shingle half a mile away. A strange stillness hung over the marshes—no bird cried, no dog barked, and even the sea seemed to heave with a heavy, oily sluggishness. I found the cliff-top path and paced it until the eastern horizon began to pale, nursing my hatred like a dying fire against the chill of the dawn.

It was shortly after five o’clock when I saw them. They had come out of a small, whitewashed bungalow tucked under the lee of the cliffs, drawn perhaps by the eerie splendour of the sky. Verrall wore a tweed Norfolk jacket and carried a walking-stick; Nettie was wrapped in a dark cloak, her face pale and pinched in the greenish twilight. She was looking up at the comet with a frightened expression, and Verrall had his arm lightly around her shoulders, speaking to her in that easy, confident murmur that I hated more than his wealth.

I stepped out from behind a clump of gorse twenty paces ahead of them. Nettie gave a sharp little cry and shrank back against the bank, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall stopped dead, his jaw tightening as he recognized me.

"Leadford," he said, and though he tried to keep his voice steady, I saw his knuckles whiten on his stick. "What in the devil’s name are you doing here?"

"I have come to settle an account," I answered, drawing the revolver from my pocket. My voice sounded hoarse and cracked in my own ears. "You have stolen what was mine, Verrall, and you thought a poor clerk from the Potteries would take it lying down."

"Don't be a fool, William!" Nettie sobbed, stepping half before him. "Put that thing away! It was my doing, not his!"

"Stand aside, Nettie," I shouted, cocking the hammer with my thumb. The sharp metallic snap echoed off the chalk face of the cliff. Verrall shoved her behind him and braced his feet, his eyes fixed on the muzzle. Just as I raised my arm to take aim, the whole dome of the sky seemed to split apart. A blinding flash of emerald fire swept from zenith to horizon, so fierce that I staggered back with an involuntary cry, dropping my elbow as the earth beneath my boots gave a dull, sickening tremor.

When I blinked the purple spots from my eyes, the wind had risen from the sea—not the salt gale of an Essex morning, but a warm, heavy vapour that rolled across the shingle like a wall of green smoke. It had a strange, pungent odour, neither foul nor sweet, resembling nothing so much as crushed laurel leaves and ozone. Within thirty seconds it had swallowed the sea, the cliffs, and the bungalow, wrapping the three of us in a luminous, emerald twilight.

I tried to raise the revolver again, cursing through my teeth, for I meant to finish my work before the fog blinded me altogether. Yet a curious numbness was creeping up my forearms. My fingers grew stiff and clumsy, and the iron weapon seemed suddenly as heavy as a lump of pig-lead. Beside me, I heard Verrall cough once—a dry, startled sound—and then sink slowly to his knees upon the turf. Nettie had already slipped down beside him, her cloak spread out like dark wings, her eyes closed as though she had been struck by a sudden, overwhelming weariness.

I fought against it with all the stubbornness of my breed. I staggered forward two paces, trying to remember why I hated this man, trying to fan the sparks of my rage. But the green mist had entered my lungs, and with every breath a cool, delicious languor flooded my veins. My knees buckled beneath me. The revolver slid from my open palm and fell silently into the damp grass. As my cheek touched the cool turf, the last thing I heard was the gentle, rhythmic wash of the tide far below—and then, for the first time in many months, I sank into a deep and dreamless sleep.

I awoke to the sound of a lark singing high overhead. For a long moment I lay quite still, staring up into a sky of the purest, most delicate blue I had ever beheld. The green mist was gone; the comet itself had vanished into the daylight, leaving the morning air as crisp and sweet as spring water.

I sat up, half expecting the familiar ache in my temples and the sour bitterness in my mouth. Instead, my head was wonderfully clear. It was as though a heavy, smoky curtain had been drawn back from my mind, letting in the clean sunlight. Ten paces away, Verrall was stirring on the grass, propping himself upon one elbow and blinking around him in bewilderment. Nettie sat beside him, her knees drawn up, gazing out toward the sparkling expanse of the North Sea with a look of quiet wonder. Between us lay my tarnished revolver, looking small, ugly, and absurdly foolish in the bright morning grass.

I picked it up, broke the cylinder, and let the five brass cartridges fall into my palm before tossing both pistol and bullets far over the edge of the cliff. The faint splash down on the wet shingle made Verrall turn his head. Our eyes met, and for the first time I saw him not as a rival or a master, but simply as a bewildered young man in a crumpled tweed coat.

"Leadford," he said softly, passing a hand across his brow. "I... I had a strange dream. We were all mad, were we not?"

"Yes," I answered, rising to my feet and brushing the dry grass from my trousers. "I think the whole world was mad."

Down in the town, a church bell began to toll—not the frantic alarm of war, but a slow, clear peal that floated up the cliffs through the still air. No guns thundered out at sea; no smoke of burning ships stained the horizon. I looked at Nettie, who was watching me with grave, tear-washed eyes, and felt neither anger nor despair—only a deep, sober pity for all the foolish things we had done in the dark, and a quiet certainty that the long night of our folly was over at last.
