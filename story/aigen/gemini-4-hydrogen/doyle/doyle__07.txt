It was in the grim autumn of that memorable year that the shadow of two great madnesses fell across my life. In our corner of Staffordshire, where the kilns belch their eternal soot against a leaden sky, men spoke of nothing save the approaching war and the strange green star that waxed brighter with every passing night. For my own part, I cared little enough for the Kaiser or the fleet, though the newspapers screamed of mobilization at every street corner. My world was bounded by the narrow, soot-stained walls of Clayton, by my miserable clerk’s stool at the pottery works, and by the bright, restless eyes of Nettie Stuart.

We had been promised to each other since the spring, though God knows what future a man earning eighteen shillings a week could offer a girl of her spirit. She was made for sunlight and music, not for the damp alleys and coughing winters of the Potteries. Yet I loved her with a fierce, hungry devotion that left no room in my breast for reason. When young Verrall began to linger near her father’s gate—Verrall, with his Oxford accent, his gleaming motor-car, and the easy arrogance of a class that had never known a patched coat—a cold poison entered my blood. I warned her against him, my voice shaking with a pride I could neither master nor conceal. She only tossed her dark curls and bade me not to play the tyrant. All that week, the green comet hung low over the chimney-stacks like a baleful lantern, and my heart was as heavy and combustible as the powder in a magazine.

The blow fell on a Tuesday evening, under a sky smeared with the dull crimson of the blast furnaces. Returning from the works with my boots caked in clay, I found a boy waiting by my lodging-house door with a crumpled envelope. I tore it open beneath the flickering gas-lamp, and the few lines written in Nettie’s neat, hurried hand seemed to sear themselves into my brain. She had gone with Verrall. She begged my forgiveness, swore she had tried to be content, and prayed I would forget her.

Forget her! I stood there in the drizzle while the laughter of some drunken colliers echoed down the lane, and I felt the last shred of my gentler nature wither into ash. It was not mere grief that possessed me; it was a black, suffocating humiliation. A gentleman had reached down into my gutter and plucked the one jewel I possessed, as carelessly as he might pluck a wildflower from a hedge. Within the hour, I had drawn my meager savings from the tin box beneath my floorboards and walked into Hanley. In a dim pawnbroker’s shop smelling of damp wool and stale tobacco, I laid down my silver for a small, heavy revolver and a box of cartridges. The broker eyed my pale face and trembling fingers with a shrewd, silent curiosity, yet he asked no questions. That night, the great green star stood directly above the town, casting a sickly, phosphorescent sheen over the wet slate roofs, and I lay awake clutching the cold iron beneath my pillow, seeing only Verrall’s smiling face along the barrel.

By dawn I was on the train for the coast, having learned from a garrulous stable-boy at the Verrall estate that the pair had driven south toward Shaphambury. The railway journey was like a passage through a madhouse. At every junction, the platforms were choked with reservists in ill-fitting khaki, shouting hoarsely as they leaned from carriage windows to kiss weeping women. Newsboys darted through the steam, waving broadsheets that shrieked of ultimatum and naval blockade in the North Sea. The whole of Europe was rushing headlong toward the precipice, yet as I sat wedged in the corner of a third-class compartment, my bruised spirit felt a savage kinship with the gathering storm. Let the empires burn, I thought, so long as I had my reckoning first.

A red-faced commercial traveler opposite me tapped his newspaper with a stubby finger, declaring that the German fleet would be at the bottom of the Channel before Sunday. Beside him, an old clergyman stared upward through the smudged glass at the eastern sky, where the comet was now visible even in the pale glare of noon—a ghostly, emerald smudge against the blue. Neither man spared a glance for the shabby, hollow-eyed youth in the corner, whose right hand never left his coat pocket. By the time the train wheezed into Shaphambury, twilight was falling, and the sea-breeze struck my soot-stained face with a sharp, unfamiliar sting. Above the darkening waters, the comet blazed with a terrible, unearthly splendor, turning the white crests of the waves to liquid jade.

Shaphambury was a quiet place of red-brick villas and chalk cliffs, far removed from the clatter of the Potteries, yet even here the fever of the hour had penetrated. As I walked along the esplanade, I saw knots of anxious townsfolk gathered beneath the lampposts, staring out to sea where the grey hulls of two cruisers slid silently past the headland. I had no money for an inn, nor any thought of sleep. Like a hound upon a fresh scent, I patrolled the high cliff path above the shingle, my eyes scanning every lighted window and every shadowed bench.

It was past ten o’clock when I saw them. They had come up from the small, secluded hotel below the downs, and were strolling slowly toward the point where the chalk drops sheer to the sea. Even in that strange, greenish twilight, I knew them instantly. Verrall wore a light tweed coat, his head inclined with that easy, protective grace which had tortured my imagination for months. Nettie walked beside him, her hand resting lightly upon his arm, a pale shawl wrapped about her shoulders. When she laughed—a clear, careless sound that floated back to me on the salt wind—a red mist swam before my eyes. I slipped behind a clump of gorse, drawing the revolver from my pocket and thumbing back the hammer with a sharp, metallic click. My heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird, yet my hand was steady as stone. Let him have his wealth and his fine name; tonight, at least, we would stand as equals before the dark.

I stepped out upon the turf path not ten paces ahead of them, blocking their way against the luminous void of the sky. Nettie stopped dead with a sharp intake of breath, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall, to his credit, did not flinch. He thrust her gently behind his shoulder and took a half-step forward, his keen eyes dropping at once to the glint of the barrel in my fist.

"Stand back, Leadford," he said, his voice remarkably steady, though I could see the pulse beating in his temple. "Put that thing away before you do a mischief you cannot mend."

"You have mended nothing, Verrall," I cried, my voice cracking with the bitterness of a hundred sleepless nights. "You have stolen all I had, and tossed it aside like a rich man’s toy!"

"Will, don’t!" Nettie sobbed, peering past his arm, her face ghastly in the emerald glare. "It was my choice! I could not bear it there—I was stifling!"

I raised the weapon, sighting squarely at the center of Verrall’s chest. Far out upon the black horizon, a dull, distant thunder rolled across the water—the first guns of the war, perhaps, or the breaking of a storm. My finger tightened upon the trigger. Then, before I could draw it home, a singular hush fell over the cliffs. The wind died in an instant. Sweeping down from the heavens came a vast, rolling tide of pale green vapor, luminous and strangely sweet, carrying a scent like crushed herbs after rain. My lungs drank it in; a sudden, delicious languor flooded my limbs, my arm dropped leadenly to my side, and I sank gently into the turf as the world dissolved into silence.

When I opened my eyes, the madness was gone. It was not merely that I had slept; it was as though a foul, stifling fog had been blown clean out of the chambers of my brain, leaving every thought as crisp and clear as the morning air. The sun was rising over a sea of molten gold, and the green mist had vanished, leaving behind a sky of astonishing, cloudless purity. Beside my hand, half-buried in the dewy grass, lay the cheap revolver. I looked at it with a quiet, detached wonder, marveling that a creature so small and brutish as the man I had been yesterday could ever have walked beneath so noble a heaven.

A few paces away, Verrall was sitting up upon the turf, rubbing his forehead with a bewildered expression, while Nettie stirred beside him, blinking in the bright light. When Verrall’s eyes met mine, there was neither fear nor anger in them—only a grave, questioning humanity. I rose slowly to my feet, leaving the pistol in the grass, and walked toward them. Nettie looked up at me, and for the first time I saw her not as a prize to be guarded or a possession to be claimed, but simply as a fellow soul, frail and hopeful, seeking her own light.

"Are you hurt, Will?" she asked softly, and her voice held no terror.

"No," I answered, and I felt a strange, gentle peace settle over my heart. "No, Nettie. All is well."

Down in the town below, no bugles blew, and out upon the shining sea the grey warships had turned quietly toward the shore. A new day had begun, and for the first time in my life, I was not afraid to meet it.
