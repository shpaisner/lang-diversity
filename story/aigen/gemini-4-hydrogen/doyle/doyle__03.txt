It was upon a Tuesday evening in late August that the blow fell, leaving me as dark and bitter as the Staffordshire slag-heaps among which I had been bred. I had returned from the pottery works with the grey clay still dried upon my knuckles, only to find my mother weeping beside the kitchen grate and a single crumpled note upon the oilcloth table. Nettie was gone. She had not merely left her home; she had fled with young Edward Verrall, the son of the very man whose mines and kilns held our valley in thrall. That a woman I had loved since childhood should cast herself into the arms of a gilded idler struck me like a physical wound. I stood by the small window, staring out over the huddled slate roofs and belching chimneys of Clayton. High above the smoke-pall hung the Great Comet, which for weeks had been the talk of every taproom and chapel. It was no longer a mere smudge of light, but a vast, menacing spear of sickly emerald, casting a pallor over the brickfields. Europe, the papers cried, was sliding into the abyss of war, yet what were the quarrels of emperors to me? My own small world lay in ashes. With a cold, deliberate steadiness that surprised even myself, I unlocked the wooden chest beneath my bed and drew forth the old five-chambered revolver I had bought from a pawnshop in Hanley. I slipped it into my coat pocket. Before the week was out, I swore, Edward Verrall would answer to me.

Three days later, I found myself wedged into the corner of a third-class railway carriage bound for the east coast, my fingers never straying far from the heavy iron in my pocket. Through a network of gossip and a bribed porter at Crewe, I had traced the fugitives to Shaphambury, a quiet watering-place where the Verralls kept a summer villa. The whole country seemed to have caught the fever of my own mind. At every station, platforms were choked with red-faced men in khaki, while newsboys ran alongside the windows shrieking of German cruisers in the North Sea and ultimatums expiring at midnight. My fellow passengers spoke in hushed, anxious voices, their faces illuminated by the strange, greenish twilight that now lingered long after sunset. The comet had grown monstrous, its luminous tail sweeping half across the heavens like a drawn scimitar. Yet I sat silent, nursing my solitary grievance. To my darkened spirit, the impending ruin of nations seemed only a fitting backdrop for the tragedy I meant to enact. A man who has been robbed of his pride and his hope cares little for the fate of empires. When at last the train hissed to a halt beside the sea, the salt air felt sharp and clean after the coal-smoke of the Midlands, though it did nothing to cool the fire in my blood. I stepped out onto the platform, a grim, shabby figure amidst the holidaymakers, and turned my face toward the cliffs.

It is a singular thing how easily a man may hunt his fellow creatures when his heart is set upon vengeance. I possessed neither wealth nor cunning, yet within two hours of my arrival in Shaphambury, I had picked up their trail as surely as a bloodhound. A cabman at the station remembered a handsome young gentleman in a grey tweed suit accompanied by a dark-haired girl in a blue travelling cloak; a maidservant at the Crown Inn confirmed that they had taken rooms at a secluded boarding-house overlooking the links. By nine o'clock, I was crouched behind a clump of wind-bent tamarisk bushes upon the cliff path, watching the lighted French windows of their sitting-room. The night was unnaturally warm, heavy with an oppressive stillness that made the breathing laboured. Even the sea below seemed sluggish, rolling in long, oily swells that caught the ghastly green reflection of the comet overhead. Presently, the curtains parted, and they stepped out onto the gravel terrace. Nettie was laughing—a light, careless sound that drove the iron deeper into my soul. Verrall stood beside her, tall, effortless, and assured, his arm resting lightly about her waist. I drew the revolver from my pocket and thumbed back the hammer. The sharp metallic click sounded loud in the silence. My hand was steady, my mind a narrow, murderous tunnel. I waited only for them to walk out along the lonely path toward the headland, where no one might interpose between us.

They came down the narrow chalk path some twenty minutes later, strolling arm in arm toward the edge of the bluff. Above them, the sky was an appalling spectacle; the great star now filled a quarter of the firmament, its emerald glare so fierce that I could read the dial of my silver watch as plainly as at noon. Far out upon the black horizon, a sudden series of orange flashes flickered, followed seconds later by a dull, rolling boom that shook the turf beneath my boots. War had broken out upon the waters. Verrall halted, staring out to sea, and Nettie pressed closer to his side. It was then that I stepped from the shadow of the gorse, blocking their return. Nettie gave a sharp cry, her hand flying to her throat, while Verrall instinctively thrust her behind him, facing me with the cool, arrogant poise of his class. "Leadford!" he exclaimed, his voice tight with astonishment. "What madness brings you here?" I did not answer him with words, for my throat was parched with hatred; instead, I raised the revolver until the muzzle pointed squarely at his breast. Nettie pleaded with me, calling my name in tones of terror and pity, yet her voice seemed to reach me from a great distance, as though through thick glass. I felt the rough steel of the trigger against my forefinger. One slight pressure, and the long, bitter account between rich and poor, between the robbed and the robber, would be settled forever.

My finger tightened upon the trigger—and then I paused, for a strange thing had begun to happen in the heavens. The dazzling green core of the comet seemed to shudder, and from its trailing plumes a vast, luminous mist detached itself, sweeping downward toward the earth with inconceivable swiftness. Within seconds, the distant flashes of the naval guns vanished behind a glowing curtain of emerald fog. A curious, sweetish odour, like bruised thyme and fresh ozone, drifted across the cliff-top, wrapping us in its cool embrace. Verrall coughed sharply, staggering back a pace, while Nettie sank to her knees upon the chalk path. As for myself, I tried to steady my aim, determined that not even the end of the world should cheat me of my revenge. Yet a profound languor was stealing through my limbs, heavier than any weariness I had ever known. The pistol felt as ponderous as a bar of lead. My anger, which had burned so fiercely for days, seemed to dissolve like smoke in a clear wind, leaving behind a vast, unutterable quiet. The green vapour thickened until I could no longer see Verrall's pale face or Nettie's outstretched hands. My knees buckled beneath me. As I sank into the soft turf, the revolver slipped from my nerveless fingers, and a deep, dreamless sleep closed over my eyes, extinguishing the last spark of my earthly malice as gently as the tide covers the sand.

When I opened my eyes, the world had been washed clean. A crisp, golden morning sunlight flooded the cliffside, and the air I drew into my lungs tasted sweeter and purer than any I had ever breathed. Above me stretched a sky of stainless blue, from which the sinister comet had utterly vanished. I sat up, bewildered, and stared at my own hands; for the first time in my life, I saw them not as the scarred tools of a labourer, but simply as the wondrous instruments of a living man. Beside me lay the revolver, its dark metal gleaming in the dew, and I kicked it aside with a shudder of genuine horror. A few paces off, Edward Verrall was rising slowly from the grass, his face pale and full of a quiet wonder, while Nettie sat near him, her dark eyes wide as she looked out toward the sea. Far out upon the shining water lay three great grey warships, motionless and silent, no smoke rising from their funnels, no thunder from their guns. Whatever had passed over us in the night had passed over them as well. Verrall turned his eyes upon me, and there was neither fear nor arrogance in his gaze—only a deep, brotherly recognition. I walked slowly toward them, feeling no shame and no resentment, my heart lightened of a burden I had carried since birth. When Nettie reached out her hand, I took it gently, knowing that the old, dark world of hatred had perished in the green mist, and that we stood together at the dawn of a gentler age.
