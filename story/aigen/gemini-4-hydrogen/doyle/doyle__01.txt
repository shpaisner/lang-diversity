A man who has known nothing but the grime of the Potteries learns to measure his life in shillings and soot. In those stifling August days, when the kilns of Burslem belched their eternal black smoke into a brazen sky, I had but one star to steer by, and her name was Nettie Stuart. Yet on the Tuesday evening, returning from my cramped desk at the clay-works with ink-stained cuffs and an empty stomach, I found her father's cottage silent and her little note pinned to the dresser. She had gone. Worse than that—she had fled south with Edward Verrall, the idle, smooth-tongued son of our local magistrate, a man who had never soiled his hands with honest toil in his life. As I stood in the narrow lane, crushing the paper in my fist, the whole world seemed to be sliding toward the abyss. Down at the corner, a ragged newsboy was screaming the latest extras—how the German fleet had slipped its moorings, how ultimata were flying between the Chancelleries of Europe, and how the Reserves were called to the colors. Men gathered under the gas-lamps with flushed, angry faces, shouting of war. Above them all, clearing the jagged silhouette of the factory chimneys, hung the comet. It was no longer a faint speck for the astronomers; it was a great, pulsing globe of emerald fire, casting a sickly, unnatural pallor over the upturned faces in the street. I looked at that green portent, and my heart hardened into flint. Let Europe burn, I thought; I had my own score to settle.

The next morning found me in a pawnshop off the Market Square, where I laid my silver watch—my father's only legacy—upon the greasy counter. With the sovereigns I received, I walked three doors down to a gunsmith's window and purchased a short-barreled revolver and a box of cartridges. There was a cold, methodical clarity in my mind, the calm of a man who has severed every tie with his past. By noon, I was wedged in the corner of a third-class carriage, speeding southward toward the Sussex coast, where a chance word from Nettie's cousin had told me Verrall's family owned a seaside bungalow. All along the line, England was in a fever. At Crewe, our train was shunted aside to let a long troop-special thunder past, its windows crammed with lads in khaki cheering and waving their caps. At every station, crowds sang patriotic airs, while newspapers fluttered from hand to hand with black headlines proclaiming that shots had already been fired in the North Sea. Nobody heeded me, a pale, threadbare clerk clutching a cheap carpetbag. I sat with my hand in my coat pocket, my fingers curled around the cold butt of the pistol. When night fell, the carriage lamps were dim, yet we needed no light; through the dusty glass, the comet blazed with an ever-increasing splendor. Its trailing tail now spanned half the heavens, bathing the flying hedgerows and quiet English pastures in an unearthly, luminous green.

I reached Shaphambury at dusk, stepping from the station into an air that smelled sharply of salt and crushed sea-lavender. What a contrast it was to the stifling, sulphurous breath of Staffordshire! Here were white chalk cliffs, rolling downs, and, clustered along the edge of the shingle, a handful of red-roofed bungalows overlooking the Channel. I found the Verralls' dwelling easily enough—a neat wooden structure set apart from the rest, with French windows opening onto a patch of coarse sea-grass. Concealing myself behind a clump of gorse upon the rise, I watched and waited. I had not been there twenty minutes before they emerged. Verrall came first, handsome and assured in his white flannels, carrying a wicker basket; Nettie followed, wearing the simple blue cotton dress I knew so well, her dark hair blown back by the evening breeze. She was laughing—a light, careless sound that cut me like a whip. To see her thus, beside the man whose wealth had bought what my honest devotion could not win, set every drop of blood in my veins aflame. Far out across the darkling waters, a dull, rhythmic booming echoed against the cliffs—the heavy guns of the Channel Fleet engaging an unseen enemy in the mist. The great war had begun in earnest, yet down there upon the sands, those two walked as though the world were their private garden. Overhead, the green comet swelled until its light rivaled the full moon, turning the breaking surf to liquid jade.

I waited until they had wandered down to the water's edge, well away from the lights of the little village. Then I rose from the gorse and descended the crumbling cliff path, my boots crunching loudly upon the loose shingle. At the sound of my approach, they turned. In the weird emerald twilight, I saw Nettie's hand fly to her throat as she recognized my face. Verrall, to his credit, showed no cowardice; he stepped instantly between us, squaring his shoulders.

"Leadford!" he exclaimed, his voice sharp with annoyance. "What madness brings you here?"

I did not answer him at once, for my eyes were fixed upon Nettie, standing pale and trembling behind his shoulder. All the pent-up misery of my narrow life—the long hours at the desk, the mockery of my poverty, the bitter ache of her betrayal—surged to my lips in a torrent of fury. I drew the revolver from my pocket. Nettie gave a stifled cry, and Verrall braced himself as though to spring, though he saw the muzzle was steady.

"Stand aside, Verrall," I said, and my voice sounded strange and hollow in my own ears. "You have everything the world can give—lands, leisure, and fine clothes. You could not leave me the one poor thing that was mine."

Verrall's jaw tightened. "She came of her own free will, man," he retorted. "Put that thing down before you do murder."

My thumb drew back the hammer with a crisp, metallic click that seemed to echo louder than the distant cannonade.

Even as I leveled the barrel at his breast, a singular change came over the night. The salt breeze died away in an instant, leaving a breathless, expectant hush. Looking past Verrall's shoulder, I saw that the green comet no longer hung distant in the firmament; its vast, luminous vapors seemed to be rushing down upon the earth like a descending curtain of emerald mist. A faint, sweet scent filled the air—pungent and cool, like crushed mint and summer rain after a long drought. My finger tightened upon the trigger. A sharp report cracked across the beach, and a puff of acrid smoke drifted upward, yet my hand had wavered at the critical second, and the bullet buried itself harmlessly in the wet sand beside Verrall's foot. He did not rush me. Indeed, he stood as though turned to stone, his eyes wide and fixed upon the sky. A sudden, irresistible languor stole through my limbs. The fierce anger that had sustained me for three days melted away like frost before a warm wind, leaving only an overwhelming desire for rest. Across the Channel, the thunder of the naval guns sputtered and ceased. The gulls that had been wheeling above the cliffs dropped silently into the grass. I felt the revolver slip from my numbed fingers and strike the pebbles. My knees buckled beneath me, and as I sank down onto the cool shingle, I saw Nettie and Verrall sinking gently beside me, all three of us claimed by the same deep, merciful slumber.

When I opened my eyes, the green glare was gone, and a dawn of extraordinary purity was breaking over the sea. Never had I beheld such a sky—a clear, washing blue, utterly free of haze, as though the very atmosphere had been cleansed by fire and water. I sat up slowly, expecting the dull headache of exhaustion, yet my mind was wonderfully crisp and alert. Beside me on the stones lay the revolver, its blued steel already dulled by the salt dew. I looked at it with a curious detachment, unable to comprehend the dark, feverish phantom who had carried it across England. A few paces away, Nettie and Verrall were stirring. Nettie sat up first, brushing the sand from her blue skirt, and when her gaze met mine, there was no terror in her eyes—only a quiet, wondering sorrow. Verrall rose to his knees, looking out across the glassy Channel where two great warships lay silent and motionless upon the horizon, their battle-flags hanging limp in the morning calm.

"The madness has passed," Verrall murmured, speaking not to me, nor to Nettie, but to the wide, peaceful sea.

I rose to my feet and walked over to them, leaving the pistol behind me in the shingle. I felt no hatred for him now, nor any wounded pride; I saw him merely as a young man caught, like myself, in the clumsy machinery of a vanished world. Together, without a word of reproach, we turned our faces toward the white cliffs, ready to greet the strange new day that awaited mankind.
