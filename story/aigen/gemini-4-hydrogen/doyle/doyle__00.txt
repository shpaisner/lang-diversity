I remember that night in Staffordshire as a man remembers the delirium of a fever. The sky above the Five Towns was ever a bruised and smoky canopy, lit from beneath by the red mouths of the blast furnaces, yet on that evening a stranger light contended with the glare of the kilns. High above the chimney-stacks hung the Great Comet, no longer a faint smear of silver for astronomers to dispute over, but a blazing emerald portent that swelled with every passing hour.

I stood in my narrow lodging in Clayton, my hand resting upon the cheap Belgian revolver I had purchased that afternoon from a pawnbroker in Burslem. My heart was a knot of black venom. Nettie—my Nettie, whose laughter had been the one clean thing in a world of coal-dust and drudgery—was gone. The note she had left upon her mother's dresser was brief and cruel in its simplicity. She had fled southward with young Edward Verrall, the idle, handsome son of the squire at Checkshill, whose pockets were as deep as my own were empty.

A man of my station is taught to swallow many insults, to touch his cap to his betters and accept his crust with gratitude. Yet there is a limit to what flesh and blood will endure. As I slipped the cold iron into my coat pocket and stepped out into the damp, soot-laden street, I swore that neither Verrall's wealth nor the ominous green star in the heavens should stay my hand.

The night express southward was packed with anxious men, their faces pale and drawn beneath the flickering oil-lamps of the third-class carriage. I sat wedged in the corner, my hat pulled low over my brow, my fingers never leaving the butt of the pistol in my pocket. Opposite me sat a stout commercial traveller and a young artillery corporal, both leaning eagerly over the evening edition of a London paper.

"They say the ultimatums expire at midnight," muttered the traveller, tapping the broadsheet with a trembling forefinger. "The Channel Fleet has already slipped its cables at Portsmouth, and the Germans have massed three corps along the frontier."

"Let 'em come," growled the corporal, though there was more bravado than cheer in his voice. "We've waited long enough to settle accounts."

Their talk of ironclads, torpedo-boats, and mobilisation washed over me like the clatter of the wheels upon the rails. It seemed only natural that Europe should be rushing toward the abyss of war; the whole world was choked with the same blind, murderous fury that burned within my own breast. Now and again, through the grimy window-pane, I caught a glimpse of the comet trailing its luminous green plume across the flying hedgerows, as though some vast celestial torch had been flung down to set the nations ablaze. I closed my eyes, nursing my hatred through the long hours of the journey, and saw only Verrall's mocking smile and Nettie's averted face.

I reached the little watering-place of Shaphambury just as the late afternoon sun was sinking into a leaden sea. How strange and clean the salt wind tasted after the sulphur of the Potteries! Yet I had no eye for the white chalk cliffs or the neat, red-roofed villas perched above the shingle. My mind was set upon a single purpose, and I walked the promenade with the dogged, silent persistence of a hound upon a fresh scent.

It was not difficult to trace them. A gentleman of Verrall's stamp, with a pretty, frightened girl upon his arm, does not pass unnoticed in a quiet seaside town. A porter at the station remembered a young couple hiring a fly for the Grand Hotel on the eastern cliff, and thither I bent my steps as the twilight deepened.

I did not enter the hotel, for my shabby coat and grim, unshaven face would have invited instant expulsion. Instead, I waited in the shadow of the tamarisk bushes beside the carriage drive. Nor did I wait in vain. Within half an hour, the glass doors swung open, and they emerged together, strolling slowly toward the lonely path that winds along the summit of the downs. Nettie wore a light summer cloak, and I saw her glance nervously over her shoulder, as though some instinct warned her of my nearness. Verrall laughed softly and drew her arm closer through his own. At that sight, a red mist swam before my eyes, and I crept out from my hiding-place to follow them into the gathering dusk.

They paused in a grassy hollow a hundred yards from the cliff's edge, where the sea murmured far below against the stones. Above us, the sky had darkened to a deep indigo, save where the comet blazed with an unearthly, emerald splendour that cast long, ghostly shadows across the turf.

"Verrall!" I called, stepping over the brow of the hollow.

Nettie gave a sharp cry and shrank back against him. Verrall spun round, his hand flying to his cane, though his face went white as chalk when he saw what I held.

"Leadford?" he stammered, recovering a measure of his aristocratic coolness. "What madness brings you here? Put that thing away, man, before you do yourself an injury."

"It is not myself I mean to injure," I said, my voice hoarse and trembling. "You have robbed me of the only thing I ever valued, Verrall. Did you think I would stay behind in the smoke and let you buy her like a trinket?"

"William, please!" Nettie sobbed, stretching out her hands. "It wasn't his fault—I chose to come!"

I raised the revolver, sighting squarely at his breast. My finger tightened upon the trigger. Yet even as I braced myself for the kick of the weapon, a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind filled the heavens. From the far horizon out to sea came the dull, heavy boom of distant guns—the first salvos of a warring world—followed instantly by a blinding flash of green fire that swallowed the stars. A strange, sweet-smelling vapour, cool and pungent as crushed mint, swept over the downs. My knees buckled beneath me; the pistol slipped from my numb fingers, and I sank into the grass as a profound, dreamless darkness closed over us all.

When I opened my eyes, the sun was risen, and a lark was singing high in a sky of cloudless blue. For several moments I lay still upon the turf, marvelling at the extraordinary lightness of my own spirit. It was as though a heavy, soot-stained curtain had been torn away from my brain, leaving every thought as crisp and clear as mountain water. The black rage that had driven me across England, the petty jealousies, the bitter pride—all had vanished like the foul dreams of a sick man who wakes at last to health.

I sat up and looked around me. A few paces away, my cheap Belgian revolver lay glinting in the dew. I picked it up, stared at its ugly, brutal barrel with a shudder of genuine disgust, and, rising to my feet, flung it far out over the edge of the cliff into the sea.

As I turned back, Verrall and Nettie were stirring upon the grass. Verrall pushed himself up on one elbow, rubbing his eyes with the bewildered air of a sleeper waking in a strange room. Nettie sat beside him, her hair damp with dew, gazing at me not with terror, but with a quiet, wondering recognition.

"Leadford," Verrall said softly, his voice stripped of all its former arrogance. "What has happened to us? That green light—that mist—"

"I do not know," I answered, and there was no anger left in my tone, only a deep and solemn peace. "But I think the madness has left us, Verrall. I am sorry for what I meant to do."

Nettie rose slowly and came toward me, extending her hand—not as a fugitive begging mercy, nor as a lover returning, but simply as one human soul greeting another in the dawn.

Together, the three of us walked down the winding path toward Shaphambury, speaking little, yet bound by a strange, quiet fellowship that needed no explanation. As we entered the town, it became clear that whatever had befallen us upon the downs had touched the entire world. The streets were filled with townsfolk who had come out of their houses in the early morning, standing in little knots upon the pavements, speaking in low, earnest voices. There was no shouting, no panic, no clamour for blood.

Outside the post office, a small crowd had gathered around the telegraph board, where the morning's dispatches were pinned. I stepped forward and read them over the shoulders of a fisherman and a retired colonel. The news was scarcely believable, yet in the clear light of that morning, it seemed the most natural thing on earth. The great fleets in the Channel had ceased firing; the opposing admirals had signalled a truce at dawn; from Berlin and Paris and London came the same astonishing word—that ministers and generals alike had woken from their stupor, horrified by the senseless slaughter they had nearly unleashed.

We walked on to the sea wall and stood looking out across the sparkling water. Far out upon the horizon, the grey silhouettes of the ironclads lay motionless upon the calm sea, their guns silent beneath the rising sun.

"What will you do now, William?" Nettie asked gently, her eyes resting upon the distant ships.

"I shall go back to Staffordshire," I said, breathing deeply of the clean salt air. "There is work to be done there, and a better life to be built—for all of us."

She smiled, and Verrall laid a quiet hand upon my shoulder. Side by side, we watched the last faint wisps of the green haze melt into the golden morning, knowing that the old, dark world was gone forever.
