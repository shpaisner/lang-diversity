I first saw the green portent above the chimney-stacks of Clayton on a Tuesday evening in May, when the air was thick with the sulphur of the kilns and my own heart was blacker still. A man who earns eighteen shillings a week as a clerk in a pottery-works has little leisure for astronomy, yet even the blindest among us could not ignore that singular visitor. It hung low in the northeast, a pale, emerald-tinted smear against the soot-stained heavens, growing nightly in brilliance while the world beneath it seemed to sink deeper into madness.

My mother sat by our narrow hearth, her knotted fingers endlessly mending a frayed collar, while I spread the evening paper across the oilcloth table. Every column screamed of mobilization. The German fleet had steamed out of Kiel; our own squadrons were massing in the Channel; and the streets of Hanley were choked with shouting youths waving penny Union Jacks. Yet my own small, bitter war consumed me more than the folly of empires. Nettie had not met me by the canal lock on Sunday, nor on Monday. A sickening suspicion had taken root in my breast, watered by a chance word from the baker’s boy, who had seen a great yellow motor-car waiting by the rectory lane.

When I stepped out into the yard to draw a breath of cooler air, the comet seemed to stare down at me like a cold, unblinking eye. Its light threw a faint, unnatural sheen upon the wet slates of the row-houses, and I swore aloud—a foolish, desperate oath—that if she had betrayed me for that idle young gentleman, neither the laws of England nor the fires of heaven should stand between us.

The truth struck me down the following noon, sharp as a knife-thrust. I found a note slipped beneath our door, written in Nettie’s neat, hurried hand, telling me she had gone away with Edward Verrall—that she was sorry, and that I must forget her. Forget her! As if a man could tear out his own lungs and walk away whistling. Verrall, whose father owned half the pits in the valley, had swept her off in his roaring petrol-carriage, leaving me to my ledgers and my squalor.

A cold, terrible composure settled upon me. I did not weep, nor did I return to the counting-house. Instead, I drew my meagre savings from the tin box behind the loose brick in the pantry—four pounds and seven shillings—and walked down into Burslem. The streets were feverish with war-rumours; men stood in knots outside the public-houses, their faces flushed with cheap ale and cheaper patriotism, cheering as a detachment of territorials marched past to the station.

I pushed through them like a ghost until I reached a dingy pawnbroker’s shop in a side-alley. In the window, amid tarnished watches and brass candlesticks, lay an old five-chambered revolver. The pawnbroker, a bleary-eyed fellow with a cough, scarcely glanced at my shabby coat as I laid down thirty shillings for the weapon and a handful of cartridges. My fingers closed over the cold iron butt with a grim satisfaction. I knew where Verrall’s people kept their summer bungalow—at Shaphambury, on the Essex coast. By nightfall I was seated in a third-class railway carriage, the heavy pistol resting in my pocket, rattling southward through a darkened and frantic land.

That journey was a phantasmagoria of smoke, shouting crowds, and lurid green light. At every junction we were delayed to let troop-trains thunder past, packed with lads in khaki who leaned from the windows to sing music-hall ditties, little knowing they were singing their way into the slaughterhouse. Twice I heard the distant, muffled boom of coastal guns practicing in the dark, and the passengers in my compartment—a nervous commercial traveller and two weeping women—spoke in hushed voices of invasion and mines in the North Sea.

I spoke to no one. I sat with my chin sunk into my collar, staring out at the flying hedgerows. The comet had swollen until its luminous tail spanned a third of the sky, bathing the sleeping countryside in a ghostly, phosphorescent twilight. Even the telegraph wires beside the track gleamed like threads of pale emerald.

When I alighted at Shaphambury shortly after midnight, the little seaside town was in a state of frantic confusion. A crowd had gathered outside the post-office, where a clerk was pinning up a fresh telegram announcing the sinking of a cruiser. Nobody spared a glance for a dusty, hollow-eyed clerk in a frayed overcoat. I turned away from the gas-lit promenade and struck out along the sandy lane that led toward the cliffs. The salt wind blew fresh in my face, carrying the rhythmic roar of the breakers, yet my brain burned like a furnace. Ahead of me, set back among stunted pines above the shingle, stood a timbered bungalow with a broad veranda. A warm yellow light shone through the drawn blinds, and parked in the gravel drive was a great yellow motor-car.

I crept through the tamarisk hedge like a poacher, my boots sinking silently into the loose sand. Every nerve in my body was drawn tight as a fiddle-string. Through a gap in the curtains I could see the interior of the sitting-room: a chafing-dish upon a snowy tablecloth, two glasses of wine, and Nettie’s slender figure seated in a wicker chair. She was laughing at something Verrall had said, her dark curls catching the lamplight. At that sight, a red mist swam before my eyes, and I drew the revolver from my pocket, thumbing back the hammer with a sharp, metallic click.

Before I could step onto the veranda, the French windows swung open. Verrall stepped out into the garden, a cigar glowing between his fingers, his evening shirt gleaming white against the darkness. Nettie followed a pace behind him, a silk shawl draped over her bare shoulders.

"Look at it, Ned," she murmured, gazing upward. "It is positively frightening."

The comet now filled half the heavens with a blinding, emerald-green radiance, so bright that I could clearly see the startled expression on Verrall’s handsome face as I stepped out from the shadow of the pines.

"Stand where you are, Verrall!" I cried, my voice cracking in the still air.

Nettie gave a sharp scream and clutched at his arm. Verrall stiffened, dropping his cigar onto the gravel, and stared at the gleaming barrel levelled at his chest.

"Leadford?" he stammered, his aristocratic composure deserting him. "Good God, man, put that thing down! Are you mad?"

"Mad enough to see justice done," I hissed, advancing a pace across the turf. My finger tightened upon the trigger.

At that very instant, the world seemed to hold its breath. A strange, rushing sound, like the sighing of a vast forest before a gale, swept down from the upper air. The green glare of the comet suddenly intensified until the sandhills, the pines, and the terrified faces before me were drenched in an unearthly, verdigris light. Then came a soft, muffled detonation—not a clap of thunder, but a deep, resonant boom that seemed to shake the very marrow in our bones.

A curious, sweetish odour filled my nostrils, sharp as ammonia yet strangely soothing, like crushed mint and ozone after a summer storm. I tried to steady my aim, to press the trigger and end my wretchedness along with Verrall’s, but a sudden, overwhelming languor seized my limbs. My arm grew heavy as lead; the revolver dipped toward the grass.

I saw Verrall stagger backward, his hands flying to his throat, while Nettie sank gently to her knees upon the gravel, her eyes closing as if in peaceful exhaustion. Beyond the hedge, along the cliff road, I heard the distant clatter of a falling rifle and the heavy thud of a sentry collapsing at his post. The green mist thickened around us, cool and luminous, wrapping the warring nations and our own petty tragedy in a single, inescapable shroud.

I tried to cry out, to cling to my precious hatred, but it was slipping away like water through my fingers. My knees buckled beneath me. The revolver dropped noiselessly into the long grass, and I pitched forward onto the cool earth, sinking instantly into a dreamless, fathomless slumber.

When I opened my eyes, the sun was already high above the North Sea, and the green terror had vanished from a sky of spotless, crystalline blue. For a long moment I lay upon the grass, blinking in the pure morning light, unable to recall why I was there. My head felt remarkably clear—lighter and sharper than it had ever been in my twenty-four years of life. The dull ache of poverty, the suffocating grime of the Potteries, and the mad, murderous jealousy that had driven me across England seemed like the faded fragments of another man’s nightmare.

I sat up and looked around. A few paces away, Edward Verrall was rising slowly to his feet, brushing the gravel from his evening trousers. Nettie sat beside the veranda steps, her hands resting in her lap, gazing out toward the sparkling sea with a look of quiet wonder. There was no fear in her eyes when she turned them upon me, only a profound, gentle recognition.

Verrall walked over to where the revolver lay gleaming in the dew-wet grass. He picked it up, looked at it with mild curiosity, and then tossed it over the cliff edge into the tide below.

"You came a long way, Leadford," he said quietly, and there was neither anger nor condescension in his voice—only the simple candour of one man speaking to another.

"I was not myself," I answered, rising to meet him.

Down on the road, two soldiers in khaki were walking slowly toward the town, their rifles slung carelessly over their shoulders, talking earnestly together as if they had just woken from a long fever. As I looked at Nettie, and then at the wide, sunlit horizon, I knew that the war was over before it had begun, and that our old, bitter world had passed away in the night.
