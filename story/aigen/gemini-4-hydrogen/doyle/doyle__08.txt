It is a singular thing, looking back across the gulf of these peaceful years, to recall the black and bitter spirit in which I walked the streets of Swathinglea during that last week of the old world. I was then a clerk of twenty-two, pinched in pocket and narrower still in soul, earning eighteen shillings a week at the pot-banks while the great furnaces belched their sulphurous smoke across the Staffordshire sky. Everything in that grim valley seemed built of soot and disappointment. Yet it was not the poverty that ate into my heart like vitriol, nor even the feverish rumours of war that set every tavern and reading-room ablaze. It was the empty place beside the canal where Nettie had promised to meet me, and the crumpled note in my breast pocket that smelled faintly of lavender and treachery.

Every evening, as I trudged homeward past the slag-heaps, the great comet hung higher in the eastern heavens. At first it had been a mere smudge of pale green fire, a curiosity for the astronomers and a jest for the penny press. Now it dominated the twilight, casting a faint, unearthly emerald sheen upon the wet slates and the dark waters of the canal. Men stopped in the alleys to stare at it, their faces upturned and ghostly, while the newsboys ran past screaming of German ultimatums and the mobilisation of the fleet. Europe was sliding toward the abyss, and the very sky seemed to bleed a warning; yet I scarcely heeded it, for my own small universe had already fallen into ruin.

I had known Edward Verrall by sight since boyhood—a slim, fair-haired young gentleman from the Hall, with the easy carriage of one who had never counted a halfpenny in his life. That he should stoop to notice Nettie, the daughter of a humble gardener, had seemed at first a mere passing whim of his class. I had been blind, fool that I was, to the quiet meetings in the shrubbery, to the borrowed books, to the sudden restlessness in her dark eyes. When she vanished from her father’s cottage on the Tuesday morning, leaving only a few hurried lines begging me to forget her, the truth struck me with the force of a physical blow. They had taken the southern express. A porter at the station had seen them—Verrall in his grey tweed travelling coat, and Nettie veiled, stepping into a first-class carriage as though she had been born to it.

That afternoon I did not return to my desk. Instead, I walked down to a dingy pawnbroker’s shop in the lower quarter of Burslem, where a tray of tarnished watches and old firearms lay behind fly-blown glass. I laid down fifteen shillings—nearly the whole of my savings—for a heavy, six-chambered revolver of an antiquated pattern, together with a box of brass cartridges. The shopkeeper, a bleary-eyed old man in a greasy skullcap, asked no questions as he wrapped the weapon in brown paper. As I slipped the cold iron into my coat pocket, a terrible composure settled upon my mind. If the nations of the earth were preparing to settle their quarrels with steel and powder, then I, William Leadford, would not be left behind.

It was Thursday before I had scraped together the railway fare for Shaphambury, whither I had traced them through the gossip of a groomsman at the Hall. The journey southward was like a passage through a madhouse. Every carriage was packed with flushed, loud-voiced men who passed newspapers from hand to hand and shouted over the rattle of the wheels. At Rugby, a crowd of reservists sang patriotic ballads upon the platform, their faces already brutalised by cheap ale and the sudden licence of impending slaughter. At every junction the placards screamed blacker headlines: the North Sea fleet had cleared for action; ultimata had been exchanged in Berlin and Paris; the great powers were locked in a death-grip.

I sat silent in my corner, my fingers curled around the butt of the revolver in my pocket. Above the speeding train, as night fell, the comet had grown to a portentous size. Its vast, fan-shaped tail of luminous green stretched halfway across the zenith, dimming the familiar constellations and bathing the flying hedgerows of England in a sickly, phosphorescent glow. My fellow-passengers glanced out at it with uneasy jests, then turned back to their maps and their talk of battleships. They could not see that the same fever which drove the Admiralties of Europe to madness was burning behind my own eyes. I hated Verrall with a cold, concentrated fury that made me deaf to the clamour of the world. He had robbed me of the one bright thing in my grey existence, and I had come south to exact the reckoning.

I reached Shaphambury after sunset, when the little seaside town was already shrouded in the eerie emerald twilight of the comet. Down by the esplanade, crowds had gathered around the telegraph office, where a bulletin was being posted; a murmur of fierce excitement ran through them like wind through dry reeds, and I caught the words shots fired off the Dogger. Far out upon the dark, restless waters of the Channel, the white beams of naval searchlights swept nervously across the horizon.

I turned my back upon the town and climbed the steep chalk path toward the cliffs, where a cluster of secluded bungalows overlooked the bay. It was there, in a little walled garden fragrant with night-scented stock, that I found them. They were standing near the low stone parapet that bordered the cliff-edge, looking out toward the distant lights of the fleet. Nettie wore a pale cloak, and her hand rested lightly upon Verrall’s arm. The sight of that quiet, unconscious intimacy snapped the last thread of my restraint.

"Stand where you are, Verrall!" I cried, stepping from the shadow of the tamarisk hedge with the revolver levelled in my hand.

Nettie gave a sharp cry and shrank back against the stone wall. Verrall, to do him justice, showed no sign of cowardice. He stepped calmly in front of her, his face pale in the green starlight, and eyed my trembling weapon with steady composure.

"Put that down, Leadford," he said quietly. "You are beside yourself. What good can come of this?"

"You stole her from me!" I shouted, my voice cracking in my throat. "You with your fine clothes and your money! You thought a poor clerk would take his beating like a dog!"

I cocked the hammer. The metallic click sounded startlingly loud in the stillness of the garden. Nettie buried her face in her hands, sobbing my name, while Verrall braced himself as though to spring. My finger tightened upon the trigger—and at that very instant, the world seemed to hold its breath.

The distant, muffled thunder of naval guns that had been rolling across the Channel abruptly ceased. The wind dropped so suddenly that the leaves of the tamarisk hung motionless as carved stone. Then, from the upper air, there came a strange, rushing sound, like the tearing of a vast sheet of silk, followed by a cool, sweet breath that swept across our faces. Looking upward, I saw that the great green fan of the comet had broken, and a dense, luminous vapour was descending swiftly upon the earth.

It rolled over the chalk cliffs like a silent sea-fog, enveloping the bungalows, the town below, and the dark waters beyond. It was not cold, like the mists of Staffordshire, nor did it choke the lungs; rather, it carried a faint, intoxicating perfume, akin to heliotrope and fresh rain. A sudden, overwhelming languor stole into my limbs. My arm grew heavy as lead; the revolver slipped from my nerveless fingers and struck the gravel path with a dull clatter. Across the garden, I saw Verrall sway and sink gently to his knees beside the parapet, while Nettie crumpled softly onto the turf. My own eyes closed of their own accord, and I fell forward into the dewy grass, sinking effortlessly into a deep and dreamless sleep.

When I opened my eyes, the dawn was breaking over the Channel in a wash of pure, translucent gold. I sat up slowly, bewildered at first by the crystalline clarity of the air. Every blade of grass, every pebble upon the path, seemed sharper and more vivid than I had ever seen it, as though a thick, smoky film had been wiped from the face of creation. Beside my boot lay the heavy pawnbroker’s revolver. I stared at it for a long moment, and a wave of profound, quiet shame washed over me—not the hot, stinging humiliation of the past week, but a calm, clear-sighted sorrow for the cramped and venomous creature I had been. I picked up the weapon, broke open the chamber, and tossed both pistol and cartridges over the parapet into the deep water below.

A few paces away, Nettie was stirring. She sat up, pushing the dark hair from her forehead, and looked around her with wide, wondering eyes. Verrall rose slowly beside her, brushing the damp earth from his coat. For a space of perhaps a minute, the three of us stood silent in the morning light, looking into one another’s faces. The fear had vanished from Nettie’s eyes; the cool arrogance was gone from Verrall’s bearing; and in my own breast, the old, festering hatred had dissolved like mist before the sun. Far out upon the sea, the great grey warships lay motionless upon the glassy water, their searchlights extinguished, their crews waking—as all Europe was waking—to the same miraculous sanity.

"William," Nettie said softly, and there was neither guilt nor dread in her voice, only a gentle pity.

"I was mad last night," I answered, and my voice sounded strange and steady in my own ears. "We were all mad. Come—let us go down into the town together and see what the new day has brought us."
