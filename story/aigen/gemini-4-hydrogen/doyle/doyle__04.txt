The smoke of Staffordshire hung low over the huddled roofs of Clayton, choking the narrow streets with the bitter breath of the kilns. I stood at the window of my mother’s cramped parlour, a crumpled sheet of notepaper clenched in my ink-stained fist, and stared out at the blackened chimney-stacks. Nettie was gone. She had left no word of remorse—only three hurried lines in her neat, childish hand to tell me that she could not bear the poverty, the grime, or my own dark tempers any longer, and that she had gone away with Mr. Edward Verrall. Verrall! The very name tasted of gall upon my tongue. He was the son of the great house upon the hill, a young gentleman with soft hands, a smooth laugh, and pockets lined with gold wrung from the sweat of men like me. For two years I had saved every spare shilling from my clerk’s pittance to build a modest home for Nettie, and he had plucked her away as carelessly as a man might pluck a wildflower from a hedge. Outside, the evening sky had turned a sickly, bruised violet, and above the jagged silhouette of the blast furnaces there hung a singular star. It was no honest, twinkling point of light, but a bloated, greenish orb with a faint, luminous smudge trailing behind it like a smear of phosphorus. The astronomers in London called it a comet, and the parsons muttered of judgment; to me, it seemed a fitting lantern for a world rotten with injustice.

Three days later I sat in the corner of a third-class railway carriage, rattling southward through the gathering dusk. In my overcoat pocket lay a heavy, old-fashioned revolver which I had purchased from a pawnbroker in Burslem, along with a box of cartridges. My mind was a furnace of single-minded fury. I had traced them through a gossiping porter at the junction—they had booked tickets for Shaphambury, a quiet watering-place upon the southern coast. Across from me, two commercial travellers were leaning over an evening newspaper, their faces flushed and their voices raised in angry debate. Every sheet in England was screaming of war. Our cruisers were steaming out of Portsmouth; the German fleet was reported massing in the North Sea; ultimatums were flying between the chancelleries of Europe like sparks in a powder-magazine. The whole continent seemed gripped by the same blind, murderous fever that was burning in my own veins. When I stepped out onto the platform at Shaphambury, the night air was heavy and strangely still. Looking up, I saw that the green comet had swollen to an appalling size. It hung halfway up the heavens, a monstrous, glowing sickle of emerald fire that bathed the whitewashed walls of the station and the upturned, anxious faces of the passengers in an unearthly, cadaverous light. No one spoke of anything else, save the coming slaughter across the water. I pulled my cap down over my eyes, gripped the cold butt of my revolver, and walked out into the darkened town.

Shaphambury lay silent beneath the uncanny radiance of the sky, a place of neat gardens and chalk cliffs that seemed a thousand miles removed from the soot and clamour of the Potteries. Out to sea, the long, pale fingers of naval searchlights swept nervously across the black water, probing the horizon for an enemy that might strike before dawn. It took me less than an hour to find the house. A cabman, bribed with a shilling I could ill afford, pointed me toward a secluded villa perched near the edge of the eastern cliffs, half-hidden behind a wall of tamarisk and wind-bent pines. I slipped through the wooden gate and crept along the border of the lawn, my boots muffled by the damp turf. A pair of French windows stood open to the warm night, spilling a rectangle of warm, yellow lamplight across the terrace. From within came the low murmur of conversation, and then a burst of light, careless laughter. My heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird. Crouching behind a cluster of laurels, I peered into the room. Verrall was lounging in a wicker chair, dressed in white flannels, a glass of wine at his elbow. Beside him sat Nettie, her dark hair catching the lamplight, her face turned up to his with an expression of breathless adoration. She looked younger, softer, and utterly free of the care that had haunted her in Staffordshire. A cold, terrible calm settled upon me. I drew the revolver from my pocket and thumbed back the hammer.

Before I could step onto the terrace, Verrall rose and offered his arm to Nettie. Together they walked out into the garden, strolling toward the low stone parapet that overlooked the sea. They stopped not ten paces from my hiding-place, gazing upward at the vast, terrifying splendour of the comet, which now filled a quarter of the southern sky with a pulsing, greenish glare. I stepped out from behind the laurels, the pistol levelled at Verrall’s chest. Nettie gave a sharp, strangled cry and clutched at his sleeve. Verrall stiffened, peering at me through the eerie twilight, his handsome face draining of colour as he recognized me. "Leadford!" he stammered, thrusting Nettie behind him. "Put that down, man! Are you mad?" I opened my mouth to speak—to pour out all the bitterness of my starved, cheated life—when a sudden, violent rushing sound filled the upper air. It was not wind, for the leaves upon the laurels hung motionless, yet a mighty, invisible tide seemed to sweep down from the stars. A dense, luminous green vapour rolled in across the cliffs, swallowing the searchlights, swallowing the sea, and enveloping the garden in an instant. A strange, sweet odour, sharp as bruised mint and infinitely piercing, filled my nostrils. My knees buckled beneath me. I saw Verrall stagger and fall, saw Nettie sink beside him like a broken lily, and then the green darkness closed over my eyes, and I knew no more.

I awoke to a world washed clean of shadow. For several moments I lay upon the cool grass, staring up into a sky of the purest, most crystalline blue, in which no trace of the comet remained. A lark was singing somewhere high above the cliffs, its notes falling like drops of clear water through the tranquil morning air. Slowly I pushed myself up upon one elbow. Beside me lay the revolver, its steel barrel dull in the sunlight. I looked at it with a sudden, overwhelming sense of wonder and disgust, as though it were a foul implement belonging to some savage ancestor, and I thrust it away into the bushes. My mind felt extraordinarily clear, stripped of the hot, suffocating fog of jealousy and rage that had poisoned my every waking hour for months. A few yards away, Verrall was sitting up, rubbing his forehead with a bewildered air, while Nettie stirred upon the flagstones of the terrace. When our eyes met, there was no fear in her face, and no hatred in mine. It was as if a fever had broken during the night, leaving us weak, humbled, and curiously akin. Verrall rose slowly to his feet and looked at me—not with the supercilious stare of the squire’s son, nor with the terror of a cornered man, but with a grave, quiet honesty. "We have been asleep, Leadford," he said softly, his voice steady in the morning stillness. "I think we have all been asleep for a very long time."

Together, the three of us walked down the winding lane toward Shaphambury, saying little, yet bound by a strange and gentle silence. As we entered the town, we saw that the streets were already filled with people—shopkeepers in their aprons, fishermen from the quay, and soldiers in khaki—all standing in quiet groups beneath the bright sun. There was no shouting, no drunken bravado, no panic. At the corner by the post office, a telegraph boy was pasting a fresh bulletin upon the board, and a small crowd had gathered to read it. We drew near and looked over their shoulders. The ink was still wet upon the paper: Fleet recalled to Portsmouth. Negotiations opened at The Hague. Mobilization suspended by mutual consent. A murmur of quiet thankfulness ran through the little crowd, and I saw a grizzled sergeant of artillery reach out and clap a passing dock-worker warmly upon the shoulder. Turning away, I looked at Nettie. She was gazing out toward the sparkling Channel, where the white sails of fishing-smacks were already putting out into the calm water. The old, grasping love I had borne her—the love that sought to possess and cage—had vanished with the green mist, leaving in its place a deep, tender wish for her happiness, whatever path she might choose. Beside her, Verrall met my gaze and held out his hand. I took it firmly, feeling for the first time in my life neither beneath him nor above him, but simply a man greeting his brother at the dawn of a new day.
