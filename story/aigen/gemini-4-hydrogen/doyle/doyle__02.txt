In the autumn of that memorable year, the Black Country lay beneath a perpetual pall of coal-smoke and despair, and nowhere was the gloom deeper than in my own embittered heart. I was a clerk then at the Checkshill ironworks—a lean, hollow-cheeked young man of three-and-twenty, with ink-stained fingers and a soul that chafed against the narrow iron cage of poverty. Eighteen shillings a week was my portion, and for that I bartered my youth in a dingy counting-house, while my mother coughed her life away in a damp terrace off the Clayton Road. Yet I had one bright thread woven into that drab existence: Nettie Stuart, the head gardener's daughter at Checkshill House. She had been my childhood playmate, my secret betrothed, the sole star in my smoky firmament. Then came young Edward Verrall, with his Oxford manners, his tailored tweeds, and his father's colliery gold. When the news reached me that Tuesday evening—that Nettie had slipped away by the afternoon express to join him at a quiet seaside bungalow in the south—the last barrier of my self-restraint gave way. I sat in my cold bedroom, staring out over the jagged silhouette of the pit-heads. Above the furnaces, hanging low in the eastern sky, burned the great green comet which the astronomers had been predicting for months. Its cold, emerald light fell across my table, glinting upon the barrel of a cheap five-chambered revolver I had bought at a Wolverhampton pawnshop. My mind was made up. If I could not have her, neither should he.

By ten o'clock the next morning I was wedged into the corner of a third-class smoking carriage, rattling southward through the Midlands. The air inside was thick with the acrid fumes of shag tobacco and damp wool, and the wooden bench vibrated beneath me with the feverish pulse of the engine. My companions—a red-faced commercial traveller, a taciturn farmer, and a nervous little solicitor's clerk—had their heads buried in the morning editions. The talk was all of war. The German ultimatum had expired at midnight; troops were massing along the Rhine; the Channel Fleet had already slipped its moorings at Portsmouth under sealed orders. The traveller thumped his knee with a rolled newspaper, declaring in a loud, aggressive voice that it was high time the Prussian bully was taught a lesson. I listened to them with a dull, detached contempt. What were kings and Kaisers to a man whose own small world had been shattered? My right hand rested within my coat pocket, my fingers curled tightly around the checkered grip of the revolver. All day the train tore through the yellowing shires, past quiet villages and bustling junctions where khaki-clad reservists stood waiting upon the platforms. As dusk settled over the southern downs, the carriage lamps were lit, casting a sickly yellow glow upon our drawn faces. Yet whenever I pressed my forehead against the cold window-pane, I saw that the green comet had risen higher, swelling visibly in the darkening vault until its pale, unearthly radiance seemed to mock the frantic little quarrels of mankind.

It was past nine when I alighted at the little coastal station of Shaphambury. The salt wind struck my face with a sudden, bracing chill, yet beneath the clean tang of the sea there lingered a curious, sharp odour, like ozone after a summer storm. I turned my back upon the town's gas-lit promenade and struck out along the cliff path toward the lonely stretch of shingle where the bungalows stood. I had learned the name of the place from a gossiping porter at Checkshill—Sea View, a solitary timbered cottage perched above the chalk downs. The night was remarkably still, save for the rhythmic hiss of the tide upon the pebbles far below. Out in the blackness of the Channel, however, the darkness was alive with movement. Long, ghostly fingers of white light swept ceaselessly across the water, probing the horizon. Twice I caught the dark, low silhouette of a cruiser steaming without navigation lights, her funnels trailing faint plumes of smoke against the emerald-tinted sky. Europe was sliding into the abyss, and even here, on this quiet English headland, the shadow of the coming slaughter lay heavy upon the land. I quickened my pace, my boots crunching upon the flinty track. Around a bend in the path, screened by a wind-bitten hedge of tamarisk, I saw the warm amber glow of curtained windows. My heart gave a savage leap against my ribs. I drew the revolver from my pocket, checked the chambers with a thumb that scarcely trembled, and crept softly toward the wooden gate.

The latch was unfastened, and I slipped into the small, neglected garden without a sound. Through the half-drawn curtains of the French windows, the whole interior of the sitting-room lay open to my gaze like a stage. A lamp burned upon a round oak table laid with cold meats and a bottle of claret. Nettie stood by the mantelpiece, wearing a simple dress of pale muslin. Even through the haze of my fury, I could see that she was troubled; her brow was furrowed, and she twisted a handkerchief nervously between her fingers. Opposite her leaned Edward Verrall, handsome and debonair in his evening flannel, speaking to her in low, persuasive tones. As I watched, he stepped forward and laid a hand upon her shoulder. A red mist seemed to swim before my eyes. I stepped out from the shelter of the tamarisk bush and set my foot upon the gravel path. At that precise instant, the night was rent by a distant, thunderous boom from the sea, followed quickly by another. The glass in the French windows rattled violently in its frame. Nettie gave a sharp cry, and Verrall, his face suddenly pale, strode to the glass doors and flung them open, stepping out onto the veranda to stare toward the Channel. Nettie followed close at his heels. I stood not ten paces from them, bathed in the eerie green glare of the comet. I raised my arm, sighting squarely at Verrall's white shirt-front, and called his name in a voice hoarse with hatred.

Verrall spun round with an oath, thrusting Nettie instinctively behind him. For a heartbeat we stood frozen—he with his eyes wide in sudden recognition, she with her hands pressed to her mouth, and I with my finger tightening upon the cold iron trigger. Yet before the hammer could fall, a strange and terrible change came over the night. From the east, where the comet's vast luminous tail now spanned half the heavens, a rolling bank of pale green vapour came sweeping swiftly across the downs. It moved faster than any earthly wind, silent and enveloping, swallowing the distant flashes of the naval guns and extinguishing the searching beams out at sea. As the mist closed around the veranda, I drew in a deep breath. The air was no longer cold; it was warm, heavy, and filled with a sweet, intoxicating scent, like crushed almonds and summer clover. Instantly, an overwhelming languor seized my limbs. My arm dropped to my side as though weighted with lead. I tried to cry out, to summon back the righteous rage that had sustained me across two hundred miles of England, but my thoughts were dissolving like smoke in a clear sky. The revolver slipped from my numb fingers and fell with a muffled thud into the dewy turf. Looking up through swimming eyes, I saw Nettie sway gently and sink into a wicker chair, while Verrall slid slowly down against the wooden railing of the veranda. Then the green darkness closed over my senses, and I fell forward upon the grass into a deep, dreamless oblivion.

I awoke to a silence more profound than any I had ever known. There was no rumble of distant trains, no thunder of naval artillery, not even the faintest murmur of wind in the tamarisk hedge. Above me, the sky was an immaculate dome of pale gold and turquoise, swept clean of every cloud and every trace of the green vapour. I sat up slowly, brushing the damp grass from my coat, and stared about me in wonder. Something within my own breast had undergone a transformation as complete as that of the heavens. The hot, suffocating fever of jealousy, the bitter resentment of my poverty, the murderous pride that had driven me south—all had been washed away, leaving behind a cool, crystalline lucidity of mind. Beside my boot lay the little revolver, its nickel plating dull with dew. I picked it up, looked at it with a quiet, detached pity for the blind fool I had been, and tossed it far over the cliff edge into the tide below. Turning toward the veranda, I saw that Verrall and Nettie were stirring. Verrall rose first, passing a hand across his forehead, and met my eyes. There was no fear in his face, nor any trace of his old careless arrogance—only a grave, wondering recognition, as though we were two brothers waking from the same dark nightmare. Nettie stood beside him, her eyes clear and unafraid, gazing at me with a gentle, sorrowful understanding. Without a word of reproach or excuse, I walked up the steps to join them, and together we stood looking out over the sunlit Channel, where the great grey warships drifted silent and forgotten upon a peaceful sea.
