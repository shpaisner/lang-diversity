If you had walked through the grim thoroughfares of Clayton in that autumn of the Great Fear, you would have thought the whole world was choked by coal-smoke and malice. I was twenty-four years of age, a clerk of the lowest grade in a potteries warehouse, with frayed cuffs, a hollow chest, and a heart soured by poverty. Every morning I trudged past the belching kilns under a sky the color of slate, earning barely enough to keep my mother and myself from the parish. Yet my private misery was only a small thread in a vast, dark tapestry. Across the Continent, the great powers were snarling like hounds on the leash. Each evening the newsboys ran through the damp streets crying of ultimatums, of mobilized armies, and of ironclads steaming into the North Sea. Men gathered under the flickering gas-lamps with flushed faces, shouting for blood, as though war might somehow cure the emptiness of their pockets. And high above the chimneys, mocking our noisy little quarrels, hung the Stranger. Night by night the great green comet had swelled in the heavens, until its luminous tail stretched like a phantom scimitar across the stars. The learned men in London wrote calmly of harmless gases and celestial mechanics, yet no man could look up at that cold, emerald glare without feeling a singular chill at the pit of his stomach. For my own part, I cared little whether Europe burned or the heavens fell, so long as Nettie remained mine. She was the one bright thing in my smoky existence, and I clung to the thought of her with the desperate fierceness of a drowning man.

It was upon a Tuesday evening that my small world shattered. I had walked out past the canal to the row of brick cottages where Nettie lived with her widowed aunt, carrying in my pocket a cheap silver brooch I had starved myself for a month to buy. I found the front door ajar and the kitchen dimly lit by a single tallow candle. Mrs. Mason sat by the cold hearth, her apron twisted in her knotted hands, her face streaked with tears. She did not rise as I entered, but merely pointed a trembling finger toward a folded scrap of paper upon the deal table. I snatched it up and held it to the flame. The handwriting was Nettie’s—neat, rounded, and merciless. She had gone, she wrote, because she could bear the grime and the hopelessness of Staffordshire no longer. She begged me to forgive her, though I knew she was already beyond caring whether I forgave her or not. At the bottom of the page, Mrs. Mason’s broken voice supplied the name the letter had omitted: young Verrall. He was the son of the great colliery owner, a gentleman with sleek hair, a motor-car, and a pocket full of gold. I stood very still in that miserable kitchen while a cold, white fury settled over my brain. The silver brooch felt like a piece of lead against my thigh. Without a word to the weeping woman, I turned upon my heel and walked back into the black night, my mind already fixed upon a single, terrible purpose.

By noon the next day I had pawned my Sunday coat and my father’s silver watch, and with the proceeds I stepped into a dingy gunsmith’s shop in a back alley of Hanley. The proprietor, a blear-eyed fellow who asked no questions of a desperate man, sold me a stout, six-chambered revolver and a box of brass cartridges. I dropped the heavy weapon into my ulster pocket, where it knocked rhythmically against my knee as I hurried to the railway station. Having learned from a gossiping neighbor that Verrall had taken a cottage near the Sussex coast at Shaphambury, I purchased a third-class ticket south and wedged myself into a crowded carriage. The compartment was thick with shag tobacco and the fever of coming war. Two commercial travelers argued fiercely over a map of Belgium, while a red-faced grazier swore that the fleet would sink the enemy before Sunday. I sat in the corner with my hat pulled low, saying nothing, my fingers curled tightly around the cold iron butt in my pocket. As dusk fell, the train rattled through the darkened shires, and through the soot-stained glass I watched the comet rise. It was no longer a mere star, but a vast, glowing presence that turned the clouds to the hue of verdigris. Whenever the train stopped at some country junction, I could hear the distant cheering of crowds and the blare of brass bands, yet within my own breast there was only the silent, steady ticking of my resolve.

I reached Shaphambury after midnight, stepping out of the little station into a sharp salt wind that blew straight off the Channel. The town was wide awake and strangely illuminated. No street-lamps were needed, for the comet now dominated the zenith, pouring down a ghostly, greenish radiance that made the white chalk cliffs look like the walls of a sepulchre. Far out upon the black water, the great searchlights of the defending fleet swept back and forth, their pale fingers probing the gloom for an unseen enemy. I knew little of the coast, yet some dark instinct guided my steps along the high cliff road toward the solitary villa where I had been told Verrall was lodging. I had walked scarcely half a mile when I heard the crunch of gravel ahead. I slipped into the deep shadow of a gorse thicket and waited, my heart hammering against my ribs. Presently two figures came into view around a bend in the path. There was no mistaking Verrall’s tall, easy carriage, nor the light laugh that floated back upon the wind. Beside him walked Nettie, wrapped in a fur-trimmed cloak I had never seen before, her arm linked securely through his. As they drew abreast of my hiding-place, I stepped out onto the chalk path, blocking their way. The emerald light fell full upon my drawn face as I leveled the revolver at Verrall’s chest. Nettie gave a sharp, strangled cry, and for three seconds we stood frozen like figures carved in stone.

"Stand back, Nettie," Verrall said, and I will do him the justice to admit that his voice scarcely trembled. He thrust her behind his shoulder and stared straight down the barrel of my weapon, his handsome face pale in the unearthly glare. "Put that down, Leadford," he added steadily. "You are mad." I opened my mouth to curse him, to tell him that a man who has been robbed of everything has no fear of the gallows, when a sudden, singular change came over the night. The sharp salt breeze died away in an instant, leaving the air as still and heavy as the interior of a vault. From the eastern horizon, a vast wall of pale green vapor seemed to roll across the sea, swallowing the distant searchlights one by one. A strange, sweetish odor, faint at first like bruised almonds and then overpowering, filled my nostrils. My finger twitched upon the trigger, yet the muscles of my arm had suddenly turned to water. Nettie sank to her knees upon the turf, clutching at her throat, while Verrall staggered sideways against a wooden railing, his eyes wide with bewilderment. A roaring sound filled my ears, like the rushing of a mighty river, and the green sky seemed to tilt and spin above me. The heavy revolver slipped from my nerveless grasp and struck the gravel with a dull clatter. I tried to take a step forward, failed, and pitched headlong into the damp grass as a deep, dreamless darkness closed over my senses.

When I opened my eyes, the green terror had vanished from the heavens. A clear, golden dawn was breaking over the Channel, and the air that filled my lungs was the sweetest, crispest thing I had ever breathed, tasting of summer meadows and clean rain. I sat up slowly upon the dewy turf, expecting the old familiar ache of hatred to return to my heart. Instead, I felt a profound and lucid calm, as though a thick, poisonous fog had been blown forever from the chambers of my brain. Beside my boot lay the revolver; I looked at it with mild curiosity, wondering what strange madness had driven me to carry so ugly a toy, and tossed it over the cliff edge into the sea. A few paces away, Verrall was helping Nettie to her feet. They looked at me, and I at them, without fear or anger. The petty jealousies, the bitter pride of class, the blind, murderous rage—all had been washed away in that night of celestial sleep. Far out upon the quiet water, the dark hulls of the warships lay motionless, their signal-flags hanging limp in the morning breeze, as if the men aboard them had likewise woken from a fevered dream. "You are unhurt, Leadford?" Verrall asked gently, extending his hand. I took it firmly, feeling no sting of humiliation, and turned to Nettie with a quiet smile. Behind us, the world was waking to a new and saner day, and for the first time in my life, I was not afraid to meet it.
