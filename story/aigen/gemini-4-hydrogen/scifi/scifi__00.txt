In those last bruised weeks of the old dispensation, Staffordshire lay beneath a perpetual lid of coal-smoke and chemical haze, as though the earth itself were smoldering in sullen resentment. William Leadford knew every cinder-path and slag-heap between Clayton and Swathinglea, and he hated them all with the pinched, helpless fury of a man whose youth was being ground into cheap earthenware and ledger ink. At twenty-two, his shoulders already carried the stoop of the counting-house, and his lungs rattled with the damp chill of his mother’s cramped parlor. Yet it was not merely poverty that soured his blood; it was the vast, inexplicable stupidity of the world. Every evening, the penny papers screamed of ultimatums, of ironclads steaming through the North Sea, of mobilization orders pinning Europe to the brink of slaughter. Men argued in the public houses with red faces and clenched fists, eager for a war they could neither understand nor survive. And above it all—above the blast-furnaces flaring orange against the soot, above the tramlines and the tenements—hung the Comet. At first it had been a mere rumor in the astronomical columns, a faint smudge of green light near the Pleiades. By August, it had swollen into a pale, unblinking eye that watched the grime of the Potteries with cold, celestial indifference. Leadford would stand in the cinder-yard behind his lodgings, staring up at that eerie emerald nucleus, and feel a strange kinship with its silent menace. Let it fall, he thought. Let it strike the earth and shatter this hideous ant-heap of masters and slaves.

Then came the Tuesday when Nettie vanished, and the sky’s slow menace narrowed to a single, burning point in Leadford’s brain. He had loved her since they were children gathering blackened blackberries along the canal bank—loved her with a fierce, possessive desperation that was half tenderness and half bruised pride. She was the one clean, bright thing in a landscape of ash. When her aunt handed him the folded scrap of paper, his fingers tore the cheap stationery before he had finished reading the three lines of cramped apology. She had gone to the coast. She had gone with Edward Verrall. The name tasted like copper and bile. Verrall was the son of the great colliery owner, a young man of immaculate flannels, effortless laughter, and pockets lined with sovereigns—a creature from that sunlit upper world which looked down upon men like Leadford as mere cogs in the machinery. All that afternoon, Leadford wandered the smoky streets in a blind, shivering stupor. The newspapers were placarding the outbreak of hostilities in the Channel, crowds were cheering outside the town hall, and the air smelled of sulfur and coming rain. In a pawnshop near the railway arches, he laid down his last two weeks’ savings for a squat, nickel-plated revolver and a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker barely looked up from his racing form. That night, as Leadford packed a single bag, the comet filled a quarter of the northern sky, its tail a luminous, ghostly fan of green fire that cast faint, unnatural shadows across his bedroom floor.

The southbound train was a rattling cage of panic and bad temper. Reservists in ill-fitting khaki crowded the corridors, their voices hoarse with cheap beer and premature bravado, while merchants in the first-class compartments argued furiously over collapsing stocks and blockaded ports. Leadford sat wedged in a corner seat, his coat buttoned to the chin, his right hand resting perpetually against the cold, heavy bulge in his pocket. Nobody noticed him. He was merely another pale, hollow-eyed clerk fleeing the industrial Midlands. Yet as the train hurtled past darkened fields and silent, waiting stations, he felt himself growing larger than the fools around him—an instrument of pure, geometric justice. By the time he stepped onto the platform at Shaphambury, dusk was settling over the English Channel, and the town seemed suspended in a trance of dread. The promenade was nearly deserted; the holidaymakers had fled inland at the first rumble of distant naval guns. Out beyond the breakwater, the gray water heaved under a sky that was no longer blue or black, but a deep, translucent jade. The comet had swelled until its head burned like a green sun behind a veil of mist, bathing the whitewashed villas and the chalk cliffs in a spectral, underwater glow. Leadford walked along the esplanade, his boots crunching loudly on the shingle-strewn pavement. Far out at sea, the searchlights of a cruiser squadron swept the horizon in nervous, sweeping arcs. He did not care about the fleets, nor the empires trembling on the edge of ruin. He had a name, a face, and six chambers of lead.

He found them a mile beyond the town, where the promenade gave way to a lonely stretch of turf above the cliffs. They were standing near a wooden shelter, looking out toward the illuminated horizon, Verrall’s arm resting lightly across Nettie’s shoulders. Even at fifty paces, Leadford recognized the easy, proprietary grace of the gesture, and a white-hot tide surged behind his eyes. He stepped off the gravel path onto the grass, his footsteps muffled, and drew the revolver. "Nettie!" he called, his voice cracking like dry wood. They turned together. Nettie gasped, her hand flying to her throat, while Verrall instinctively stepped half a pace in front of her, his handsome face hardening in the emerald twilight. "Leadford," Verrall said, keeping his voice low and steady. "Put that thing down. You’re out of your mind." Leadford raised the barrel, his arm trembling with the accumulated misery of twenty-two years. He wanted to speak—to shout all the bitter truths of the pit-banks and the slums—but his throat was locked. His finger tightened on the trigger. Before the hammer could fall, a strange, rushing sound filled the upper air, like the sigh of a vast, unseen forest. The green light of the comet suddenly flared into blinding, incandescent brilliance, swallowing the stars and the distant searchlights alike. A warm, sweet-smelling vapor, heavy with an unfamiliar, exhilarating pungency, rolled down from the sky in billowing, luminous curtains. Leadford’s knees buckled. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers into the grass. He saw Nettie sink slowly to the turf, saw Verrall sway and drop beside her, and then the emerald mist closed over his eyes, bearing him down into a profound and dreamless silence.

When Leadford opened his eyes, the world had been washed clean. A pale, golden dawn was breaking over a waveless sea, and the air he drew into his lungs tasted like mountain spring water—crisp, sweet, and devoid of the slightest taint of coal-smoke or salt-damp. He sat up slowly, expecting the familiar ache in his ribs, the dull throb of bitterness behind his temples. Instead, his mind felt extraordinarily clear, like a still pool from which every particle of mud had settled. Beside him in the dew-soaked clover lay the nickel-plated revolver. He picked it up, turned it over in his palm, and felt a sudden, overwhelming wave of astonishment—not horror, precisely, but a quiet, detached pity for the wretched creature he had been the night before. How small, how frantically blind that anger seemed in the luminous breadth of this morning! He tossed the weapon over the cliff’s edge, listening faintly for the distant splash among the rocks below. Turning, he saw Verrall sitting up a few yards away, rubbing his forehead with a bewildered expression, while Nettie stirred beside him, her dark hair tangled with blades of grass. There was no panic in their waking. They looked around at the radiant sky, at the pale green ghost of the comet now fading harmlessly into the eastern light, as though they too had emerged from a long, feverish delirium. Far down the coast, the town of Shaphambury lay utterly still; no sirens wailed, no newsboys shouted of slaughter in the Channel. It was as if the great, clumsy machine of human hatred had simply stopped in the night.

Leadford stood up and walked across the wet turf toward them. As he approached, Verrall rose to his feet, and for the first time in his life, Leadford saw no arrogance in the young man’s posture—only a candid, open-eyed wonder that matched his own. Nettie stood beside him, her face pale in the morning sun, yet stripped of all the frightened defiance she had worn in Staffordshire. "Will," she said softly, and there was neither guilt nor pleading in her voice, only the simple recognition of one human soul greeting another. "I am sorry for last night," Leadford said, and marveled at how easily the words came, without a shred of humiliation. "I was sick with a madness we all shared. Look at the sky. Something has passed over us." Verrall nodded slowly, gazing out over the tranquil sea where the warships now drifted like harmless gray toys, their searchlights extinguished, their crews doubtless waking to the same inexplicable peace. "It’s gone," Verrall murmured, as if speaking of a lifelong burden. "All the noise, all the urgency... I can’t even remember why we thought we had to fight, or why we built such ugly things." They stood together on the cliff-top, the clerk, the master’s son, and the girl from the Potteries, bound no longer by the squalid jealousies of the old world, but by a quiet, expansive fellowship. Leadford looked at Nettie’s hand resting near his own, and felt no sting of loss. He loved her still, he realized, yet with a vast, unselfish clarity—as one loves the morning light, or the promise of a green earth waking at last from its long, dark sleep. Below them, smoke began to rise from a single chimney in the town, thin and white against the blue, marking the first quiet hearth of the new day.
