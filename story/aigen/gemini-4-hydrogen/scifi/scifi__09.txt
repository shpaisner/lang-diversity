The smoke of the Five Towns hung low over the Staffordshire valleys, a greasy, sulphurous blanket that trapped the heat of the blast furnaces and turned the twilight into a perpetual bruise. William Leadford walked along the cinder path beside the canal, his boots grey with dust, his cheap collar chafing his neck. Above the jagged silhouettes of the bottle kilns, the sky was clearing just enough to reveal the Thing that had occupied the astronomers for months: a pale, greenish-gold smear, no longer a mere point of light, but a vast, feathery plume stretching across the zenith.

In his coat pocket, William’s fingers worried the crumpled pages of Nettie’s final letter. She had gone with Verrall. It was a simple, brutal equation of the world as William knew it: Verrall possessed a motorcar, a smooth, unhurried voice, and the leisure afforded by his father’s collieries; William possessed twelve shillings a week as a junior clerk, a mother with a hacking cough, and a mind poisoned by envy. At his elbow, a crumpled newspaper discarded on a stile screamed of mobilization in the Balkans, of German cruisers steaming through the North Sea, of a continent sliding helplessly toward the abyss of war. The whole world, it seemed to William, was a cramped, ill-tempered cage, and the great green star overhead was merely a cold witness to its impending ruin. He spat into the stagnant canal. Let the empires burn, he thought, so long as he could settle his own small, venomous account before the end.

By the following afternoon, William had pawned his silver watch—the only heirloom of a respectable, defeated family—and purchased a second-hand revolver from a dim shop near the railway arches. He boarded the southbound train with the heavy iron weight dragging at his ulster pocket, his jaw set in a rigid mask of injured pride. The carriages were packed with reservists summoned to their depots, young men with flushed faces who sang patriotic music-hall songs to drown out the rhythmic clatter of the wheels. Yet whenever the train rattled over a viaduct, the singing would falter as faces pressed against the grimy glass to stare eastward.

Even in broad daylight, the comet was now visible—a ghostly, emerald-tinted crescent hanging like a scimitar above the hazy horizon. An old woman in the corner muttered about the Book of Revelation, while a red-faced commercial traveler loudly insisted that the Admiralty would have the German fleet at the bottom of the Channel before the week was out. William said nothing. He watched the soot-blackened Midlands give way to the lush, indifferent pastures of the south, nursing his hatred like a banked coal. He knew where Verrall’s family kept their seaside villa, a white-stuccoed retreat perched above the chalk cliffs of Shaphambury. As the train hissed to a halt at the coastal terminus, twilight was falling once more, and the air smelled sharply of salt and ozone. William stepped onto the platform, invisible among the anxious crowds, and turned his face toward the cliffs.

The promenade was deserted, the summer visitors having fled inland at the morning’s rumors of naval bombardment. William climbed the winding cliff path, his breath coming in short, ragged gasps, his boots slipping on the chalk. Above him, the comet had swollen into an astonishing spectacle, a luminous fan of jade and silver that cast faint, double shadows across the turf. He reached the laurel hedge bordering the Verrall villa and crouched in the damp grass, peering through the gaps.

The French windows of the drawing-room stood open to the warm evening. Inside, bathed in the golden glow of oil lamps, Nettie sat at a small table, wearing a dress of pale muslin that William had never seen. Verrall stood beside her, leaning over the back of her chair, speaking in low, laughing tones as he poured wine into two tall glasses. The sight of their effortless, sunlit ease—so utterly remote from the grime and pinched desperation of Staffordshire—sent a blinding surge of blood to William’s temples. He stepped through the gap in the hedge, his boots crunching loudly on the gravel terrace, and raised the revolver.

Nettie saw him first. Her wineglass slipped from her fingers, shattering on the parquet floor. Verrall spun around, his handsome face draining of color as he thrust Nettie behind him. "Leadford!" Verrall barked, his voice cracking between terror and aristocratic indignation. "Put that down, you lunatic!" William’s finger tightened on the cold iron trigger. Yet at that precise instant, the sea breeze died into an absolute, suffocating stillness, and a strange, sweet fragrance—like crushed mint and summer rain—washed over the terrace.

William tried to squeeze the trigger, to summon the white-hot fury that had carried him across half of England, but a sudden, profound languor seemed to unstring his sinews. He looked up, bewildered. The vast green plume of the comet was no longer a distant spectacle in the heavens; it had descended. A luminous, emerald mist was swirling down from the upper air, thickening with every breath, swallowing the distant headland and the dark expanse of the Channel in a soft, phosphorescent fog.

Inside the drawing-room, the oil lamps sputtered and burned with a strange, bluish halo. Nettie sank slowly onto the edge of a wicker chair, her wide eyes fixed not on the gun in William’s hand, but on the glowing vapor curling through the open doorway. Verrall swayed, catching himself against the mantelpiece, his mouth opening as if to speak, though no sound emerged. Across the town below the cliffs, a distant brass band that had been playing marching tunes on the pier faltered into a discordant groan and fell utterly silent.

The air grew cool and intoxicatingly sweet. William’s hatred did not merely fade; it seemed to dissolve like soot in clean water, leaving behind a vast, quiet emptiness. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and fell harmlessly onto the turf. He dropped to his knees, resting his forehead against the cool stone of the terrace step, watching the green luminescence pool around his hands. There was no pain, no terror of the end—only an overwhelming, velvety drowsiness that wrapped the warring nations and his own small, broken heart in a single, merciful stupor.

When William opened his eyes, the world had been washed clean. It was morning, and a sun of extraordinary brilliance hung over a glassy, silver sea, yet the light possessed a crispness, a piercing lucidity, that made his old memories of daylight seem like peering through smoked glass. He sat up on the terrace, touching his face in wonder. The chronic, dull ache behind his eyes—the lifelong companion of bad air, poor food, and simmering resentment—had vanished entirely.

Beside his knee lay the cheap revolver, its blued steel glistening with dew. William picked it up, turned it over in his palm, and felt a sudden, sharp pang of pity—not for himself, but for the frantic, cornered animal he had been only twelve hours ago. How absurd, how pitifully cramped, that anger seemed in the vast clarity of this new morning! He laid the weapon gently down and rose to his feet, brushing the damp grass from his trousers.

On the threshold of the French windows, Verrall was sitting upon the oak sill, his elbows on his knees, staring at his own outstretched hands as if seeing them for the first time. Behind him, Nettie stood clutching the doorframe. Her face was pale, her eyes rimmed with tears, yet there was no trace of yesterday’s terror in her gaze—only a quiet, breathless astonishment. When she looked at William, she did not shrink back. For a long moment, the three of them remained silent, listening to the gulls wheeling overhead and the gentle, rhythmic lap of the tide against the chalk below, as if they were the sole survivors of a vanished century.

Verrall was the first to break the silence, his voice stripped of its old, careless arrogance. "We were mad, Leadford," he said softly, looking up at the clerk with clear, unclouded eyes. "All of us. I look back at yesterday, and it is like remembering the fever-dreams of a sick child. The things we valued—the pride, the hoarding, the cruelty..." He shook his head slowly, unable to finish.

Footsteps crunched on the gravel lane beyond the hedge, and a coastguard in a navy jersey appeared at the gate. He did not carry his rifle, and his face wore the same luminous, quiet wonder that William saw in his rivals. "Have you heard?" the man called out, his voice carrying effortlessly in the still air. "The telegraph is buzzing from London. The fleets in the North Sea have struck their colors—not in surrender, mind you, just... stopped. The Admirals and the German captains are signaling one another like old friends. They say the Prime Minister woke up in Whitehall and wept for the folly of it all."

The coastguard wandered on toward the town, leaving a profound stillness in his wake. William stepped up onto the terrace and looked at Nettie. He knew, with a certainty that warmed him to his core, that he loved her still—far more truly than he had in Staffordshire, because he no longer wished to own her, or to punish her for seeking the light. She stepped forward and took his rough, ink-stained hand in both of hers, then reached out to touch Verrall’s shoulder. Below them, the last wisps of the comet’s green vapor were lifting from the sea, revealing a world waiting, rational and unafraid, to be built anew.
