The coal-smoke of Clayton hung low over the Potteries, a greasy shroud that even the summer wind could not lift. William Leadford sat at his cramped deal table, the cheap kerosene lamp sputtering beside Nettie’s folded note. Three lines of hurried, slanted ink were all she had left him: she had gone south with Edward Verrall, and William was not to follow. His knuckles whitened against the scarred wood. All his life he had breathed the sulphur of the kilns, counted his shillings, and endured the condescension of men like Verrall—men with soft hands, inherited leisure, and effortless charm. Nettie had been the sole clean thing in his narrow existence, and now she too had been bought by the sunlit world beyond the slag heaps.

Leadford stood and crossed to the narrow dormer window. Above the jagged silhouette of the bottle ovens, the night sky was bruised with a strange, unfamiliar luminescence. The comet had grown terrifyingly bright over the past fortnight. No longer a faint, scholarly smear among the constellations, it now hung like a vast, pale-green scimitar, casting a faint, sickly sheen over the slate roofs of Staffordshire. Astronomers in London were writing breathless letters to the newspapers about cyanogen and nitrogenous vapours, yet the men in the taverns barely looked up. Their eyes were fixed on the headlines of impending war.

Leadford turned back to the room. From the bottom of his tin trunk he drew a small, nickel-plated revolver he had bought three weeks prior in a fit of vague, anarchic despair. He slipped it into the pocket of his frayed tweed coat, gathered his remaining sovereigns, and went out into the soot-darkened street to catch the midnight train south.

The third-class carriage rattled southward through the Midlands, smelling of damp wool, stale tobacco, and cheap newsprint. Every seat was taken by men whose faces were flushed with an ugly, restless excitement. Spread across their knees were the late editions, screaming of broken treaties, mobilized corps along the Rhine, and the gathering of the Grand Fleet at Spithead.

"They’ll have to teach the Kaiser a lesson before harvest," a red-faced commercial traveller declared, slapping his rolled-up newspaper against his palm. "Mark my words, by Friday the Channel will be choked with ironclads."

Leadford said nothing. He pressed his forehead against the vibrating glass, watching the dark hedges blur past. Inside his coat pocket, the cold metal of the revolver pressed against his ribs, a silent companion to his own private declaration of war. He felt a bitter kinship with the frantic, bellowing empires of Europe. Let them burn, he thought. Let the cruisers shell the coastal towns and the artillery churn the wheatfields into mud. What was the civilization of Europe to him, save a vast, grinding machine that trapped men like himself in the soot of Staffordshire while men like Verrall plucked the flowers?

As the train cleared the outskirts of London and turned toward the Sussex coast, the dawn did not break in its accustomed grey. Instead, the eastern horizon glowed with a weird, phosphor-green radiance. The comet’s tail now spanned a third of the firmament, a luminous, feathery plume that seemed to brush the very stratosphere. Even the loudest patriots in the carriage fell silent for a moment, blinking uneasily at the emerald twilight, before returning to their maps and their hatreds.

Shaphambury lay white and immaculate beside the English Channel, a resort of manicured lawns, striped awnings, and chalk cliffs that seemed to belong to another planet than the black valleys of the north. Leadford walked along the esplanade with his collar turned up, a gaunt, dust-streaked spectre among ladies in muslin and gentlemen in white flannels. His boots were caked with Midland mud; his eyes were bloodshot from two sleepless nights.

Yet even here, the fever of the approaching war had infected the air. Along the promenade, clusters of holidaymakers stood with opera glasses raised toward the horizon, where three grey-hulled cruisers steamed slowly eastward, trailing smudges of black coal smoke against the pale sea. Rumours darted through the crowd like swallows—that Portsmouth had been bombarded, that the French coast was ablaze, that the Cabinet had fallen.

Leadford ignored them all. He checked the boarding houses near the cliffs, asking after a tall young gentleman and a dark-haired girl from the north, until a porter pointed toward the quiet, tamarisk-lined path that wound above the cove.

He found them within twenty minutes. They were seated on a wooden bench overlooking the shingle, Nettie in a pale blue frock he had never seen before, leaning her head lightly against Verrall’s shoulder. They were speaking in low, anxious tones, their eyes fixed not on each other, but on the terrifying sky. For the sun, though it was past noon, hung like a tarnished copper coin behind a thickening veil of emerald mist. The comet was no longer a distant star; its gaseous envelope was brushing the upper air, turning the Channel water the colour of tarnished brass.

Leadford’s hand closed around the grip of the revolver. He stepped onto the gravel path, the crunch of his heavy boots cutting through the murmur of the tide below. Nettie turned first. The colour drained from her cheeks as she recognized him, and she rose with a sharp, breathless cry.

"William!"

Verrall sprang to his feet, stepping instinctively between them, though his handsome face was pale and drawn. "See here, Leadford," he said, his voice tight. "There’s no sense in making a scene. She chose to come with me."

"She didn't choose," Leadford snarled, pulling the weapon from his pocket. The barrel wavered in his trembling fist, pointed squarely at Verrall’s chest. "You bought her, just like your kind buys everything else. You think because you have clean nails and a banker’s draft, you can—"

He never finished the sentence. Out over the Channel, a dull, concussive boom rolled across the water—the distant, muffled thunder of a naval gun. Another followed, and then a third, as if the madness of Europe had finally snapped its tether.

Simultaneously, the wind died completely. A strange, heavy warmth settled over the cliffside, and from the seaward horizon rolled a dense, luminous wall of pale-green vapour. It swept over the cruisers, swallowed the white beach, and enveloped the three of them in an instant. It did not choke Leadford; instead, it smelled faintly, exquisitely, of crushed mint and summer rain after lightning. A profound, overwhelming lethargy seized his limbs. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and thudded onto the turf. Verrall swayed, clutching at the bench, and Nettie sank softly to her knees as the green world dissolved into a velvet, dreamless dark.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was shining with a clarity he had never seen in his life.

He sat up slowly on the short, salt-bitten grass. The green mist had vanished entirely, leaving the sky an intense, crystalline azure, purged of every speck of haze or industrial smoke. A lark was singing somewhere above the chalk downs, each note falling like a drop of pure water.

Leadford looked down at his hands. Beside his knee lay the nickel-plated revolver, glinting in the morning light. He stared at it with a sudden, bewildered revulsion, as though he had woken from a long, delirious fever to find himself clutching a venomous insect. Where was the burning, suffocating knot of hatred that had driven him from Staffordshire? Where was the humiliation, the poisoned pride, the desperate craving to possess? His mind felt extraordinarily light, washed clean of every petty grievance, as if an unseen hand had swept the cobwebs from a darkened room.

A few paces away, Verrall stirred and pushed himself up onto one elbow, blinking in the brilliant light. He looked at Leadford, and for the first time, there was neither arrogance nor fear in his eyes—only a quiet, questioning wonder. Beside him, Nettie sat up, brushing a strand of dark hair from her forehead. She saw the revolver on the grass, then looked up at William’s face, and the terror that had haunted her features the day before melted into astonishment.

"William," she whispered, not in fear, but in recognition. "What has happened to us?"

Leadford picked up the revolver, walked to the edge of the low cliff, and tossed it into the deep, clear water below, where it sank without a ripple of anger. When he turned back, Verrall and Nettie were standing together, looking out toward the town and the sea.

A profound stillness lay over Shaphambury. Below them, people were emerging from the boarding houses and cottages onto the esplanade, not in panic, nor shouting the slogans of yesterday, but moving with a quiet, bewildered dignity. Along the horizon, the three great grey cruisers sat motionless upon the glassy Channel. No black smoke poured from their funnels; their signal flags hung limp in the gentle breeze. Whatever strange, sweet-smelling gas the comet had poured into the Earth’s atmosphere, it had touched every human brain alike, stripping away the ancient, animal fog of suspicion and territorial rage.

Leadford walked back to the bench and sat down beside them. He looked at Nettie—at the curve of her cheek, the quiet intelligence in her eyes—and realized he loved her still, perhaps more truly than before, because he no longer wished to cage her. And looking at Verrall, he saw not a class enemy, nor a rival, but simply another man, newly awakened and blinking at the dawn of a vast, unfamiliar day.

"The war is over before it began," Verrall murmured, gazing out at the silent warships.

"More than the war," Leadford said softly, feeling the immense, rational future opening before them like the sunlit sea. "Everything we thought was solid has melted away. Now, at last, we have to learn how to live."
