The Potteries lay beneath a perpetual bruise of coal-smoke, yet even through that grimy canopy the green star had begun to assert itself. William Leadford watched it from the cinder-path behind his mother’s cottage, his hands thrust deep into the pockets of his threadbare coat. In the right pocket, his fingers closed around the cold, fluted grip of a cheap Belgian revolver. Two days ago, Nettie had vanished from Swathinglea with Edward Verrall—Verrall of the great house on the hill, Verrall with his motor-car, his soft hands, and his effortless insolence. Leadford’s lungs burned with the sulphur of the blast furnaces, and his mind burned with a sharper, meaner poison. All his life he had been cramped into small rooms, fed on stale bread and bitter grievances, taught his place by men like Verrall. Nettie had been the one bright, untarnished thing in his narrow world, and now she too had been bought away. Above the jagged silhouettes of the bottle-kilns, the comet hung like a pale emerald wound in the sky, its faint tail stretching toward the zenith. The newspapers called it a marvel of astronomy, a harmless accumulation of incandescent gas sweeping through the inner solar system. Leadford cared nothing for its chemistry. To him, it was merely a cold witness to his humiliation, shining indifferently upon a world that belonged entirely to the rich. He drew a ragged breath, spat into the black marl at his feet, and turned toward the railway station. He knew where Verrall’s people kept their seaside bungalow; he had heard Nettie speak of Shaphambury once, with a longing he had been too poor to satisfy.

The southward train rattled through a countryside feverish with rumours of war. In the third-class carriage, three clerks and a commercial traveller argued loudly over the morning’s special editions, slapping the folded newsprint with ink-stained fingers. An ultimatum had been delivered; the German fleet was reportedly steaming out of Kiel; British dreadnoughts were already darkening the North Sea with their coal-smoke. The traveller spoke with a kind of breathless, vicarious bloodlust, demanding that the government strike first and strike hard. Leadford sat in the corner, his cap pulled low, watching the green-tinged dusk gather over the Midlands pastures. It struck him as a grotesque farce that empires should hurl millions of men into slaughter over strips of frontier and diplomatic pride, while he sat here nursing a private murder in his coat pocket. Yet he felt no pity for the nations, any more than he felt pity for himself. The whole fabric of human society seemed rotten—a vast, noisy machine grinding the poor into fuel for the furnaces of the wealthy. At every station, crowds lingered on the platforms, staring upward before hurrying back to the telegraph boards. The comet had grown visibly larger since the previous night, its core a dazzling, unearthly beryl that cast faint, double shadows beneath the gas-lamps. When Leadford stepped out onto the platform at Shaphambury, the salt wind carried a strange, metallic tang, as though the approaching celestial body were already grazing the outer fringes of the atmosphere. He paid no heed to the posters screaming of mobilization. Pulling his collar high, he set off along the dark cliff road.

He found the bungalow perched above a lonely sweep of shingle, its whitewashed walls glowing faintly in the eerie emerald twilight. The comet now dominated half the eastern sky, a colossal plume of luminous mist that turned the English Channel into a sheet of hammered pewter. Leadford crouched behind a wind-bent tamarisk hedge, the revolver drawn and heavy in his sweating palm. Through the uncurtained French windows, he saw them. Verrall stood by the hearth, handsome and careless in his evening jacket, pouring wine into two glasses. Nettie sat near the table, her dark hair catching the strange green light from the sky, her face turned away from the glass Verrall offered her. Even from fifty paces, Leadford could see the tension in her shoulders—not the easy triumph he had imagined, but a troubled, restless melancholy. Rage flared in him nonetheless, hot and blinding. Who was Verrall to bring her here and make her sad? Leadford stepped out from the hedge, his boots crunching loudly on the gravel path. At the sound, Nettie turned toward the window, and Verrall’s head snapped up. Leadford raised the revolver, leveling the barrel at the glass pane, his thumb drawing back the hammer with a sharp metallic click. He meant to shatter the glass, to end Verrall’s smug existence and his own wretchedness in two swift reports. Yet as his finger tightened on the trigger, a sudden, inexplicable lethargy washed over his limbs. The air around him seemed to thicken, growing heavy and strangely sweet, like the scent of crushed almond blossoms drifting in from the sea.

A vast silence descended upon the cliffs. The restless mutter of the Channel surf seemed to dull, muffled by an invisible blanket of vapour settling down from the heavens. Leadford tried to shout, to force his finger to pull the trigger, but his arm felt as though it were weighted with lead. Looking up, he saw that the comet’s luminous tail had engulfed the zenith, pouring a soft, phosphorescent green haze directly into the upper atmosphere. The mist was descending visibly now, curling over the cliff-edge like slow, luminous smoke, swallowing the tamarisk hedge and the whitewashed walls of the bungalow. Inside the room, Verrall had dropped his wineglass; it shattered silently—or so it seemed to Leadford’s failing ears—against the floorboards. Nettie had risen from her chair, her hands pressed to her throat, before sinking gently to her knees upon the rug. Leadford staggered forward two paces, his boots scraping the gravel, and then his knees buckled beneath him. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and clattered into a tuft of sea-thrift. There was no pain in this strange suffocation, only an overwhelming, oceanic drowsiness that dissolved his anger as water dissolves salt. As the green vapour closed over his face, cool and fragrant, his last conscious thought was not of Nettie, nor of Verrall, nor even of his own bitter poverty. He thought dimly of the millions of men in the trenches and the warships across Europe, wondering in a vast, detached stupor whether they too were falling asleep beneath this silent, emerald rain. Then the darkness, soft and profound, claimed him altogether.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the world had been washed clean. Above him stretched a sky of unimaginable depth and clarity, a radiant morning blue untouched by the slightest smudge of smoke or mist. For several minutes he lay motionless on the damp grass, breathing deeply. The air tasted crisp, vital, and extraordinarily pure, as though he had spent his entire life breathing the stale exhaust of a cellar and had only now stepped out into the open day. He sat up slowly, rubbing his forehead. At first he could not recall why he was lying in a stranger’s garden by the sea; then the memories of the previous night returned—the train, the hatred, the long walk in the dark, the pistol. He looked down and saw the revolver lying half-buried in the grass beside his boot. Picking it up, he turned it over in his hands, staring at the oiled steel with a sensation of profound, wondering pity. How small, how pitifully cramped his mind had been! It felt as though a fever had raged in his blood for twenty-five years and had broken in the night, leaving him weak, perhaps, but entirely sane. He laid the weapon carefully upon a flat stone, emptied the chambers, and tossed the brass cartridges far out over the cliff-edge into the sparkling sea. Turning toward the bungalow, he saw that the French windows stood open to the morning breeze. Edward Verrall was sitting on the low stone step, his elbows on his knees, staring out across the sunlit water with an expression of quiet, humbled astonishment.

Leadford walked slowly up the gravel path. Verrall heard his footsteps and looked up, yet there was no start of alarm in his eyes, no trace of the old, defensive arrogance. Between them, the invisible walls of class and rivalry seemed to have evaporated with the night’s strange mist. "You came here to shoot me," Verrall said, his voice remarkably calm, stripped of its usual affected drawl. "I did," Leadford replied, sitting down on the grass a few paces away. "I hated you. I hated the world that made you rich and me desperate." Verrall nodded slowly, looking down at his own pale hands. "I was a fool, Leadford. We were all living in a kind of madness—grasping, hoarding, hurting one another in the dark." From inside the bungalow came the sound of light footsteps, and Nettie appeared in the doorway. She looked pale in the bright morning sun, yet her eyes were clearer and steadier than Leadford had ever seen them. She looked from Verrall to Leadford, and no cry of fear escaped her lips. Instead, she stepped out onto the grass and knelt beside them both, her gaze resting gently on Leadford’s weathered face. "William," she whispered, "what has happened to us?" Before he could answer, the distant sound of church bells drifted up from the town of Shaphambury below—not the frantic, clanging alarm of war, but a slow, resonant peal of wonder. Down on the coast road, a group of soldiers in khaki were walking without their rifles, laughing softly as they pointed toward the spotless sky. Leadford looked out over the shining Channel, knowing with an unshakeable certainty that the guns across Europe had fallen silent, and that humanity, waking from its long, feverish nightmare, had finally begun to live.
