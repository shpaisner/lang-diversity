The soot of Staffordshire seemed to have settled not merely upon the crooked brick terraces and the stunted hawthorns, but into the very marrow of William Leadford’s bones. For three days after Nettie’s disappearance, he had wandered between the roaring blast furnaces of Swadlincote and the cold, damp silence of his mother’s parlor, nursing a misery that had hardened into a single, jagged purpose. Nettie—who had laughed with him among the slag heaps and promised him a life beyond the grime—had fled south with young Verrall, whose father owned half the pits and whose pockets jingled with effortless gold. 

In the pawnshop behind the market square, Leadford had traded his silver watch and his Sunday coat for a battered six-chambered revolver and a handful of cartridges. The iron felt heavy and strangely comforting against his ribs as he walked out into the twilight. Above the jagged silhouettes of the pit-head wheels and the belching chimneys, the sky was no longer merely dark. The comet hung there, a vast, luminous smear of pale emerald, its tail sweeping halfway across the zenith like the plume of an approaching archangel. Men in the public houses spoke of nothing else—save, perhaps, the rumors of war. Astronomers in London had written that the Earth might pass through the outer fringes of that celestial vapor before the week was out, yet the people of the Five Towns stared up at it with dull, preoccupied eyes, too exhausted by their wages and their grievances to fear the stars. Leadford did not fear it either. He looked at the green portent, felt the cold pistol against his heart, and turned his face toward the railway station.

The southbound train rattled through the Midlands like a frantic, iron beetle, carrying Leadford away from the smoke and into a landscape of pale, moonlit pastures. Yet even here, in the cramped third-class carriage, there was no peace. Two commercial travelers in loud tweed coats sat opposite him, their knees crowded together, slapping folded newspapers as they argued over the German ultimatum. 

"Mobilization by Friday, mark my words," the stout one declared, tapping a thick finger against the bold black headlines. "The Channel fleet is already steaming out of Portsmouth. They’ve got the guns, and we’ve got the ships, and it’s high time we settled who’s master of the seas."

"And the cost?" asked a gaunt clerk in the corner, nervously adjusting his spectacles. "The papers say the banks are already shivering."

Leadford pressed his forehead against the vibrating windowpane, shutting out their voices. To him, the impending slaughter of nations was merely a larger, noisier version of the cruelty he knew in his own life—the endless, blind jostling of the strong against the weak, the proud against the desperate. Outside, the quiet English fields lay bathed in an eerie, greenish twilight. The comet had grown visibly larger since the previous evening; its nucleus burned with a steady, unblinking brilliance that outshone Jupiter and cast faint, ghostly shadows from the telegraph poles flashing past the tracks. The travelers occasionally glanced out at it, muttered something about "queer weather," and returned immediately to their maps of troop movements and naval blockades. They were sleepwalkers, Leadford thought bitterly, marching toward a precipice while the very heavens shifted above their heads. His own hand crept into his coat pocket, his fingers closing around the knurled grip of the revolver.

He reached the seaside town of Shapham just as the dusk deepened into night. Here, the air smelled of salt and wet shingle rather than coal smoke, and the grand hotels along the promenade blazed with electric light, as if defying the strange green radiance that spilled down from the northern sky. Leadford’s boots were dusty, his collar frayed, and the uniformed porters outside the Metropole eyed him with cold suspicion as he lingered in the shadows of the tamarisk bushes. 

He did not have to wait long. Shortly after nine, the glass doors of the hotel swung open, and they emerged onto the esplanade. Nettie was wearing a cloak of pale grey silk that Leadford had never seen before, her dark hair pinned up beneath a feathered hat. Beside her walked Verrall, tall and effortless in his evening dress, laughing down at her with the careless assurance of a man who had never once wondered how the next week’s rent would be paid. A wave of scalding humiliation broke over Leadford, so fierce it nearly blinded him. 

He followed them at a distance as they left the bright lamps of the promenade and turned onto the narrow, chalky path that climbed toward the eastern cliffs. The sea below hissed softly against the rocks, its dark surface gleaming with an oily, emerald sheen. Soon, the music of the hotel band faded behind them, replaced by the lonely crying of gulls. Nettie paused near the summit of the headland, leaning against a wooden railing to look out at the glowing sky. Verrall stepped close, placing a possessive hand upon her shoulder. Leadford’s breath came in ragged, shallow gasps. He stepped out from behind a clump of gorse, drawing the revolver from his coat.

"Stay where you are, Verrall," Leadford said, his voice trembling with a terrible, pent-up intensity. 

Nettie spun around with a sharp cry, her hand flying to her throat. "Willie! Oh, God—Willie, no!"

Verrall stiffened, thrusting Nettie instinctively behind him. In the ghostly light of the comet, his handsome face looked pale and suddenly uncertain, stripped of its drawing-room polish. "What is the meaning of this?" he demanded, though his voice cracked slightly. "Leadford, you’re mad. Put that thing down before someone gets hurt."

"Someone is going to get hurt," Leadford whispered, raising the pistol until the barrel pointed squarely at Verrall’s chest. "You took everything I had. You come down to Staffordshire like a lord among beggars, and you buy what you please, and you think there’s no reckoning. Well, here is the reckoning."

"Willie, please!" Nettie sobbed, trying to push past Verrall’s arm. "It wasn’t his fault—I wanted to go! I couldn't bear the smoke, the poverty—"

"Be quiet!" Leadford shouted, his finger tightening on the cold steel of the trigger. He saw nothing now but the white blur of Verrall’s shirtfront and the mocking shadow of the life he had been denied. 

Then, the sky tore open. 

There was no sound of thunder, only a sudden, blinding crescendo of green light that swallowed the cliffs, the sea, and the distant town in a single, soundless flash. A moment later, a rushing wind struck them—warm, violent, and carrying an extraordinary, sweetish fragrance, like crushed mint and ozone. Leadford tried to cry out, tried to pull the trigger, but his lungs seized as the luminous vapor enveloped him. His knees buckled. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers, and as he fell into the damp grass, he saw Nettie and Verrall collapsing beside him, their faces upturned to a firmament that seemed to be dissolving into emerald fire.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the green fire was gone. Above him stretched a sky of such pristine, delicate blue that it made his chest ache simply to behold it. A lark was singing somewhere high above the headland, each note falling through the morning air like a drop of clear water. 

He sat up slowly, bracing himself for the familiar, grinding ache behind his eyes—the dull weight of exhaustion and resentment that had been his constant companion for twenty-five years. Yet there was nothing. His mind felt astonishingly light, as though a thick, stifling fog had been swept clean out of the chambers of his brain. He looked down at his own rough, coal-scarred hands resting on the dew-soaked clover, and for the first time in his life, he felt no shame in them. 

Beside him lay the revolver, its cheap nickel plating dulled by the morning mist. Leadford stared at it with mild, detached curiosity, as an adult might stare at a broken wooden sword he had once brandished in a childhood tantrum. How small, how absurdly frantic that creature had been—the William Leadford of yesterday, who had believed that spilling another man’s blood could heal the ugliness of the world. 

A few paces away, Nettie stirred, letting out a long, soft sigh. Beside her, Verrall pushed himself up onto one elbow, blinking in the golden sunlight. They did not scramble up in terror, nor did they reach for one another in panic. They simply sat upon the grass, looking out over the glittering sea with expressions of quiet, childlike wonder, as if they, too, had just woken from a long and feverish dream.

For a long minute, none of them spoke. The silence between them was no longer heavy with accusation or dread; it was merely peaceful. Verrall looked across at Leadford, and there was no trace of the old, defensive arrogance in his eyes—only a profound, unguarded recognition. 

"Leadford," Verrall said softly, his voice steady and strangely gentle. "Are you hurt?"

"No," Leadford replied, surprised by the warmth in his own tone. "No, I am quite well. And you?"

"I feel... as though I have slept for a hundred years," Verrall murmured, passing a hand over his forehead. He glanced at the pistol lying in the grass, then looked back at Leadford without a flicker of fear or anger. "What happened to us last night?"

"The comet," Nettie whispered. She had drawn her knees up beneath her grey silk cloak, looking out toward the horizon. "It touched the Earth. I remember the light... and the wind." She turned her eyes to Leadford, and he saw her clearly—not as a prize he had lost, nor as a traitor who had wounded his pride, but simply as a young woman who had been frightened of a dark and soot-stained life, seeking light wherever she could find it. A deep, quiet compassion welled up inside him, washing away the last remnant of his bitterness.

Below them, the town of Shapham lay bathed in the morning sun, strangely silent. No factory whistles blew; no newsboys shouted in the streets about fleets and ultimata. It seemed to Leadford that the great, restless madness of Europe had broken in the night, just as his own had broken upon the cliffs. He reached out, picked up the revolver, and with an easy, unhurried motion, tossed it far out over the chalk edge. They heard no splash—only the gulls wheeling below. Then, together, the three of them rose and walked down the path toward the waking town.
