The smoke of Swetford hung low over the cinder-heaps, a greasy, sulphurous canopy that even the April gale could scarcely tear apart. William Leadford stood by the cracked window of his mother’s parlour, his knuckles white against the sill, staring out at the jagged silhouettes of the pot-banks and the distant, pulsing glare of the blast furnaces. In his coat pocket lay a crumpled sheet of cheap notepaper. He did not need to read it again; every rounded, hesitant word of Nettie’s farewell was etched into his brain like acid upon copper. She had gone with Edward Verrall—Verrall with his crisp tweed suits, his effortless laughter, and his father’s money—leaving Leadford to the soot, the ledger-books of the coal-factor’s office, and a suffocating, parochial despair.

On the horsehair sofa lay the evening paper, its bold black type screaming of ultimatums in the North Sea, of German cruisers steaming out of Kiel, and of the British Home Fleet clearing its decks for action. The whole continent was sliding toward an abyss of iron and fire, yet Leadford felt no dread of the coming slaughter. Rather, a dark, venomous satisfaction stirred in him; if the world was to burn, let it burn cleanly, and take Verrall’s comfortable class along with it. Above the black rim of the slag-mounds, where the smoke thinned into the cold twilight, a pale emerald smear hung motionless among the stars. For three weeks the astronomers had charted its approach, and tonight the comet’s tail seemed to stretch a full hand’s breadth across the eastern sky, shedding a faint, unnatural luminescence upon the slate roofs of Staffordshire.

The next morning Leadford did not go to the coal-factor’s office. Instead, he walked through the drizzle to a narrow street behind the market square, where a pawnbroker’s three brass balls gleamed dully through the grime. Within ten minutes he emerged with a heavy, nickelled Belgian revolver and a box of cartridges buttoned tightly inside his waistcoat. The cold weight of the iron against his ribs seemed to steady the fever in his blood, giving his aimless misery a sharp, terrible direction. By noon he had drawn his meager savings from the post office and boarded the southbound express, wedged into a crowded third-class carriage that smelled of wet wool and cheap tobacco.

Around him, men in flat caps argued hoarsely over the mobilisation orders, their faces flushed with a strange, brittle excitement. They spoke of torpedoes, of blockade, of teaching the Kaiser a lesson, as though war were a grand football match played on a larger pitch. Leadford said nothing. He pressed his forehead against the rain-streaked glass, watching the blackened Midlands give way to the wet green pastures of the south. As dusk fell, the train hurtled past quiet villages where knots of people stood in the lanes, their faces tilted upward. Through the rattling window, the comet was now a formidable apparition, a vast, misty plume of verdigris light that dwarfed the constellations. It bathed the passing hedgerows in a ghostly, underwater pallor, and Leadford, gripping the revolver in his pocket, felt himself to be an instrument of that same cold, pitiless sky.

He reached the coastal town of Shaphambury shortly before midnight, stepping out into a salt-laden wind that tasted sharp and clean after the coal-smoke of the Potteries. Even at that late hour, the seaside resort was humming with an anxious, sleepless energy. Along the esplanade, squads of territorial soldiers marched in heavy boots toward the railway sidings, their bayonets catching the blue-white glare of the arc lamps. Out in the Channel, the long, nervous fingers of naval searchlights swept the horizon, probing the darkness for the silhouettes of enemy destroyers. Telegraph boys darted past the shuttered boarding houses, and from the windows of the Grand Hotel came the clatter of plates and the high, brittle laughter of officers drinking before embarkation.

Leadford ignored the commotion. Having gleaned Verrall’s destination from a careless boast made to a mutual acquaintance in Swetford, he turned away from the promenade and climbed the winding chalk path toward the eastern cliffs. There, perched above the rhythmic thunder of the surf, stood a cluster of secluded bungalows rented by summer visitors. The comet now dominated half the zenith, its nucleus a blinding emerald star surrounded by a luminous, fanning haze that cast crisp double shadows across the turf. Pausing beside a wind-bent tamarisk hedge, Leadford saw a light burning behind the drawn curtains of the furthest cottage. Two silhouettes moved across the linen blind—one broad and gesturing, the other slight and unmistakable. His breath came in short, ragged gasps. He drew the revolver from his coat, thumbed back the hammer with a metallic click, and crept toward the low stone wall of the garden.

The glass doors of the verandah stood slightly ajar to the mild night air, and Leadford slipped through the gap in the hedge until he stood within twenty paces of the house. Inside, a lamp burned on a wicker table beside a scatter of newspapers. Verrall was pacing the floorboards, his handsome face drawn and irritable as he gestured at the headlines, while Nettie sat huddled in a basket chair, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. She looked smaller than Leadford remembered, her eyes wide with a quiet, bewildered fear—not of him, he realized with a sudden jolt of rage, but of the looming war that threatened to sweep Verrall away to the fleet.

Leadford stepped onto the gravel path, raising the revolver until the foresights aligned with the centre of Verrall’s chest. His finger tightened on the trigger. At that exact instant, the sea wind dropped dead. The roar of the breakers below the cliff seemed to mute, as though a heavy curtain had been drawn across the world. Looking up involuntarily, Leadford saw the sky above the Channel ignite with a silent, cascading brilliance. A vast veil of pale green vapour—thin as gossamer yet strangely heavy—was descending from the comet’s tail, wrapping the cliffs, the sea, and the distant searchlights in an iridescent fog. A faint, sweet scent, like crushed laurel leaves and summer ozone, filled his nostrils. His knees buckled beneath him; the revolver slipped from his numb fingers into the wet grass, and as the luminous mist closed over his eyes, the venomous knot inside his chest seemed to unravel into a deep, dreamless sleep.

When William Leadford opened his eyes, the sun was already above the horizon, pouring a flood of clear, crystalline light across the chalk downs. He lay upon his side in the dew-soaked grass, his coat damp, his limbs stiff with the chill of the morning. Yet as he pushed himself up onto one elbow, he felt no ache in his head, no lingering trace of the dark fever that had driven him south. Instead, a profound, almost startling lucidity possessed his mind. The air he drew into his lungs was intoxicatingly pure, stripped of every trace of smoke, salt-rot, and staleness, as though the earth itself had been rinsed by an invisible tide.

Beside his knee lay the nickelled Belgian revolver, its barrel beaded with morning dew. Leadford stared at the weapon for a long moment, and a wave of quiet astonishment washed over him. It seemed to him now not an instrument of justice, nor even of sin, but a pathetic, clumsy toy forged by a species of frightened children. He picked it up, ejected the brass cartridges into his palm, and tossed both gun and bullets over the low stone wall into the thickets of gorse below. Looking down toward Shaphambury, he saw that the town lay in an extraordinary hush. No trains whistled in the sidings; no bugles sounded from the barracks. Smoke no longer belched from the coastal batteries, and across the rooftops, tiny figures stood motionless in their gardens and on the esplanade, gazing upward or speaking to one another in quiet, unhurried postures. The green fog had vanished completely, leaving behind a world that looked freshly painted, sharp-edged, and impossibly vast.

A faint creak of floorboards made him turn. The glass doors of the verandah had swung wide, and Nettie and Verrall stood together on the threshold, blinking in the bright morning sun. For a second, Leadford’s old habit of shame urged him to crouch behind the hedge, to hide his frayed cuffs and soot-stained boots from their eyes. Then that impulse, too, dissolved like mist before the sun. He walked calmly up the gravel path toward them, empty-handed and unafraid.

Verrall saw him first. There was no flash of arrogance on his face, no defensive stiffening of his shoulders—only a deep, wondering recognition, as if they were two strangers waking in the same lifeboat after a shipwreck. Nettie took a step forward, her eyes clear of tears, and looked at Leadford with a tenderness stripped of all guilt or pity. They did not speak of the letter, nor of the nights of jealousy, for those things belonged to a cramped, suffocating dream from which all three had just emerged. Instead, Verrall lifted a hand and pointed silently toward the sea. Out beyond the breakwater, two grey ironclad cruisers sat motionless upon the glassy swell of the Channel, their massive gun turrets pointing harmlessly at the sky, their signal flags hanging limp in the calm air. No smoke rose from their funnels; no shells shrieked across the water. As Leadford stood beside the woman he had once thought to kill and the man he had sworn to destroy, he understood that the old world of coal-smoke, narrow hatreds, and drifting fleets had ended in the night, and that humanity was only now beginning to draw its first true breath.
