William Leadford sat in the narrow, soot-stained parlor of his mother’s cottage in Clayton, staring at the cheap Belgian revolver resting on the oilcloth table. Outside, the Potteries exhaled their perpetual breath of coal smoke and kiln-fume, choking the low Staffordshire sky. Yet even through that greasy pall, a strange, pale-green luminescence bled downward. The comet had grown. Three weeks ago it had been a mere smudge in the telescope of the Greenwich astronomers; now it hung above the slag heaps like a silent, emerald scimitar.

Leadford barely cared for the sky. His universe had collapsed into a single, burning grievance: Nettie. She had gone with Edward Verrall—Verrall with his crisp flannel suits, his motorcar, his effortless assumption that the world and its women belonged to his class. Leadford slipped the heavy iron pistol into his greatcoat pocket. His fingers trembled, not with fear, but with the sour, intoxicating heat of a wronged man’s pride.

At the corner of the lane, a newsboy’s hoarse cry cut through the clatter of clogs on cobblestones. "Ultimatum! German fleet in the North Sea! War expected!" Men spilled from the public houses, their faces flushed with ale and sudden, aimless fury, eager to cheer for a slaughter they did not understand. Leadford pushed past them toward the railway station. The whole continent was gearing up for murder, it seemed, and he was merely ahead of the fashion. He bought a third-class ticket to Shaphambury on the coast, where the clerk at the telegraph office had whispered Nettie’s name had been sent.

The night train rattled southward, its gas-lit compartments thick with tobacco smoke and the anxious chatter of strangers. Across from Leadford, two commercial travelers in bowler hats leaned over a spread newspaper, their fingers jabbing at maps of the Baltic and the Dogger Bank. They spoke of dreadnoughts, of torpedoes, of the inevitable clash of empires that would decide the fate of Europe before Monday.

"They say the Admiralty has sealed the Channel," one muttered, glancing nervously at the black windowpane. "And look at that thing in the heavens. My wife swears it’s a sign. A judgment."

The other laughed, a brittle, uneasy sound. "The papers say it’s nothing but cyanogen gas and cosmic dust. The Earth will brush through the tail tonight, and we shan't even feel a draught."

Leadford pressed his forehead against the cold glass, shutting out their voices. Above the rushing hedgerows and sleeping Midlands towns, the comet blazed with an intensity that made the stars look faint and sickly. Its long, feathery tail stretched halfway across the zenith, shimmering with a cold, unearthly verdigris. In the darkness of his coat pocket, his hand closed around the grooved grip of the revolver. Let Europe burn, he thought; let the ironclads sink each other into the mud. His war was smaller, sharper, and infinitely more real. Every clack of the iron wheels over the sleepers seemed to hammer out a single, rhythmic accusation: Verrall. Nettie. Verrall. Nettie. By the time the train hissed to a halt at Shaphambury, his mind was a tight, dark knot of resolution.

Shaphambury smelled of salt, damp shingle, and expensive leisure—a world entirely divorced from the grime of Staffordshire. Along the esplanade, the great white-fronted hotels stood like arrogant cliffs above the tide, their windows glowing with warm, yellow electricity. Leadford walked the gravel paths with his collar turned up, a gaunt, shabby shadow drifting among the holidaymakers who lingered outdoors, necks craned toward the sky.

Out over the dark expanse of the English Channel, searchlights from the naval forts swept the horizon in long, nervous arcs, probing the mist for enemy cruisers. Yet no one seemed able to look away from the comet for long. Its green radiance bathed the whitewashed facades and the wet sand in a ghostly, theatrical light, stripping the warmth from human faces.

Leadford found them near the western cliffs, where a cluster of tamarisk bushes shielded a private bungalow belonging to Verrall’s aunt. Through the uncurtained French windows, he saw Nettie. She was laughing, her dark hair catching the lamplight as she poured tea, looking so effortlessly at ease in that plush, alien room that a fresh wave of bitterness scalded Leadford’s throat. Verrall stood by the mantelpiece, gesturing languidly with a cigarette.

Leadford crouched in the damp shrubbery, waiting. The wind off the sea had died away completely, replaced by a curious, heavy stillness. Above the cliffs, the green tail of the comet seemed to have expanded until it occupied half the vault of heaven, pulsing faintly like a living aurora. He checked the cylinder of his revolver by touch, his heart hammering against his ribs as he waited for the lights in the bungalow to go out, or for the door to open.

Before dawn, the bungalow door finally clicked open. Verrall and Nettie stepped onto the terrace, wrapped in heavy coats, drawn outside by the strange transformation of the night. The sky was no longer black; it was a vast, swirling canopy of luminous green, as though the earth had plunged into the depths of an emerald sea. No birds sang. Even the distant, rhythmic wash of the surf seemed muffled, heavy as oil.

Leadford stepped out from the tamarisks, his boots crunching loudly on the gravel. Nettie gasped, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall stiffened, stepping instinctively in front of her, his eyes widening as he recognized the haggard figure from the Potteries.

"Leadford?" Verrall’s voice cracked slightly. "What madness brought you here?"

Leadford did not answer with words. He raised the revolver, leveling the dark muzzle at Verrall’s chest. His arm felt rigid as cast iron, fueled by months of humiliation and sleepless nights. "You stole her," Leadford whispered, though his voice sounded thin and absurd beneath that immense, glowing sky. "You took what wasn't yours."

Just as his finger tightened on the trigger, a faint, sweet odor drifted across the terrace—like crushed almonds and summer rain. A pale green mist, tangible and cool, began to settle from the zenith, curling around their ankles and spilling over the cliff edge. Leadford blinked. His eyelids grew suddenly, irresistibly heavy. The fierce, knotted rage in his chest seemed to loosen, unspooling like a snapped spring. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers and struck the gravel with a dull thud. Across from him, Nettie swayed and sank gently to her knees, while Verrall slumped against the stone balustrade. Together, beneath the silent, descending veil of cosmic vapor, the three of them surrendered to a deep, dreamless sleep.

Leadford woke to a silence so profound it felt like the first morning of the world. He lay upon the gravel, yet his limbs were neither stiff nor cold. When he drew a breath, the air tasted extraordinarily crisp, stripped of the sour staleness of coal smoke and human anxiety. Above him, the green mist had vanished entirely, and the comet was now only a faint, receding smear against a sky of dazzling, immaculate blue.

He sat up, his gaze falling immediately upon the revolver resting beside his knee. For a long moment, he stared at the ugly lump of iron with utter bewilderment, as though it were a relic dug from the grave of a forgotten savage. He remembered his hatred—the burning, claustrophobic obsession that had driven him across England—and it felt remote, pitiful, like the tantrum of a feverish child. His mind was astonishingly clear, washed clean of vanity and malice.

A few paces away, Nettie stirred, pushing back her dark hair as she sat up. Verrall, too, was waking, blinking in the bright morning sunlight. When their eyes met Leadford’s, there was no shriek of terror, no bluster of aristocratic pride. Verrall looked at the pistol, then at Leadford’s pale, earnest face, and offered a slow, quiet nod of understanding.

"You came to kill me," Verrall said softly, without accusation.

"I did," Leadford replied, picking up the revolver and tossing it casually over the cliff edge into the deep water below. "I was blind. We were all blind."

Nettie rose and walked over to them, looking from one man to the other with tears of quiet wonder in her eyes, seeing them both, perhaps for the first time, not as rivals or masters, but simply as men.

They walked down the winding cliff path toward Shaphambury together, three figures moving in an unhurried, companionable silence. The town below was stirring. Shutters were being flung wide, and townsfolk stood in their doorways and along the esplanade, blinking up at the luminous sky with expressions of dazed, gentle astonishment. There was no shouting, no panic. A milkman had abandoned his cart to sit on the sea wall, conversing quietly with a policeman and a ragged dockworker as though they had been lifelong friends.

Near the post office, a telegraph boy stood holding a sheaf of yellow slips, his bicycle leaning against a lamppost. A small crowd gathered around him, not jostling or clamoring, but waiting patiently as the local magistrate read the latest dispatches aloud.

"From the Admiralty," the magistrate’s voice carried clearly through the still air. "The fleets met off the Dogger Bank at dawn. The green vapor passed over both squadrons. When the crews awoke, not a single gun was fired. The admirals have signaled a truce, and the stokers are coming up on deck to share rations."

A murmur of quiet relief rippled through the crowd, followed by a profound, thoughtful stillness. The old world—with its smoky kilns, its jealous borders, its bitter divisions of master and man—had ended in the night, dissolved by a breath of alien gas.

Leadford turned to Nettie and Verrall. He knew now that Nettie would never belong to him in the old, grasping way he had once demanded; nor, he realized, did she belong to Verrall. They were free creatures in a newly minted earth. Looking at Nettie’s serene face, Leadford felt a deep, steady warmth—not the scorching fire of possession, but a quiet love that wished her only joy, as the three of them stepped forward into the bright, uncharted morning.
