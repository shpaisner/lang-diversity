The railway carriage rattled southward through the soot-stained heart of England, carrying me away from the blast furnaces of Staffordshire and toward the sea. In my coat pocket, my fingers never left the cold, checkered grip of the revolver I had bought in Birmingham with the last of my savings. Every rhythmic clack of the iron wheels seemed to beat out their names: Nettie and Verrall, Nettie and Verrall. She had chosen his smooth, gentlemanly ease over the grime beneath my fingernails, and the thought of them together in some bright seaside lodging burned in my chest like vitriol.

Outside the smeared window, the evening sky was no longer the honest black of my youth. A vast, pale-green smudge hung above the horizon, its feathery tail stretching halfway across the zenith like a luminous broom. Men in the compartment muttered over their evening papers, their faces lit by the feeble oil lamp and the eerie, emerald twilight seeping through the glass. The headlines shrieked of ultimata, of mobilized reserves, and of ironclad squadrons steaming into the North Sea. Europe was a dry powder-keg, and the newspapers were already striking the matches. Yet I cared nothing for the Kaiser or the Admiralty. My own private war was all that remained to me, and as the train shrieked into the junction, I stepped onto the platform with murder wrapped tightly in my threadbare coat. Overhead, the comet pulsed with a cold, indifferent splendor, casting long, sickly shadows across the bustling station as newsboys wailed the latest dispatches of impending slaughter.

By noon the following day, I reached the coastal town of Shaphambury, where the chalk cliffs tumbled down to a restless, slate-gray sea. Even here, far from the Potteries and the belching chimneys, the world felt feverish and deranged. Along the promenade, prosperous holidaymakers clustered around the telegraph office, their parasols forgotten as they read the latest bulletins pinned to the mahogany boards. Rumor had it that shots had already been exchanged off the Dogger Bank, and a detachment of red-coated soldiers marched past the bandstand to the cheers of anxious, red-faced clerks.

I wandered the gravel walks like a ghost from another planet, my boots cracked and dusty, my collar frayed. At the Grand Hotel, behind tall panes of plate glass, waiters in crisp linen carried silver trays to tables draped in white damask. It was there I first caught sight of Verrall’s motor-car, parked beside the portico like a gleaming brass beetle. The sheer, effortless insolence of his wealth tightened my throat. I spent the afternoon lurking near the bathing machines, watching the tide creep in beneath a sky that had grown strangely heavy and brassy. Long before sunset, the green comet became visible even in the daylight—a monstrous, glowing spearhead piercing the upper air. The gulls wheeled silently, unsettled by the unnatural stillness, and a faint, sweetish odor, like crushed almonds and ozone, began to drift across the shingle. I checked the chambers of my revolver one last time beneath my coat, waiting for the dusk that would draw them out into the open.

Dusk fell not with comforting darkness, but with an uncanny, phosphorescent twilight. The comet had swelled until its misty coma dominated the heavens, bathing the chalk headlands and the silent sea in a ghostly, aquamarine glow. I found them at last, walking along the high footpath that wound above the cliffs toward the deserted dunes. Nettie wore a pale summer dress that seemed to drink in the emerald radiance; she was laughing, her hand resting lightly on Verrall’s sleeve.

Watching them from the shadow of a gorse bush, all the accumulated bitterness of my twenty-two years—the cramped cottage, the coughing fits from the kiln dust, the endless deference owed to men like Verrall—surged into a single, blinding resolve. He had everything: money, leisure, education, and now the one bright thing I had ever dared to claim. As they paused by a wooden stile to look out over the luminous water, I stepped onto the path, my boots crunching loudly on the flint.

"William!" Nettie gasped, the color draining from her face in the weird green light.

Verrall instinctively stepped half a pace in front of her, his jaw setting with the practiced authority of his class. "Leadford? What madness is this? Go back to Staffordshire."

"I have come for what you stole," I said, my voice trembling with a terrible, hollow resonance. I drew the revolver from my pocket. The barrel gleamed like wet ice in the comet’s rays.

"Put that down, you fool!" Verrall barked, though I saw the sudden flicker of terror in his eyes as he gauged the distance between us. Nettie cried out my name again, reaching forward, her face twisted with a pity that only inflamed my humiliation.

I raised the heavy iron weapon, aiming squarely at Verrall’s chest. My finger tightened on the trigger. Yet at that precise instant, a profound and terrifying silence fell over the headland. The distant murmur of the surf seemed to vanish, swallowed by a sudden, rushing sigh in the upper atmosphere. Looking up involuntarily, I saw that the comet’s tail had broadened into a vast, incandescent curtain of emerald vapor, sweeping down toward the earth like a falling sea.

My hand jerked, and the revolver cracked sharply in the still air. The bullet whined harmlessly into the chalk turf beside Verrall’s boot. Before I could cock the hammer for a second shot, the green mist enveloped us. It was not cold, nor did it choke the lungs like the smoke of the Black Country; instead, it carried an intoxicating, crystalline sweetness that rushed straight to the brain. My knees buckled beneath me as if the marrow had turned to water. Through swimming eyes, I saw Verrall stagger backward and sink onto the grass, while Nettie folded gently beside him like a broken flower. The pistol slipped from my numb fingers, and as the emerald darkness closed over my mind, the last thing I felt was a strange, overwhelming sense of relief.

When I opened my eyes, the green nightmare had vanished. Above me stretched a sky of such immaculate, breathtaking purity that it seemed newly forged—a deep, vibrant azure streaked with the rose-gold of early dawn. I sat up slowly on the dewy turf, expecting the familiar throb of anger, the sour taste of defeat, and the grinding ache of my own insignificance. None of them were there.

Instead, my mind felt as clear and spacious as the morning itself. It was as though a heavy, soot-caked curtain had been ripped from the windows of my soul, letting in the clean sunlight of reason. I looked down at the revolver lying in the grass a few feet away. The sight of it filled me not with triumph or regret, but with a profound, quiet astonishment—like an adult stumbling upon a cruel, broken toy from a forgotten childhood. How small, how feverish and blind my hatred seemed in this tranquil air!

A few yards away, Verrall stirred, pushing himself up on one elbow. He blinked at the luminous sky, then looked at me. There was no fear in his face, nor any of the haughty condescension that had defined him yesterday. Beside him, Nettie sat up, brushing blades of grass from her skirt, her eyes wide with the same bewildered clarity. Down below, in the valley of Shaphambury, the church bells were not ringing an alarm, nor could I hear the harsh shouting of newsboys; only the peaceful, rhythmic wash of the sea against the shingle rose through the crystalline air.

I rose to my feet, picked up the revolver, and walked to the edge of the cliff. Without hesitation, I tossed the weapon out over the white chalk drop; it tumbled silently through the morning light and vanished into the blue water far below. When I turned back, Nettie and Verrall were standing together, watching me.

"William," Nettie said softly. Her voice held none of the frantic guilt of the night before, only a gentle, wondering recognition. "What has happened to us? To the world?"

"I think," I replied, marveling at the steadiness of my own voice, "that we have woken up."

Verrall stepped forward, extending his hand—not as a superior offering charity, nor as a rival seeking truce, but simply as one man greeting another. I took it, and for the first time in my life, I felt no bitterness at the softness of his palm. We walked back together along the cliff path toward the waking town. Already, small groups of townsfolk and soldiers were emerging onto the promenade, looking up at the pale, receding wisp of the comet in the eastern sky with expressions of dazed, luminous joy. In the harbor, the smoke of the warships was thinning, and men in uniform were laughing as they tossed their rifles into the surf. I knew then that the old world of squalor, jealousy, and senseless slaughter was dead, and that whatever strange, vast future awaited us beneath that purified sky, we would build it together as equals.
