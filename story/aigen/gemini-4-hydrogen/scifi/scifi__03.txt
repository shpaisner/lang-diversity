In the cramped, soot-stained parlor of his mother’s cottage in Clayton, William Leadford sat cleaning a cheap, five-chambered revolver with an oily rag. Outside the narrow window, the Staffordshire Potteries lay beneath their usual pall of yellow smoke, yet even through that industrial grime, a strange, greenish luminescence stained the twilight. For three weeks the comet had swelled in the northern sky, growing from a faint smudge among the constellations into a jagged spear of emerald fire. Men in the public houses argued whether it meant pestilence or the end of the world, while the newspapers screamed of a different doom. Every edition brought fresh headlines of ultimatums, mobilized reserves, and German cruisers steaming through the North Sea mist. William cared nothing for empires or dreadnoughts. His world had narrowed to the cold iron in his palm and the memory of Nettie’s face—pale, laughing, and turned away from him forever. She had gone with Edward Verrall, the young gentleman with the smooth hands, the motor-car, and the easy, condescending smile that seemed to purchase everything William could only covet from afar. William slipped five brass cartridges into the cylinder and snapped it shut. The click sounded crisp and final in the quiet room. Down the street, a newsboy’s shrill voice echoed the madness of Europe, crying out that the Channel Fleet had put to sea. William pocketed the revolver, pulled his worn cap low over his eyes, and stepped out into the gathering green dusk, bound for the railway station.

The southbound train rattled through the darkened Midlands like a frightened beast. William sat wedged in a third-class compartment between two silent reservists in khaki and a red-faced commercial traveler who kept muttering over a crumpled Daily Telegraph. Through the soot-streaked glass, the English countryside looked unfamiliar, its hedges and pastures washed in a sickly, phosphor-green glow from the sky. The comet was higher now, its bright nucleus casting faint, double shadows across the carriage floor whenever the train slowed. The traveler tapped his newspaper, declaring to no one in particular that war with Germany was inevitable before midnight, and that the fleet would sweep the seas by dawn. One of the soldiers nodded grimly, staring at his own knuckles. William said nothing. He kept his right hand buried in his coat pocket, his fingers curled around the checkered grip of the revolver. A strange kinship bound him to the feverish headlines and the grim men beside him. Europe was rushing toward slaughter over borders and pride, and he was rushing toward the Essex coast to settle a debt of blood and humiliation. It seemed to him that the whole world had grown tight, airless, and unbearable, coiled like a spring ready to snap. When the train finally hissed to a halt at the junction near Shaphambury, William pushed past the jostling soldiers onto the platform, his eyes fixed on the eastern horizon where the emerald tail of the comet fanned upward like a drawn sword.

Shaphambury was a resort town paralyzed by dread. Along the empty esplanade, gas lamps flickered weakly, dwarfed by the monstrous radiance overhead. The comet now spanned a third of the heavens, a vast, fan-shaped plume of luminous vapor that turned the breaking surf into liquid jade. Down on the shingle, a handful of townsfolk stood in silent clusters, staring out across the black water. From somewhere far beyond the horizon came a low, rhythmic thudding—not thunder, but the unmistakable concussion of heavy naval guns. War had begun out there in the darkness, miles from shore, where men were drowning in the cold sea beneath an alien star. William turned his back on the crowd and followed the sandy track that wound along the cliffs toward the lonely dunes. He had traced them easily enough; a porter at the station remembered a young gentleman in a grey touring coat and a dark-haired girl asking the way to the solitary bungalow above the marsh. The wind off the sea was warm and curiously still, carrying the faint, acrid tang of cordite or perhaps only the salt of the tide. Ahead, nestled in a hollow of marram grass, a single square of yellow lamplight glowed behind drawn curtains. William slowed his pace, stepping off the path into the soft sand so his boots made no sound. His heart hammered against his ribs, steady and merciless as the distant cannonade.

He waited in the shadow of a gorse bush until the bungalow door opened. Nettie stepped onto the wooden veranda first, wrapped in a pale shawl, followed closely by Verrall. They stood together at the railing, looking up at the terrible, emerald-lit sky, their faces turned toward the distant flashes that pulsed along the sea’s edge. Even from thirty paces away, William could see how Verrall’s arm rested lightly around her shoulders, protective and assured. A surge of bitter, choking rage rose in William’s throat, washing away the last remnant of his hesitation. He stepped out from the gorse, crunching over the dry shells, and raised the revolver. "Nettie!" he called out, his voice hoarse and ragged against the hush of the dunes. She gasped, spinning around, while Verrall instinctively stepped in front of her, shielding her with his body. In the eerie green glare, Verrall’s handsome face looked pale and startled, yet he did not cower. "Leadford?" Verrall said, his tone sharp with disbelief. "Put that thing down. Are you mad?" William laughed, a short, ugly sound that caught in his chest. He took two slow steps forward, the barrel trembling slightly in his grip as he aimed at the center of Verrall’s coat. "You stole everything I had," William whispered, his finger tightening on the trigger. "You and your kind—you take and you take, and you think there’s never a reckoning." Nettie cried out his name, reaching past Verrall’s shoulder, just as the sky above them tore open in a silent, blinding sheet of emerald light.

William pulled the trigger, yet the sharp crack of the pistol seemed small and absurdly distant, swallowed by a sudden, rushing sigh that swept across the heavens. The bullet tore harmlessly into the wooden steps beside Verrall’s boot. Above them, the comet’s vast tail had grazed the upper reaches of the Earth’s atmosphere, and a strange, luminous mist began to condense out of the clear night air. It was not cold, nor did it choke the lungs; instead, a sweet, pungent fragrance—like crushed mint and summer rain after a long drought—filled the hollow of the dunes. The distant booming of the naval guns faltered, then ceased altogether, as though a heavy velvet curtain had been drawn across the sea. William tried to raise the revolver for a second shot, desperate to cling to his hatred, to finish what he had come so far to do. But his arm felt extraordinarily heavy, and the fierce, knotted tension in his chest began to unravel like wet thread. Across the veranda, Nettie’s knees buckled; she sank gracefully to the boards, while Verrall slumped beside her, his head dropping onto his arm without a groan. William’s own eyelids grew leaden. He staggered backward, the pistol slipping from his numb fingers into the soft marram grass. As he sank onto the cool sand, watching the emerald fog thicken until it blotted out the stars, his last thought was neither of vengeance nor of war, but of an immense, inexplicable peace.

When William opened his eyes, the green fog was gone, and the world lay bathed in a dawn of astonishing, crystalline clarity. The eastern sky was a pale, spotless gold, devoid of both the comet’s menacing plume and the smoky haze that usually clung to the horizon. He sat up slowly, brushing fine white sand from his worn coat. Beside his boot lay the cheap revolver, its nickel plating dull with morning dew. He picked it up, turned it over once in his palm, and marveled at how small, ugly, and senseless the contraption looked in the pure light of day. With a quiet flick of his wrist, he tossed it deep into the tangled gorse. He stood and looked out across the sea. Far beyond the surf, two dark steel dreadnoughts rested motionless upon the glassy water, their funnels smokeless, their guns silent, as if the men aboard them had woken from the same long nightmare. Turning back toward the bungalow, William saw Nettie and Verrall stirring upon the veranda. Nettie sat up first, pushing her dark hair from her eyes, and looked straight at him. There was no terror in her gaze, no guilt, only a quiet, wondering recognition. Verrall rose stiffly, looked down at the splintered mark in the wooden step, and then met William’s eyes with a steady, unguarded candor. For the first time in his life, William felt no envy, no bitterness, and no fear. He walked slowly toward the veranda steps, ready to speak to them—not as an injured rival, nor as a creature of soot and hunger, but as a man stepping into a newborn, rational world.
