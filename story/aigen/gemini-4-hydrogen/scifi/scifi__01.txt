The third-class carriage rattled southward, bearing William Leadford away from the blackened brick and belching chimneys of Staffordshire. In his threadbare coat pocket, the cheap Belgian revolver knocked rhythmically against his hipbone, a cold, dense reassurance. Opposite him, an abandoned newspaper rustled in the draft, its bold black headlines screaming of ultimatums, mobilized reserves, and ironclads steaming into the North Sea. Europe was poised on the lip of a precipice, shivering with a terrible, collective fever, yet Leadford scarcely cared. His own small world had already shattered. Nettie—his Nettie, promised to him in the smoky twilight of the Potteries—had fled with young Verrall, the mine-owner’s son, seduced by clean linen, soft hands, and the effortless arrogance of wealth.

Leadford pressed his forehead against the soot-streaked windowpane. Above the darkening hedgerows of the Midlands, the sky was no longer a familiar dome of indifferent stars. Hanging low in the east was the comet. For weeks it had been a mere astronomers’ curiosity, a faint smudge of pale light reported in the penny press; now it was a monstrous, sweeping scimitar of greenish-white fire. Its tail fanned across a third of the firmament, bathing the rushing English countryside in a ghostly, unfamiliar luminescence. The other passengers in the carriage murmured anxiously, glancing upward between their fearful discussions of German cruisers and continental trenches. Leadford merely stared at the green star with a grim, vindictive satisfaction. Let the world burn, he thought. Let the empires tear each other to pieces under that baleful eye. Before the sky fell, he would have his reckoning on the Sussex coast.

By the time he reached the seaside resort of Shaphambury, night had fallen, though it was a night unlike any in human memory. The gas lamps along the esplanade burned with a feeble, yellow insignificance against the vast, emerald radiance pouring from the heavens. The town was a study in frantic contradiction. At the railway station, young men in ill-fitting khaki jostled through tearful crowds, their brass buttons gleaming faintly green as a brass band blared patriotic airs with desperate, off-key bravado. Newsboys darted between carriages, shrieking of broken treaties and imminent invasion, their faces pale and pinched beneath the cosmic glare.

Leadford slipped through the commotion like a shadow. His boots were gray with dust, his collar frayed, his mind narrowed to a single, burning point of hatred. He found the Grand Hotel easily enough; its white stucco facade towered above the cliffs, illuminated by electric globes that seemed to mock his poverty. Through the tall, curtained windows of the dining room, he glimpsed crystal chandeliers, silver service, and the silhouettes of wealthy guests pretending that neither the war nor the sky above could touch them. He did not enter. Instead, he took up his vigil on the dark, wind-swept promenade across the street, his fingers curled around the butt of the revolver. Hours dragged by. The patriotic music faded; the crowds thinned as an eerie, breathless hush settled over the Channel. Leadford shivered, not from the sea breeze, but from the intoxicating poison of his own resolve, waiting for the glass doors of the hotel to swing open.

Near midnight, they appeared. Verrall came first, impeccably tailored in an evening coat, laughing softly at something he had said, with Nettie clinging to his arm. She wore a pale silk wrap that caught the comet’s unearthly glow, making her look like a phantom drifted down from the stars. They did not turn toward the town, but strolled along the gravel path leading up to the chalk downs above the sea, drawn—like half the waking world—to watch the celestial prodigy.

Leadford followed at a distance, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. Here, away from the town’s lamps, the comet’s light was blindingly distinct, casting sharp, double-edged shadows across the short turf. The sea below looked like a plain of beaten copper and verdigris. As Nettie and Verrall paused near the cliff’s edge, looking out toward France, Leadford stepped from behind a clump of gorse.

"Nettie," he called out. His voice sounded thin and cracked in the vast silence.

She spun around with a sharp gasp, her hand flying to her throat. Verrall stepped instinctively in front of her, his patrician features hardening as he recognized the haggard, wild-eyed figure before them. "Leadford? What the devil are you doing here?"

Leadford drew the revolver. The steel glinted green. "I've come to settle accounts, Verrall. With both of you."

Before Verrall could lunge or Nettie could scream, a sudden, inexplicable change swept over the night. The salt wind died instantly, as though a great invisible door had slammed shut. High above, the green veil of the comet’s tail seemed to thicken and descend, dissolving into a luminous, palpable mist that smelled faintly of bruised violets and summer lightning.

The mist did not drift; it poured downward from the zenith, heavy and strangely warm, enveloping the cliffs, the sleeping town, and the restless Channel in a soft, emerald twilight. Across the continent of Europe, that same atmospheric tide was sweeping over millions. In the North Sea, great ironclad dreadnoughts lost their way as lookouts slumped silently over their railings; along the borders of Alsace and Flanders, batteries of Krupp guns stood unmanned beneath the glowing sky, while the telegraph wires linking London, Paris, and Berlin hummed with unanswered signals before falling utterly mute. Humanity’s frantic machinery of slaughter was grinding to a halt, choked by a breath of alien air.

On the cliff path, Leadford tried to pull the trigger. His forefinger trembled against the metal, spurred by years of humiliation, of cold Staffordshire winters and bitter, unspoken slights. Yet a profound, velvet heaviness was invading his limbs. The rage in his chest seemed to uncoil, not into calm, but into a vast, dizzying numbness. He saw Verrall stagger, reaching blindly for the wooden railing of the cliff path, and Nettie sinking slowly to her knees upon the turf, her pale wrap pooling around her like spilled milk.

Leadford’s knees buckled. The revolver slipped from his nerveless fingers and struck a flint stone with a dull clack. He pitched forward into the damp grass, his cheek pressed against the cool earth. Looking up through heavy, closing lids, he saw the sky had vanished entirely behind a swirling canopy of green luminescence. Then, with a sound like a long, collective sigh, consciousness left him, and the old world—with all its borders, its jealousies, and its ancient, bloodstained pride—passed into a deep and dreamless sleep.

Morning broke, not with the murky, coal-tinted gray of an English dawn, but with a crystalline clarity that made the eyes ache with wonder. Leadford woke slowly, drawing a deep breath of air that tasted sharper, sweeter, and infinitely more vital than any he had ever known. It was as though his lungs, clogged since childhood by the soot of the Potteries, had been rinsed with mountain spring water.

He sat up, blinking in the pure, golden sunlight. The grass around him sparkled with heavy dew, and the sea below stretched out in a dazzling expanse of cobalt blue. Beside his boot lay the Belgian revolver, looking absurdly small and foul—a rusted, childish toy from a forgotten nightmare. He stared at it, and for the first time in his life, he saw his own heart without the distorting lens of vanity and resentment. The cramped, poisonous hatred that had driven him across England seemed like a foul fever he had finally sweated out. He felt a sudden, overwhelming pity for the wretched man he had been only hours before.

A few paces away, Nettie stirred, pushing herself up on one elbow. Near her, Verrall was waking, rubbing his temples with a bewildered expression. When Nettie’s eyes met Leadford’s, there was no terror in them, no defiance, and no shame—only a clear, quiet astonishment.

"Will?" she whispered, her voice devoid of the brittle defensiveness she had worn for months.

"Nettie," he replied gently. He looked at Verrall, expecting the old sting of class hatred, the urge to sneer at the man’s fine clothes and soft hands. Instead, he saw only another frightened, bewildered mortal waking into a strange new day. "Are you hurt?" Leadford asked them both, and meant it with every fiber of his being.

Together, the three of them walked down the winding path toward Shaphambury, moving slowly, like travelers newly arrived in an undiscovered country. No one spoke of last night’s pistol, nor of the broken promises that had brought them to the cliffs. Such things belonged to a smaller, darker species of men that had vanished in the green mist. When they reached the esplanade, they found the town stirring in a strange, hushed awakening. Doors stood open; men and women in nightclothes and crumpled evening dress stood on their doorsteps, staring at the radiant sky and at one another with eyes washed clean of suspicion.

Down by the seawall, a platoon of reservists in khaki had gathered. Their rifles lay in a tangled heap upon the pebbles, abandoned like useless firewood. One young soldier was laughing softly as he tossed his ammunition belt into the foaming surf, while an elderly gentleman in a silk top hat—a man who yesterday would have crossed the street to avoid a common laborer—sat beside him on a bench, sharing a loaf of bread and speaking in earnest, quiet tones. Across the street, the morning’s newspaper placards, screaming their frantic declarations of war, hung torn and unheeded in the breeze, utterly irrelevant in the light of this new dawn.

Leadford paused beside Nettie and Verrall, looking out across the shimmering Channel. Far on the horizon, a faint smudge of coal smoke marked where a warship had drifted, its engines dying, its purpose forgotten. The comet had not brought the fiery apocalypse the prophets had feared; instead, it had altered the very air men breathed, burning away the ancient madness of greed, envy, and bloodshed. Ahead of them lay the immense, uncharted labor of rebuilding a sane world, and as Leadford looked at his companions, he knew that humanity’s true history was only just beginning.
