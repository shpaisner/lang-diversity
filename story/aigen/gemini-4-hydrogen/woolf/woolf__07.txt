The soot settled softly, interminably, upon the cabbage leaves in the narrow yard, upon the slate roofs of Clayton, upon William Leadford’s knuckles as he sat by the kitchen window. Inside, the clock ticked with a dry, consumptive rattle; outside, the Potteries exhaled their yellow smoke into a sky that was no longer honest twilight, but stained—yes, stained a pale, bruised emerald by that thing hanging above the chimneys. His mother moved behind him, the scrape of her iron pot against the grate sounding like the very voice of their poverty, endless and uncomplaining. He hated it; he hated the grease on the oilcloth, the dampness rising from the flagstones, the way his own chest felt hollowed out and stuffed with cinders. In his coat pocket, his fingers closed over the cold, ribbed butt of the revolver. Five shillings it had cost him, a week’s bread, a week’s tea, bought from a pawnbroker with watery eyes. Nettie’s letter lay crumpled beside the sugar bowl, three lines of neat, sloping ink—Forgive me, Will, I cannot bear this greyness any longer—and behind the words, he saw Verrall. He saw Verrall’s white hands, his linen collar, the easy, insulting tilt of his chin as he handed Nettie into a motorcar. Up above the smoke-stacks, the comet burned brighter tonight, a swollen green jewel, indifferent as a duke. Let it fall, William thought, staring at the poisoned sky; let it crack the world in two like a cheap saucer.

At Shaphambury, the sea air tasted of salt and expensive leisure, and Nettie stood by the tall bow window of the boarding-house, watching the tide creep up the shingle. How strange it was, she thought, pressing her cool forehead against the glass, to be here, wrapped in a shawl of softest cashmere, with no grate to blacken and no mother’s cough echoing down the stairs. Behind her, Edward Verrall was tapping an egg with a silver spoon, humming a bar from The Merry Widow. His hair was damp from his morning bathe; he looked, in his loose flannel jacket, entirely untouched by the grime that clung to the rest of England. Yet even as she smiled back at him—for he was kind, oh, wonderfully kind, and his laughter made the world seem light as thistledown—a shadow fell across the white tablecloth. It was not merely the memory of William’s dark, furious eyes, though she felt them sometimes like needles between her shoulder blades. It was the sky itself. All afternoon the fishermen had stood by their boats, squinting upward at the greenish haze that dimmed the sun, muttering over the newspapers Verrall had tossed onto the sofa. Ultimatum, the black headlines cried. Mobilization in the North Sea. Verrall only shrugged, pouring the cream, saying that politicians were always barking at the moon. But Nettie, watching a gull wheel against that queer, luminous green, felt a sudden chill, as if the whole bright, fragile bubble of their happiness were floating over an abyss.

Click-clack, click-clack, sang the iron wheels beneath the third-class carriage, carrying William southward through the bruised dusk. Opposite him, two commercial travellers in bowler hats leaned over a spread of evening papers, their voices rising and falling with a terrible, cheerful certainty. Dreadnoughts, they said; the Channel Fleet; we shall teach the Germans a lesson before Michaelmas. Their cigar smoke curled against the greasy upholstery, mingling with the smell of damp wool and coal dust. William stared out at the flying hedgerows, at the telegraph wires dipping and rising like frantic black threads. What did he care for their empires, their flags, their grand, bloody quarrels? His own war was small, sharp, and housed in his right pocket. Yet as the train hurtled past darkened stations—where clusters of young men in caps already stood beneath the gas-lamps, singing loud, hollow songs—he felt a strange kinship with the madness. The whole continent was tightening like a fist; Europe was holding its breath, waiting for the blow, just as his own heart hammered against his ribs, waiting for the sight of Verrall’s throat. Above the racing clouds, the comet had grown vast, a trailing smudge of phosphor and ice, casting a sickly, theatrical glare over the English fields. Even the cows standing knee-deep in the mist looked ghostly, painted in verdigris. Nobody seemed to notice, or if they did, they only spat into the gravel and talked louder of guns.

Dusk had fallen by the time he reached the cliffs above Shaphambury, though it was a dusk unlike any he had known—a shimmering, underwater twilight in which the chalk path glowed like bone. The sea below muttered against the shingle, restless and dark. He waited behind a clump of gorse, the salt wind stinging his eyes, his fingers stiff around the iron grip. Then he heard them. First came a woman’s laugh, light and sudden as a silver coin dropped on stone, and then Verrall’s deeper murmur. They emerged from the gloom of the promenade onto the grassy headland, walking close together, their figures mere dark strokes against the pale wash of the surf. Nettie’s scarf fluttered, a pale ribbon in the greenish dark. A terrible roaring filled William’s ears, louder than the tide, louder than the engines of Staffordshire; it was the sound of every humiliation he had ever swallowed, rising like bile. He stepped out onto the path. "Nettie!" he cried, though his voice cracked, thin and absurd in the vast salt air. She gasped, clutching Verrall’s arm; Verrall froze, half-turning, his face a pale, startled oval. William raised the pistol. He did not think of justice, nor of love, only of shattering that smooth, effortless calm. He pulled the trigger. The crack of the shot was sharp, ridiculous, swallowed instantly by the wind—and at that very second, the sky above them seemed to split, not with lightning, but with a silent, blinding rush of emerald mist.

It was not death, though the earth seemed to sigh and let go of its weight. A soft, sweet vapour, the colour of young willow leaves and deep sea-water, folded itself over the headland, over the town, over the distant, ironclad ships steaming through the dark Channel. William’s knees gave way; the revolver slipped from his numb fingers and vanished into the turf. He sank down, breathing in the strange air, which tasted faintly of mint and rain and something older than memory. Around him, the world was unmaking itself in absolute silence. Somewhere out in the blackness, a bugle began to blow a call to arms, and stopped mid-note, as if the brass had melted into the mist. In Staffordshire, the furnaces flickered and dimmed; in London, the shouting crowds grew quiet in the streets, looking up with parted lips as the green cloud washed over the soot-blackened stones. Time itself seemed to loosen its grip, like a wave poised at its crest before breaking, suspended in a vast, luminous hush. William lay against the damp earth, watching Nettie and Verrall sink softly to the grass a few paces away, like children overtaken by sleep in the middle of a game. The knot in his chest—that hard, bitter stone of envy and grief—dissolved, simply melted away into the cool emerald dark, leaving behind a lightness so profound he felt he might float upward into the stars.

When he opened his eyes, the sky was neither blue nor grey, but the colour of a thrush’s egg, washed clean by an unimaginable rain. The sun was rising over the sea, and every blade of grass beside his cheek held a single bead of dew that burned like a diamond. William sat up slowly, marvelling at the lightness of his own hands, at the quietness of his breath. A few feet away, half-buried in the clover, lay the cheap revolver. He looked at it for a long moment—a crooked, rusty little thing of iron and wood—and felt no shame, only a mild, wondering pity for the creature he had been yesterday, stumbling through the dark with murder in his pocket. Turning his head, he saw Nettie stirring upon the turf, and beside her, Verrall, propping himself on one elbow, blinking in the clear morning light. They looked at one another across the short space of grass, the three of them, stripped of every shield and every grievance. Nettie’s eyes were wide and clear as rainwater; there was no fear in them, nor any of that old, frantic defiance. And Verrall—William saw him now not as a thief or a master, but simply as another fragile, breathing soul, bewildered by the sudden beauty of the dawn. No one spoke; words seemed clumsy, like heavy boots in a garden. Somewhere beyond the bright horizon, the guns of Europe lay silent in the grass, their crews waking to the same astonishment. William rose to his feet, brushing the earth from his knees, and held out his hand—not to claim her, nor to strike him, but simply to help them both stand up in the light of the new day.
