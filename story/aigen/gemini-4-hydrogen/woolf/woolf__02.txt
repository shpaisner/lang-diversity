The soot fell, softly, persistently, settling upon the chipped saucer, upon the greasy edge of the Daily Mail, upon the knuckles of William Leadford’s clenched hand. Outside, the Potteries breathed their thick, yellow breath against a sky that had forgotten how to be blue. In the kitchen behind him, his mother coughed—a dry, rattling sound, like pebbles shaken in a tin—and asked if he had finished his tea. He did not answer. He was staring at the headlines: Crisis in the North Sea. The German Fleet Sails. Men were marching somewhere, boots striking cobbles, bayonets catching the sun, while here, in Clayton, the very air tasted of sulphur and defeat. And yet, above the smoking kilns and the black slag heaps, hung the star. A pale, greenish smear against the dusk, growing larger every night, indifferent to the Kaiser, indifferent to the price of coal, indifferent to the fact that Nettie had gone.

"Gone," he muttered, and the word tasted of iron. She had taken her straw hat, her little silver brooch, her laugh—that quick, bright laugh that used to dart through the gloom of the parlour like a swallow—and she had given them to Verrall. Verrall, with his clean cuffs and his father’s money, his motorcar that smelled of leather and petrol, his easy way of leaning against a gate as though the whole green earth belonged to him. William’s fingers twitched against the oilcloth. Fourteen shillings a week, and ink on his cuffs, and the whole of Europe sliding toward the abyss; and she had chosen a silk tie and a smooth voice. The green star pulsed, faint and cold, through the grimy pane.

He walked out into the lane, where the cinders crunched beneath his boots, and there it was again—the memory of the garden behind the vicarage, the smell of damp privet, the way the light had caught the tiny hairs on Nettie’s forearm. Why must the mind keep turning these things over, like a tongue probing a broken tooth? She had looked at him—not with cruelty, no, that would have been easier to bear—but with a kind of frightened pity, as one looks at a dog caught in a trap. I can’t, Willie, she had said, her fingers twisting the ribbon of her sash. I want to live. As if he were death! As if his love, which burned in him like a furnace fed with bad coal, were merely a damp cellar she must escape!

In the lining of his coat, heavy against his ribs, lay the revolver. He had bought it in Birmingham with money meant for winter boots, and now its cold, oiled weight was the only solid thing in a world gone fluid and treacherous. Everywhere, men were buying iron, casting iron, loading iron into the bellies of grey ships; why should he alone go unarmed? He saw Verrall’s face in the dusk—the smooth jaw, the slightly parted lips, the amused certainty in those pale eyes—and a red mist rose behind William’s forehead, blotting out the kilns, blotting out the comet, leaving only the narrow, scorching track of his own humiliation. He would follow them to the sea. He would stand before them, not as a clerk to be pitied, not as a beggar at the gate, but as a man who held the end of things in his hand.

The train rattled south, tearing through the Midlands, shaking the soot from its iron ribs until, all at once, the black country fell away and there were hedges, and elms standing knee-deep in buttercups, and red cows lifting their heavy heads to watch the carriages rush by. William sat in the corner of the third-class compartment, his knees pressed against the knees of a commercial traveller who smelled of peppermint and damp wool. Between them lay a newspaper, folded to the war news, its black type screaming of ultimatums and mobilizations. Nobody spoke. Every eye strayed, sooner or later, to the window, where the afternoon light seemed strangely altered—thinned out, tinted with a faint, unearthly emerald, as if the sun were shining through bottle glass.

When he stepped out at Shaphambury, the sea air struck him like a slap. It was vast, this emptiness of water and sky, after the cramped alleys of Staffordshire; it made his chest ache with a sudden, furious loneliness. The waves broke along the shingle with a long, dragging sigh—shhh, shhh—indifferent to the little boarding houses, indifferent to the grand hotel perched white and arrogant upon the cliff. Above the dark horizon, the comet now filled half the heavens, a luminous, trailing plume of green fire, casting no warmth, only a chill, phosphor glow upon the wet sand and the upturned faces of the townsfolk gathered along the promenade. Someone was crying softly near the bandstand; a dog barked, sharp and frantic, at the sky. William walked past them all, his hand in his pocket, his eyes fixed upon the lighted windows of the cliff-top hotel, where silk dresses moved behind the lace curtains and the world still pretended it was yesterday.

He found them on the cliff path, where the tamarisk bushes bent low in the salt wind and the turf smelled of wild thyme. They were walking slowly, Nettie’s hand tucked into the crook of Verrall’s arm, her white shawl fluttering like a moth against his dark coat. How small she looked, how fragile, beneath that immense, green-lit sky! William stepped out from the shadow of the gorse, and the gravel grated beneath his heel.

"Nettie," he said. His voice sounded thin, ripped away by the wind.

She stopped, her hand flying to her throat; Verrall stepped half a pace forward, his face pale in the comet’s glare, yet even then—God, how he hated him for it!—composed, upright, ready to parley with a madman as though he were a tradesman with an unpaid bill.

"Leadford," Verrall began, "what on earth—"

William raised the revolver. The iron shook in his hand, though his heart felt curiously still, a cold stone in his breast. Around them, the sea seemed to pause. The wind dropped. A strange, sweetish scent—like crushed mint, like snow, like the breath of an orchard after rain—crept over the cliff edge, thick and palpable, curling around their ankles. William tried to pull the trigger, tried to summon the hot, familiar rage that had carried him all the way from Staffordshire, but his fingers were heavy, impossibly heavy, as if dipped in lead. The green mist thickened, blinding the hotel lights, swallowing the stars. He heard Nettie give a little sigh, not of terror, but of immense fatigue, and saw Verrall’s knees buckle gently into the grass. Then the turf rose up to meet William’s cheek, soft as a pillow, and the great, green darkness folded over his eyes.

Light—not the yellow, smoky glare of the Potteries, nor the brassy heat of August, but a clear, washed radiance, like water poured over white pebbles—woke him. A lark was singing somewhere high above the cliff, its notes falling like drops of silver rain. William lay still for a long moment, staring at a single blade of grass beside his nose. It was extraordinarily green; every vein in it seemed to pulse with a quiet, triumphant life. He sat up, expecting the old weight in his chest, the familiar, sour knot of grievance and envy that he had carried since boyhood, as naturally as he carried his own ribs.

It was gone.

He looked down at his hands—the bruised knuckles, the ink-stain that never quite washed out of the forefinger—and felt a sudden, overwhelming tenderness for them, as if they belonged to a child who had worked very hard in the dark. Beside him in the wet clover lay the revolver, its barrel already filmed with dew, looking absurdly small and clumsy, a pitiful scrap of iron. He picked it up, marvelling that anyone could ever have thought a piece of metal might settle the ache of a human heart, and tossed it gently over the cliff edge. Far below, the sea received it without a sound. He thought of his mother coughing in the narrow kitchen; he thought of the young men in grey uniforms and khaki, standing bewildered on the decks of battleships, waking from the same long, feverish dream. How strange it was—how blindingly simple—that they had all been so frightened, and so cruel, when the sky was so wide.

A few yards away, Nettie stirred in the grass. She pushed herself up on one elbow, her hair tangled with bits of clover, her white shawl damp with dew, and looked straight at him. There was no fear in her eyes now, no shrinking; only a clear, wondering recognition, as if she were seeing him for the first time without the smoke of the Four Towns between them. Beside her, Verrall sat up slowly, rubbing his forehead, his silk tie crooked, his face stripped of its old, defensive pride.

"Willie," Nettie said softly. Her voice had lost its brittle edge; it sounded like water running over stones. "What has happened to us?"

"We were asleep," William said, and he rose and walked toward them, feeling the damp earth spring beneath his boots. "All of us. For a very long time."

Verrall looked up at him, squinting against the morning sun, and offered his hand—not with the condescension of a gentleman, nor the guilt of a rival, but simply, as one man offers a hand to another on a steep path. William took it. The warmth of Verrall’s palm surprised him; they were made of the same clay, after all, breathing the same astonishing air. Nettie stood up between them, brushing the grass from her skirt, and together the three of them turned toward the sea. Below the cliffs, the little town was waking; smoke began to rise from a chimney, thin and blue against the brightness, and far out on the horizon, where the great grey warships had steamed through the night, there was only the empty, glittering water, and the white gulls wheeling in the sun.
