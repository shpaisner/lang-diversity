The kettle hissed on the hob, a thin, fretful sound against the deeper, ceaseless rumble of the kilns beyond the window. William Leadford sat at the oilcloth table, his knuckles grey with the grime of the yard that no amount of yellow soap could quite coax from the creases, and stared at the newspaper. Mobilization. The black letters marched across the cheap paper, stiff and belligerent, while above the chimneys of Clayton, beyond the smear of coal smoke and the jagged silhouettes of the slag heaps, a pale green star hung in the evening sky. It had grown larger since Tuesday. People stopped in the cobbled lanes to point at it, their faces upturned and momentarily emptied of worry, before returning to their pennies, their coughs, their dread of the coming war. Yet for William, the whole vast machinery of Europe—the diplomats in Berlin, the ironclads steaming through grey northern waters—was nothing beside the image of Nettie Stuart’s wrist, the way a loose curl fell against her collar when she laughed. He loved her with a fierce, bruised possessiveness that tasted like copper in his mouth. To be poor in Staffordshire was to live inside a box with the lid nailed down; only Nettie, with her quick eyes and her lightness, seemed to belong to the open air. His mother sighed by the grate, folding a darned stocking. Outside, a cart rattled past, and the green light of the comet fell faintly upon the wet slate roofs, cold and indifferent as a jewel dropped in the mud.

By Thursday the box had broken open, and there was nothing inside it but wind and humiliation. She had gone—not with a word spoken plainly to his face, not even with the decent cruelty of a quarrel, but vanished into the bright, effortless world of the people who owned the mines and the potteries and the very air one breathed. Verrall. Even the name had the smooth, round sound of a sovereign spinning on mahogany. William had seen him once, lounging in the back of a motorcar in white flannels, his skin untouched by soot, his eyes lazily amused as if the whole grimy valley were a stage set up for his passing entertainment. And Nettie had gone to him; she had stepped out of Clayton as easily as one steps out of a soiled pinafore. Walking down the lane past the brick walls, William felt the revolver in his coat pocket knocking against his hip—heavy, solid, a knot of cold iron in the midst of his spinning thoughts. He had bought it with his saved shillings, though even as he paid the man in the pawnshop, he had seemed to watch himself from a great distance, as though another William, small and frantic, were playing out a dreary farce. Overhead, the comet was no longer a point of light; it had unfurled a great fan of emerald vapor across the zenith, turning the twilight the color of deep sea-water. The men at the corner were shouting about an ultimatum, their voices hoarse with whisky and terror, but William heard only the blood hammering in his ears.

All through the afternoon the train tore southward, shaking its wooden carriages as if trying to rattle itself free of the century. Opposite William, two clerks in bowler hats rustled their evening papers, their faces pale in the strange, greenish gloom that seeped through the dirty glass. Ultimatum. The Fleet. They spoke of battleships as though they were toys they had wound up and could no longer stop, their voices rising and falling in nervous, meaningless gusts. William looked out at the passing fields—the red brick of the Midlands giving way to gentler hills, to chalk and hedgerows, to the bright, indifferent sprawl of the south. Everything was bathed in that uncanny, luminous mist from the sky, so that the sheep in the meadows looked like ghostly shapes drifting under water. He pressed his forehead against the vibrating pane. Why should he care if the empires tore themselves to pieces? Let the guns roar; let the sky fall. In his mind he saw Nettie’s face turned up to Verrall’s, saw Verrall’s hand resting lightly on her elbow, and a terrible, scorching shame seized him, worse than any hunger he had ever known. When he stepped onto the platform at Shaphambury, the salt air struck his lips—clean, sharp, smelling of kelp and wet shingle, utterly unlike the sulfur of home. Yet even here, by the edge of the sea, men stood in knots beneath the gas lamps, reading the latest dispatches, while above the dark horizon the comet blazed, immense and silent, filling half the heavens with its pale, unearthly fire.

He found them in the dusk where the tamarisk bushes leaned crookedly over the sand, their leaves whispering in the evening breeze. A white bungalow stood back from the shore, its windows lit, spilling warm yellow squares across the grass; and there, moving slowly along the path between the sea-lavender, were the two figures he had crossed England to destroy. Nettie wore a pale dress that seemed to catch every stray beam of the comet’s light; she was laughing, a clear, unhurried sound that carried over the wash of the waves. Verrall walked beside her, bareheaded, his hands in his pockets, bending his head to listen with that easy, assured grace that made William’s fingers tighten on the butt of the revolver. He stepped out from the shadow of the hedge. Nettie saw him first; her laughter broke off, a small, sharp intake of breath, and Verrall turned, startled, his hand half-raised. William lifted the pistol. He meant to speak, to shout some bitter, jagged truth about the pits and the hunger and the way the rich stole even the little light poor men had—but the words tangled in his throat. Above them, the sky seemed to open. A vast, cool wave of green mist rolled silently across the sea, swallowing the stars, swallowing the bungalow, fragrant as crushed mint and infinitely deep. His arm grew heavy as lead. The sand tilted beneath his boots. He saw Nettie sway, saw Verrall’s knees buckle, and then the green darkness closed over him, soft as sleep, washing every thought away.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world had been washed clean. He lay in the coarse grass beside the path, his cheek pressed against a tuft of sea-thrift, and for a long moment he simply watched a beetle climbing a blade of grass, marveling at the tiny, iridescent perfection of its back. There was no ache in his temples, no sour taste of soot or anger in his mouth. The air he drew into his lungs was extraordinarily sweet, sharp with salt and morning, and his mind felt like a room whose shutters had been flung wide after years of stale darkness. Sitting up, he saw the revolver lying a few inches from his boots, its barrel dull with dew. He picked it up, turned it over in his palm, and felt a sudden, quiet astonishment—not horror, not even shame, only a vast bewilderment that anyone should ever have carried such a clumsy, ugly little piece of iron in order to stop another human heart. He laid it down gently among the stones. All the old furniture of his mind—the cramped rooms of Clayton, the gnawing envy of Verrall’s smooth hands, the fierce, desperate clutching at Nettie as though she were a possession he might lock in a drawer—had dissolved like cobwebs in a bright wind. He stood up and brushed the sand from his coat. Far out, the sea lay smooth as silk, catching the gold of the morning, and he felt neither poor nor wronged, only awake, for the first time in his life, to the sheer, inexplicable wonder of being alive.

A few paces away, Nettie was sitting up on the grassy bank, her hair loose about her shoulders, staring out at the water as though she were seeing the ocean for the first time. Beside her, Verrall stirred, rubbed his eyes, and sat up slowly, his white shirt crumpled and stained with grass. Neither of them looked at William with fear; when Nettie’s eyes met his, there was no guilt in them, nor any of the old, coquettish defiance, only a clear, steady recognition. William walked over and sat down on the bank near them, his knees drawn up, looking out toward the horizon. For a long time nobody spoke; the only sound was the gentle, rhythmic shush of the tide turning on the shingle, and the distant cry of a gull wheeling in the cloudless sky. "We were going to kill each other," Verrall said at last, his voice quiet and stripped of its old, mocking drawl, as if he were speaking of a dream he had already half forgotten—not only the three of them, William knew, but the great ships in the North Sea, the armies massed along the rivers, all that vast, blind madness of yesterday. Nettie reached out and laid her hand lightly on the grass between them, not choosing one or the other, simply resting there in the warmth of the sun. Down the coast, a church bell began to ring, clear and unhurried, and William watched the light dancing on the waves, knowing that whatever they had been—master, laborer, lover—was over, and that the day had scarcely begun.
