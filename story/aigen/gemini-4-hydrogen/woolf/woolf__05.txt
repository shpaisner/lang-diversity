The smoke from the Potteries hung low over Clayton, a bruised, sulky purple against the evening sky, and yet—if one tilted one’s chin above the chimney pots and the ironworks—there it was, hanging like a drop of sea-water on a dark pane, the comet. William Leadford paused by the canal bridge, his boots heavy with the grey mud of the lane. All day the newspapers had rustled in the offices like dry leaves before a storm: ultimatums, cruisers steaming out of Portsmouth, the Chancellor’s stiff collar and stiffer words. War, they said, was a matter of hours. And in William’s pocket, his knuckles bruised against the coarse lining, lay nothing but three shillings and a folded scrap of Nettie’s handwriting, smelling faintly of lavender soap. How absurd, he thought, watching a coal-barge slide blackly through the oily water, that empires should rattle their sabres while a man’s whole universe shrank to the curve of a girl’s wrist. He hated the soot that settled in the creases of his neck; he hated the cracked plates on his mother’s dresser, the eternal smell of damp wool and boiled cabbage, the way his own voice roughened when he tried to speak of anything beyond wages. Above the slag heaps, the green star pulsed—cool, indifferent, terribly bright, as if some lantern had been swung out from behind the curtains of the world to inspect the little beetles scuttling below. William turned his collar up against the chill and walked on, his shadow stretching thin and jagged across the cinders.

In the kitchen, the clock ticked with a dry, elderly persistence, measuring out his mother’s silence. She sat by the grate, her hands folded in her lap like worn gloves, watching the kettle that would not boil. Nettie was gone. Not merely out for a stroll past the allotments, nor lingering by the draper’s window, but gone—swept up into that other England of smooth lawns, tennis flannels, and motor-cars that smelled of leather and expensive petrol. Edward Verrall had come for her; Verrall, with his easy laugh and his father’s money, who had never in his life wondered whether a shilling would stretch to Friday. William stood by the window, staring at the brick wall of the yard, and felt a cold, bright needle enter his chest. It was not grief, precisely; it was a furious, suffocating shame. They had taken his one green shoot and transplanted it to a conservatory, leaving him the cinders. Later, in the back room of a pawnbroker’s in Burslem, he laid down his coins and took up the revolver—a squat, ugly thing of dull metal, heavy as a stone in his coat pocket. All around him in the street, men stood in knots beneath the gas-lamps, reading the late editions, their faces upturned and pale. The fleets were moving in the North Sea, someone shouted; the world was catching fire. William touched the cold iron through the wool of his pocket and smiled, a tight, bitter movement of the lips. Let it burn, he thought; let it all burn down to the ash.

Far away, where the chalk cliffs broke cleanly into the salt foam, Nettie stood at the tall window of the hotel bedroom and watched the sea turn the colour of deepest damson. Behind her, Edward Verrall was whistling softly as he knotted his silk tie before the looking-glass—a careless, pleasant sound that seemed to belong to summer afternoons and strawberries under the cedar tree. She had escaped; she had stepped out of the narrow, soot-stained corridor of Staffordshire into this spaciousness of white curtains and rustling skirts. Yet, as she pressed her forehead against the cool glass, a strange restlessness stirred beneath her ribs. The sea was too vast, the sky too empty, save for that uncanny green star that had grown, night by night, until it cast a faint, watery sheen over the very bedspread. She thought, for a fleeting, unbidden moment, of William’s dark, fierce eyes, of the way his fingers trembled when he handed her a book of poems—as if the pages might scorch him. Poor William, always angry at the world for being what it was. Edward came up behind her, his hands resting lightly on her shoulders, warm and assured. "Look at the light on the water," he murmured, his breath brushing her ear. "Like emerald glass. Have you ever seen anything so queer?" Down on the esplanade, a newsboy’s cry floated up through the surf—something about Belgium, something about the reserves—thin and papery against the roar of the tide, and Nettie shivered, drawing the white curtain half across the sea.

The third-class carriage smelled of stale tobacco and wet macintoshes. William sat wedged in the corner, his knees pressed against the opposite seat, while two commercial travellers argued over the evening paper, their voices rising and falling like saws through timber. "They’ll have to fight," said the one with the red whiskers, slapping the page. "You can’t have the Germans marching through—" The train jolted over the points, drowning the rest in a clatter of iron. William did not listen. He had found the name of the seaside town on an envelope in Nettie’s drawer, and now every turn of the wheels carried him closer to the cliff, the hotel, the white-washed wall where his humiliation waited to be settled. Outside the smudged glass, the English countryside unrolled—hedgerows, dark elms, a lonely farmstead—all bathed in a pale, unearthly twilight. For the comet was no longer a star; it had spread across half the heavens like a peacock’s tail of luminous green mist, dimming the moon, turning the sheep in the meadows to ghostly, emerald shapes. Even the travellers fell silent at last, peering out at the sky with uneasy, half-ashamed faces, as though caught eavesdropping on something too vast for them. Only William kept his eyes fixed on his own reflection in the dark pane—hollow-cheeked, resolute, clutching the pocket of his coat. He would find them. He would show Verrall that a man from Clayton was not a thing to be brushed aside like dust from a sleeve.

By the time he reached the cliffs, the air itself seemed to have thickened, though it was not heavy like the fog of the Potteries; rather, it was sweet, cool, and faintly luminous, smelling of crushed mint and rain-washed stone. The hotel lights glowed behind tall pines, yellow and feeble against the vast green canopy of the sky. William crouched by the low flint wall of the garden, his breath coming in short, ragged gasps. And then, through the gap in the laurels, he saw them. They were walking slowly along the gravel path toward the cliff’s edge, Nettie’s pale shawl wrapped tight about her shoulders, Verrall leaning down to catch her words. Something snapped in William’s chest—a dry, brittle twig of rage. He stepped out onto the gravel, the revolver cold and slick in his sweating palm, his throat constricting around a cry he could not quite utter. They turned, startled, their faces pale green in the uncanny light. "William!" Nettie gasped, her hand flying to her throat. He raised his arm; he meant to speak, to accuse, to pull the trigger and shatter this smug, velvet night—but even as he sighted along the barrel, a great, silent wave of vapour rolled in from the sea. It was not smoke; it was like the breath of a sleeping giant, infinitely gentle, infinitely deep. His arm grew heavy as lead. Verrall staggered, his hand catching at a pine branch; Nettie sank slowly to her knees on the grass. Across Europe, the guns were falling silent in the mud, though none of them knew it. William’s fingers uncurled; the pistol dropped with a dull thud into the clover, and he slid down into a tide of dreamless, emerald sleep.

When William opened his eyes, the world had been washed in cold spring water. The green mist was gone, and in its place rose a dawn of astonishing, translucent clarity, pale gold and rose above a glassy sea. For a long moment he lay still upon the crushed clover, listening to a lark singing somewhere high above the chalk—a thin, silver thread of sound that seemed to stitch the sky to the earth. He looked at his own hand, resting palm-upward in the grass, and saw the grime beneath the fingernails, the small scar on the knuckle, as if they belonged to someone he had read about in a book long ago. Where was the knot in his chest? Where was the hot, cramped room of his anger? He sat up. A few paces away, Edward Verrall was rising slowly to his feet, brushing dry pine needles from his coat, blinking with the bewildered innocence of a child woken too early. Beside him, Nettie sat with her knees drawn up, her shawl fallen back, staring out at the horizon where a great grey battleship lay motionless on the water, harmless as a toy. When she turned and saw William, there was no terror in her face—only a quiet, wondering recognition. William stood up, leaving the ugly lump of iron in the grass without a second glance. He walked toward them, and Verrall did not step in front of her, nor did Nettie shrink away; they stood together in the cool morning light, three small figures on the edge of the land, looking at one another as if for the very first time, stripped of their clothes of pride and grievance, waiting to learn how to speak in this new, uncrowded world.
