The soot settled on the window-sill, grey as ash from a dead grate, and still the tramcar clanged down the High Street, shaking the little blue jug on the dresser. William Leadford stood with his forehead pressed against the glass. Out there, beyond the slag-heaps and the black ribs of the potteries, the sky had turned a curious, bruised violet, and through the smoke-wreaths hung the star—not a star, the papers said, though nobody in Staffordshire cared much for astronomy when bread was fourpence-halfpenny and the masters were locking out the men. A pale green smudge, trailing a faint veil of light above the chimney-pots.

In his coat pocket, his fingers closed round the cold iron of the revolver. Three pounds it had cost him—half his savings, scraped together while his mother coughed in the back kitchen, her kettle whistling its thin, perpetual complaint. Nettie, he thought, and the name tore through him like a rusty nail. How she had stood by the canal bridge in her white muslin, letting the lock-keeper’s lad, no, worse—Verrall, with his yellow dogcart and his smooth, insolent hands—lift her up as if she were a parcel of silk. Gone. Gone to the sea, to Brighton or Eastbourne or some grand hotel where the waiters bowed and the champagne fizzed. And all the while, down in the street, the newsboys were screaming about the North Sea, about German cruisers steaming through the dark, about the Empire on the brink. Let it burn, William thought, his heart hammering against his ribs. Let the whole rotten world crack in two; he would find them first.

The third-class carriage smelled of stale tobacco and damp wool, and the wheels beneath the floorboards beat out a tireless, maddening refrain: Verrall, Verrall, Verrall. Opposite him, a commercial traveller rattled the Daily Telegraph, his spectacles sliding down his nose. Ultimatum, the black headlines shouted; Mobilization; The Fleet at Sea. Men were sharpening bayonets in barracks; women were weeping at railway barriers; Europe was a great, clumsy ship drifting sideways onto the rocks, and nobody at the helm could hear the breakers for the noise of their own shouting.

William stared out at the flying hedgerows, the sudden flash of chalk cuttings, the flat, prosperous fields of the south that knew nothing of coal-dust or hungry bellies. He felt small and sharp as a needle. His mother’s face—pinched, patient, grey—rose before him for a second, and he thrust it down. What had patience ever bought her? A damp room and a cough. No; the world belonged to those who took it, to Verrall with his easy laugh, his tennis flannels, his way of looking at a girl as though she were a peach on a south wall. When the train slowed at last and the salt wind struck his cheek through the lowered sash, William stepped onto the platform with his hand still in his pocket. Overhead, the evening sky was vast and clear, emptied of smoke, and the green comet burned brighter now, casting a faint, uncanny sheen upon the whitewashed station roof, like moonlight filtered through deep water.

On the terrace above the shingle, Nettie leaned her elbows on the painted iron rail and watched the sea turn the color of pewter. Behind her, through the open French windows, she could hear Edward’s voice—so light, so wonderfully unhurried—asking the waiter for another siphon of soda. It was all like a picture in a magazine, she thought, the white tablecloths, the red geraniums in their urns, the soft, continuous sigh of the tide. Yet a strange restlessness pricked beneath her ribs. Why did the gulls keep circling so low, crying without settling? And why did Edward’s laughter sound, just for a moment, a little hollow, as if the vast, greenish dusk were swallowing it up?

She thought of William—not as he was, with his fierce eyes and ink-stained fingers, but as a shadow left behind in a dark room. She had been afraid of him; his love had been a heavy coat she could scarcely breathe inside. With Edward, everything was air and sunlight. Yet when Edward came out onto the terrace, slipping his arm around her waist, smelling of Turkish cigarettes and lavender water, she shivered.

"Look at that thing," he murmured, tilting his chin toward the sky, where the green star hung enormous over the Channel. "Makes the water look like absinthe, doesn't it? They say there's a battle off the Dogger Bank tonight. Ironclads sinking in the dark. Come inside, Nettie; the wind's turning cold."

She let him draw her toward the light, yet she looked back once over her shoulder at the empty esplanade, half-expecting to see a figure standing by the lamp-post.

They walked down to the cliffs after dinner, when the hotel band had stopped playing and the only sound was the drag of pebbles under the receding tide. William followed them. He had waited by the tamarisk hedge until the white blur of Nettie’s shawl appeared against the dark; he had crept behind them along the turf, his boots wet with dew, his breath tearing at his throat. Every humiliation of his twenty years—the cracked boots, the jeers of the clerks, the cold hearth—gathered into a single burning point behind his eyes.

"Nettie!" he cried, stepping out from the shadow of the gorse.

She gasped, pressing back against Verrall’s arm. Even in that eerie, emerald twilight, William could see the sudden white oval of her face, the startled widening of her eyes. Verrall stepped forward, tall and solid in his evening coat, his cane raised slightly—not in fear, only in annoyance, as one might wave away a stray dog.

"Leadford? What the devil—"

The revolver was out before William knew he had drawn it. The wind flattened the marram grass; far out at sea, a searchlight swept the horizon, looking for enemies. William fired. The crack was absurdly small against the roar of the surf, and Verrall staggered, clutching his shoulder, though whether from the bullet or the sudden terror of the night, William never knew. For at that instant, a great hush fell upon the cliffs. A sweet, cool vapour, smelling faintly of crushed mint and ozone, rolled in from the sea—not mist, not smoke, but the very breath of the comet, wrapping their knees, their chests, their faces. William’s fingers opened; the iron dropped softly into the grass, and he sank down, down into a green and dreamless sleep.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world had been washed clean.

He lay on his side in the short cliff-turf, his cheek pressed against a tuft of sea-thrift. A small brown beetle was climbing a blade of grass three inches from his nose, its carapace catching the morning light like a drop of polished amber. For a long time he simply watched it, filled with a quiet, unhurried wonder. Where had it gone—that tight, hot knot behind his breastbone, that fever that had driven him across half of England with murder in his pocket? It was gone, as completely as a bad dream vanishes when the curtains are drawn back.

He sat up slowly. Below him, the sea stretched out in long, luminous ribbons of turquoise and silver, utterly calm. He saw the revolver lying a yard away in the wet grass, its barrel already dull with dew, and he felt a sudden, half-smiling pity for the poor, cramped creature who had carried it there. How small they had all been, scurrying through their smoky streets, hoarding their pennies and their hatreds, while all the time the sky was so wide, the air so sweet! Somewhere down in the town, a church bell began to ring—not a warning, not a tolling for the dead, but a clear, wandering note that drifted up the cliff-face and dissolved in the bright air. He stood up, brushing the earth from his knees, and looked along the path where two figures were stirring beside the gorse.

Verrall was sitting with his back against a chalk boulder, his coat-sleeve torn where the bullet had grazed the cloth, though he seemed scarcely aware of it. Beside him, Nettie was kneeling, her hair loose about her shoulders, staring out across the Channel with the dazed, clear eyes of a child woken early. When William’s shadow fell across the grass, neither of them started. There was no fear left in the world to start at.

"William," Nettie said. Her voice was quiet, stripped of its old coquetry, its old nervous haste. She looked at his bruised knuckles, his dusty boots, and then up into his face, and he saw that she understood everything—the dark kitchen in Staffordshire, the bitter love, the long, blind fury—and forgave it as one forgives a storm that has passed.

"Are you hurt?" William asked Verrall, kneeling beside them on the turf.

Verrall touched his shoulder and shook his head, a slow, wondering smile touching his lips. "A scratch. Nothing. Look out there, Leadford."

Far off, beyond the headland, two grey battleships lay motionless upon the glassy water, their smoke-stacks idle, their guns silent. No signals flashed from their masts; no newsboys cried along the esplanade below. Whatever madness had driven men to build those iron hulls and send their sons to drown in the dark had evaporated in the night, leaving only the gulls wheeling in the sun, the smell of thyme on the warm turf, and the three of them sitting together on the cliff-edge, strangers no longer, waiting for the day to begin.
