The soot settled on the window-sill, fine and black as pepper, even though his mother wiped it twice a day with a damp rag. William stood by the sink, watching the grey light thicken over the Potteries, where the bottle-kilns squatted like giant, smoky jugs against the bruised sky. Nettie had gone. The fact of it had no edges yet; it was simply a cold draught blowing through the narrow kitchen, rattling the teacups on the dresser. Gone with Edward Verrall—Verrall of the big house on the hill, with his crisp linen and his effortless, laughing voice that seemed to buy everything it touched. William’s fingers tightened on the edge of the tin basin. On the oilcloth table lay the Sentinel, its black headlines screaming of ultimatums, of armies massing along the Rhine, of ironclads steaming through the dark. Europe was tilting toward the abyss, the men in the tavern shouted, banging their pewter pots; yet what was Europe to a man whose own small world had been kicked to pieces? Above the slag-heaps, where the evening star ought to have been, hung the comet—a pale, greenish smear, like a drop of stagnant water on glass. It looked down on the cramped rows of brick, on the weeping women and the swearing men, with a cold, indifferent brilliance. William reached for his coat. He would not stay here to choke on coal-dust while they laughed together by the sea.

The third-class carriage smelled of stale tobacco and damp wool, and the red plush beneath him was worn down to the grey canvas. Click-clack, click-clack, sang the wheels, hurrying him south, away from the furnaces, away from his mother’s silent, frightened eyes. In his coat pocket, his hand rested on the revolver. It was heavy, oiled, brutally simple—a knot of cold iron that seemed to anchor his drifting, feverish thoughts. Whenever he closed his eyes, he saw Nettie as she had been in June, pinning a sprig of hawthorn to her blouse, her chin tilted up, her laughter spilling out like water from a pitcher. How could she? The question beat in time with the rails. How could she give that to Verrall? Across the compartment, a clerk in a bowler hat rattled his newspaper, muttering about the Fleet, about the Kaiser’s madness, his voice thin and anxious. Outside the smudged window, the hedgerows and spinneys of middle England rushed past in a blur of darkening green, beneath a sky that had lost all ordinary colour. The comet had grown; its tail stretched half across the zenith now, casting a sickly, phosphorescent glow over the quiet pastures and the sleeping sheep. It seemed to William that the whole world was holding its breath, swollen with an unnatural heat, waiting for the match to be struck—whether by the great guns in the Channel, or by his own shaking hand in the dark.

At Shaphambury, the sea was the colour of pewter, lifting and falling against the shingle with a heavy, rhythmic sigh. Nettie stood on the gravel terrace of the hotel, drawing her silk shawl tighter around her shoulders, though the evening air was strangely warm. Behind her, through the tall french windows, she could see Edward leaning over a table, pouring claret, talking to an elderly gentleman in tweed about the mobilisation orders. Edward was charming; he moved through the world as though the furniture had been arranged expressly for his comfort. Yet watching him, Nettie felt a sudden, sharp loneliness, as if she were looking at a painting of a room she could never quite enter. She turned back to the water. Far out, beyond the pier, long white fingers of light swept the horizon—the searchlights of the cruiser squadron, probing the dark. A dull, muffled thump rolled across the bay, vibrating faintly in the soles of her slippers, like a heavy door slammed in a distant corridor. War, people whispered on the promenade, glancing nervously upward. For there, arching over the black silhouette of the cliffs, hung the great green star, immense and terrible, turning the white crests of the waves to emerald. She thought, for a fleeting second, of William—of his rough, ink-stained hands, his fierce, narrow pride—and shivered, wishing she could wash the green light from her skin.

He found them on the cliff path above the town, where the coarse grass bent in the salt wind and the chalk dropped sheer to the roaring dark below. Nettie’s pale skirt glimmered like a moth in the uncanny twilight; beside her, Verrall’s dark figure moved with that easy, hateful grace. William stepped out from behind a clump of gorse. His heart hammered so violently against his ribs that he could scarcely breathe, and his voice, when he forced it out, sounded thin and cracked against the boom of the sea. "Nettie!" She stopped, her hand flying to her throat; Verrall swung round, his pleasant face stiffening into wary surprise. William’s hand came out of his pocket, the barrel gleaming faintly green in the comet’s glare. "You stole her," he cried, though the words seemed absurdly small under that vast, blazing arch of sky. Verrall took a step forward, calm, measuring the distance, while Nettie gave a low, choked cry. William raised the iron, his finger tightening on the curve of the trigger—and then the air changed. A strange, sweet scent, like crushed clover and summer rain, drifted down from the heavens, thick and cool. A pale green vapour curled over the cliff-edge, wrapping their ankles, rising to their chests, silencing the distant thunder of the naval guns. William tried to shout, tried to hold onto his precious, burning rage, but his knees buckled softly beneath him. The revolver slipped from his numb fingers into the grass, and they sank down together, swept under by a sudden, fathomless tide of sleep.

Light woke him—not the smoky, reluctant dawn of Staffordshire, nor the feverish green glare of the night before, but a clear, washed blue that seemed to pour straight through his eyelids into his brain. William lay still in the damp grass, listening. A lark was singing somewhere high above the cliffs, its notes dropping like bright coins through the stillness. He sat up, blinking, and felt an astonishing lightness in his chest, as though a heavy, rusted lock had snapped open in the dark. Beside his knee, half-hidden in a tuft of clover, lay the revolver. He looked at it for a long time. How small it was, how pitifully foolish—a lump of scrap iron, as meaningless as a child’s discarded wooden sword. A few paces away, Edward Verrall was stirring, pushing himself up on one elbow and staring at his own pale hands with quiet, bewildered wonder. Further off, Nettie sat with her knees drawn up, her hair unbound and tangled with grass, gazing out toward the Channel. William followed her eyes. Down in the bay, the great grey warships rode at anchor, motionless, their funnels clean of smoke, their guns pointing harmlessly at the empty horizon. The madness that had driven men to butcher one another, the dark, airless cellar in which he had hoarded his pride and his hatred—all of it had been blown away, leaving only the salt wind and the vast, unclouded sky.

They walked down the winding path toward the shingle together, the three of them, saying very little, for the old words—the words of accusation and possession, of rich and poor—felt clumsy and out of place in this bright, washed morning. When they reached the shore, the tide was coming in, turning the smooth pebbles over with a steady, whispering hiss that seemed to measure out a gentler kind of time. William looked at Nettie. He saw her now not as a prize to be locked in a drawer and guarded against the world, nor as a jagged wound in his vanity, but simply as a woman standing in the sun, her grey eyes clear, the wind lifting a stray lock of hair across her cheek. Beside her, Verrall looked smaller than he had in Staffordshire, stripped of his fine airs, just a young man in a rumpled coat, squinting against the glare off the water. Verrall bent down, picked up a flat grey stone, and sent it skipping across the glassy surface of a rock-pool—one, two, three bright splashes before it sank. He glanced back at William and smiled, a shy, uncertain smile, and William found himself nodding in return. There was no need for grand speeches or dramatic pardons. They stood together on the edge of the sea, listening to the gulls wheel and cry in the clear air, feeling the quiet, ordinary miracle of the day beginning.
