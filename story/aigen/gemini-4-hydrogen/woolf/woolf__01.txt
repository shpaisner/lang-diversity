The smoke hung low over Clayton, a grey woollen fleece tangled among the bottle-kilns, catching the last, bruised light of the afternoon. William Leadford stood by the window, his knuckles white against the chipped sill, watching the soot settle on the cabbages in the yard below. Inside, the kettle hissed—a small, fretful, domestic sound, like his mother’s breathing in the parlour. Everything was small here; everything was pinched, stained with coal-dust and the sourness of cheap vinegar. Down in the street, a boy ran past, his boots clattering on the cobbles, shouting something about the North Sea, about ultimatum and mobilization, the words torn apart by the wind and flung against the brick walls. War, they said. Europe was sliding, heavy and blind, toward the edge. And yet, above the jagged roofs, above the pall of Staffordshire smoke, hung that other thing—the green star, swelling night by night in the pale sky, trailing its faint, luminous veil across the constellations. William pressed his forehead to the cold glass. They talked of battleships and ministries, of Nettie—ah, Nettie!—as if the world were solid, as if the tea-cups on the oilcloth table would sit there forever. He felt the hard, cold weight in his coat pocket, the iron thing he had bought with his hoarded shillings. It was the only real thing left in a world that had grown thin as paper, ready to tear. His fingers closed over the grip, tracing the chequered wood, while the green light strengthened, turning the wet slates of the roofs to the colour of sea-water.

How she had looked in the lane by the canal, her straw hat tilted, the ribbon fluttering against her neck! She had promised—or had she? No, she had only smiled, with that vague, soft yielding that made a man think she was his, until someone brighter came along. Edward Verrall, with his white flannels, his motor-car throbbing at the gate, his voice full of easy, careless laughter that smelled of sunshine and money. They had gone south, to the sea, leaving William to the ledger and the soot. He walked out onto the cinder-path, his boots crunching. Was it love he felt, or only a terrible, scorching humiliation? Both, twisted together like wire. He saw them in his mind’s eye: Nettie’s bare wrist resting on a wicker table, Verrall leaning forward, the white cuff of his shirt gleaming. They were happy; they had forgotten him as one forgets a dull Tuesday. The thought was a needle in his brain. Above him, the comet’s tail seemed to stretch further tonight, a ghostly feather brushed across the dark. Let the ships collide in the Channel, he thought; let the old world crack like a dropped plate. He had his ticket in his waistcoat pocket, third-class to the coast, and the revolver nestled warm against his hip. He would find them where the waves broke, and he would make them look at him, just once, before the sky fell.

The carriage rattled, a rhythm of iron on iron, carrying him south through the darkening shires. Across from him sat a stout man in a bowler hat, his newspaper shaking slightly in his hands as he read the evening dispatches—the mobilization of the reserves, the silence from Berlin, the panic on the Exchange. Beside him, a woman clutched a string bag of apples, staring out at the passing hedgerows with wide, frightened eyes. Nobody looked up at the window-top, where the night was no longer black, but steeped in a strange, translucent emerald, as if the whole of England were sinking beneath deep water. William stared at his own reflection in the glass, ghostly and pale, superimposed over rushing fields and quiet church spires. How absurd they were, these people, trembling over their banks and their borders, while the green flame hung poised above their heads! And yet, was he not just as absurd, clutching his little parcel of vengeance? No—his anger was sharp, a blade he kept honed against his ribs. Nettie had chosen the smooth road, the bright silk, the laughter without thought; she had left him in the dark. The train shrieked, plunging into a tunnel, and for a moment there was only the roar of smoke and cinders, until they burst out again into the eerie, phosphorescent dusk, nearer now to the salt air, nearer to the end. The stout man folded his paper with a sharp, nervous snap, muttering something about the fleet, and the woman sighed, her fingers tightening on the netted string.

The sea at Shaphambury was not blue, nor grey, but a shifting sheet of tarnished silver beneath the comet’s glare. William walked along the shingle, the stones rolling and clicking beneath his worn heels. How quiet the little wooden bungalows were, crouched behind the tamarisk hedges, their windows glowing like amber beads in the greenish gloom. He knew which one; he had watched them from the dunes at dusk—seen Verrall’s tall shadow cross the blind, heard the sudden, bright trill of Nettie’s laugh, light as breaking glass. A terrible stillness lay over the coast, as if the tide itself were hesitating, listening for the distant thunder of guns across the Channel. William crept closer, his breath shallow, the salt stinging his cracked lips. Through the uncurtained French window, he saw them: a white tablecloth, a bowl of peaches, Nettie leaning her chin on her hand, her hair catching the lamplight. She looked so soft, so untouched by the grime of Clayton, by the terror of the newspapers. Rage rose in his throat, bitter as gall. He drew the revolver from his pocket; the metal was cold, slick with the damp sea-mist. He stepped onto the wooden veranda. One step, two. Inside, Verrall turned his head, startled, his glass halfway to his lips. Nettie’s eyes widened. And then, before William could lift his arm, before the hammer could fall, the air outside seemed to shiver and part.

It was not a sound, though for an instant he thought he heard a vast, soft sigh, like wind passing over miles of ripe wheat. A mist, pale green and faintly sweet, was drifting in from the sea, curling around the veranda posts, spilling over the threshold. William tried to shout, tried to steady the trembling barrel, but his hand felt suddenly heavy, as though filled with warm sand. The revolver slipped from his fingers and struck the boards with a dull, meaningless thud. Through the glass, he saw Verrall sink gently back into his chair, his eyelids fluttering shut; he saw Nettie bow her head onto her folded arms beside the bowl of peaches, as if overtaken by a sudden, innocent weariness. Far out in the dark, beyond the horizon where the great grey battleships lay steaming toward one another, the same cool vapour must be drifting across the decks, silencing the bugles, softening the iron wheels of the guns. William sank to his knees on the rough wood. There was no pain, no anger left—only an immense, unfolding quiet, like sinking into deep, clear water. The scent of it was like bruised mint and rain on hot stone, washing away the coal-smoke that had clung to his coat for years, washing away the sourness of his own heart. His cheek touched the damp planks; the sea whispered below, slow and rhythmic, and he let go. The green light pulsed once, twice, bathing the tamarisk leaves, the shingle, the sleeping faces inside the room, and then everything dissolved into a dreamless, breathless dark.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world had been washed to the bone. A lark was singing somewhere high above the dunes—a clear, silver thread of sound spinning down through air so pure it made his chest ache with wonder. He sat up slowly, looking at his own hands, stained still with ink and Clayton soot, yet they seemed to belong to a stranger, or to someone he had known long ago in a fever. Beside him lay the revolver, absurd and small, a dark little toy of iron that meant nothing at all. He stood up, brushing the sand from his knees, and looked through the open window. Nettie and Verrall were waking too. She lifted her head, blinking in the bright morning light, and Verrall looked across the table at her, not with triumph, not with careless pride, but with a quiet, bewildered tenderness. When Nettie saw William standing on the veranda, she did not shrink back; she did not scream. She simply rose and came to the door, her eyes clear and unclouded, as if they had both just woken from the same long, troubled night. "William," she said, and her voice had no fear in it, only a gentle, wondering recognition. He looked past her, out over the sparkling sea where no smoke of warships stained the sky, and felt no bitterness, no claim upon her—only a vast, luminous pity for what they had been, and a quiet, boundless joy in the morning.
