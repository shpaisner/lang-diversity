The soot settled on the windowsill like black snow, soft and greasy, and William Leadford drew his finger through it, leaving a pale grey scar. Beyond the glass, the kilns of Clayton smoked into a sky already bruised with twilight—a low, sulphurous ceiling that pressed down upon the red-brick terraces, the cobbles, the endless clatter of clogs. His mother’s voice rose from the scullery, thin as a cracked plate, complaining of the price of lard, of the damp in her knees; and William’s heart, tight and hot as a pebble in a sling, knocked against his ribs. Nettie was gone. She had taken her little straw hat, her laugh that was like water spilled on warm stone, and she had stepped into Verrall’s motor-car—Verrall with his pale flannel trousers, his easy, lounging insolence, his pockets lined with the rent of half the county. How cleanly the rich stole! They did not even know they were thieving; they merely reached out a manicured hand and plucked what a poor man had starved to keep. William looked up, past the belching stacks, past the jagged slate roofs. There it hung—a smudge of green fire, no larger than a farthing, yet steady as a lighthouse in the murk. The newspapers called it a wanderer, a harmless visitor of ice and gas, though the men at the corner whispered of omens, of the German fleet steaming through the grey North Sea. Let them sink one another, William thought, his jaw aching; let the whole rotten edifice of iron and velvet crack down the middle.

The third-class carriage smelled of damp wool, stale tobacco, and the sweat of clerks going south. All along the bench, newspapers rustled like dry reeds in a ditch—Ultimatum, Mobilisation, The Dreadnoughts at Kiel—the black type jumping beneath the dim oil lamp as the train rattled over the points. A commercial traveller in a bowler hat tapped the page with a blunt fingernail, explaining to no one in particular how the Chancellor must yield by Tuesday, or else the guns would speak. William pressed his forehead against the cold pane. Out there in the rushing dark, hedges and ditches fled backward, and above them the green star had grown into a pale, luminous feather, brushing the clouds. His right hand lay inside his coat pocket, his fingers curled around the ribbed butt of the revolver. Five shillings it had cost him at the pawnshop in Swetnam, its barrel dull as lead, its chambers waiting. It was absurd, he knew, to carry this small, secret war through a country humming with armies; absurd to care for one girl’s faithlessness when empires were sliding like glaciers toward the sea. Yet the two things were woven from the same coarse thread. The great world was a bully in broadcloth, and Verrall was its handsome son, smiling over his wine while men like William coughed their lungs out in the clay-pits. The train shrieked for a tunnel, plunging them into roaring blackness, and William closed his eyes, seeing only Nettie’s white throat as she turned away from him at the garden gate.

At Shaphambury, the sea was never entirely silent; even behind the tall sash windows of the Grand Hotel, it breathed in and out, a long, shushing sigh over the shingle. Nettie stood by the curtain, watching the light drain from the water. It was not the honest blue of afternoon, nor the honest black of night, but a strange, translucent emerald, as if the whole bay had been poured into a glass bottle and held up to a lamp. Behind her, Verrall struck a match. The little flare of yellow caught the silver on the tea-tray, the folded crispness of the Times, the smooth, unlined plane of his cheek. "Come away from the window, darling," he murmured, his voice lazy, affectionate, already a little bored. "You'll catch a chill." She did not move. Only three days ago she had thought that to sit in a room with heavy velvet curtains, to hear the waiter’s soft tread on the carpet, would be to enter heaven. And Verrall was kind—oh, he was wonderfully kind, tossing sovereigns to porters as if they were apple cores, kissing her wrists as though she were made of porcelain. Yet she felt curiously weightless, like a leaf floating on a millpond before the sluice opens. In the square below, a newsboy was shouting something hoarse and broken; men in evening dress paused on the pavement to buy sheets, their white shirt-fronts gleaming in the greenish dusk. She thought of William’s fierce, thin face, his ink-stained knuckles, the way he held her arm too tightly when he was angry. A shiver ran down her spine, and she wondered, looking at the great green fan spreading across the sky, whether anyone on earth was truly safe tonight.

He found them on the cliff path above the town, where the turf was short and springy with sea-thrift. Dusk had deepened into a luminous, unnatural twilight; the comet now filled half the northern sky, a vast, silent plume of sea-green fire that turned the chalk cliffs to bone and the sea beneath to beaten pewter. Down in the harbour, the signal lamps of a cruiser were winking frantically—dot, dash, dot—spelling out the ruin of Europe. William stepped from the shadow of the gorse. He heard Nettie’s sharp intake of breath, saw Verrall’s tall figure stiffen, the pale blur of his face turning toward the intruder. "Leadford," Verrall said, and even now there was that infuriating, effortless composure in his tone, as if William were a beggar asking the time. "What the devil are you doing here?" William did not answer with words; his throat was dry as ash. He drew the revolver from his pocket, the little click of the hammer sounding absurdly loud against the wash of the waves. Nettie cried out, stepping between them, her hands fluttering like startled birds. "Will! Don't—please—" Then the wind changed. It was not a gust from the sea, but a sudden, immense exhalation, warm and faintly sweet, like bruised thyme or the air before summer thunder. A greenish mist seemed to curl around their ankles, rising softly from the grass. William’s arm grew heavy—so terribly heavy—and the pistol slipped from his numb fingers into the clover. Across the bay, the cruiser’s winking light blurred into a halo. The anger that had burned in his chest for months went out like a candle in a cellar, and he sank to his knees, laying his cheek against the cool, sweet earth as the world folded its eyes.

Morning did not break; it simply stood there, clear as spring water. William opened his eyes and lay for a long moment watching a beetle climb a blade of fescue. How astonishingly green the grass was! Not the grime-choked green of Staffordshire, nor the feverish emerald of last night’s sky, but a pure, washed colour, trembling with dew. Above him, a lark was pouring out its song—note after bright note, falling like coins into a well. He sat up, brushing a dry clover-head from his sleeve, and waited for the familiar weight to drop back into his ribs: the hatred, the humiliation, the knotted fist of his own poverty. Nothing came. His mind felt like a room whose windows had been flung wide after years of sickness, the stale curtains torn down, the floorboards scrubbed white by the sun. Ten paces away, Verrall was sitting up on the turf, his hair tousled, his collar unbuttoned, staring at his own hands with an expression of mild, bewildered wonder. Beside him, Nettie stirred, raising her head from the grass, her straw hat crushed flat beneath her shoulder. William looked at them, and for the first time in his life he saw them without the mist of his own pride between them. Verrall was no longer a gilded tyrant, nor Nettie a prize to be hoarded or broken; they were simply two fragile, breathing creatures, waking in the same sunlight as himself. Nearby, half-hidden in the weeds, lay the little iron revolver, rusted already with the night’s dew, looking as foolish and archaic as a flint arrowhead. William rose, walked over to Verrall, and held out his hand to help him up.

They walked down the winding chalk path toward the town together, three silhouettes against the dazzling sea, saying very little at first. The air had a crisp, crystalline lightness, as though all the smoke of the Midlands and all the gunpowder of the arsenals had been rinsed from the atmosphere. At the edge of the promenade, a bundle of morning newspapers lay tied with twine outside a shuttered shop; the black headlines—WAR DECLARED, FLEETS ENGAGE—stared up at the empty street, meaningless as proclamations from a vanished kingdom. Down by the breakwater, a few townspeople stood in small, quiet knots, not shouting or weeping, only looking out at the horizon where the cruiser rode at anchor, her guns silent, her sailors leaning idly along the rail in the morning sun. Nettie stopped by the low stone wall and rested her elbows upon it, looking down at the clear green tide sliding over the pebbles. "We were so small," she said softly, not to either of them in particular, her voice steady and unafraid. "How we tore at one another in the dark." Verrall stood beside her, his fingers lightly touching the stone, his old, protective irony entirely gone; he looked younger, almost shy, as if learning the names of things for the first time. William leaned against the wall beside them, feeling the warmth of the sun soak through his shabby coat into his shoulders. He thought of his mother in the damp scullery, of the kilns of Clayton, of the long, grey years that had seemed a prison. They would go back, he knew, to mend what had been broken—not with the old fury of ownership, nor the dull submission of the beaten, but as neighbours in an open house. The sea broke gently against the stones below, again and again, washing the shingle clean, while high above them the pale ghost of the comet faded into the boundless blue of day.
