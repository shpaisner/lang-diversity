The soot settled on the windowsill, fine as powdered ink, and beneath it the geraniums in their cracked pots seemed to gasp for a sky that was never quite blue. William Leadford stood by the table, his knuckles raw from the ironworks, staring at the square of cheap notepaper until the loops of Nettie’s writing blurred into the pattern of the oilcloth. She was gone. Gone with Edward Verrall—Verrall of the great house on the hill, whose boots never knew the red mud of Clayton, whose voice had that smooth, indolent fall like water over marble. In the grate, his mother’s kettle hissed, a small, patient sound of poverty enduring itself; outside, the Potteries breathed their perpetual grey sigh over the rows of identical brick. And yet, William thought, pressing his thumb against the edge of the table, it was not merely that she had gone, nor that Verrall had taken her as one plucks a briar rose from a hedge without noticing the thorns. It was the vast, indifferent weight of things—the coal dust in his lungs, the shilling short in his week’s wages, the way the world arranged itself into terraces and hovels, and expected him to touch his cap to the arrangement. Above the chimney stacks, where the evening was bruising to a deep violet, a pale green smudge hung in the air, faint as a thumbprint on glass. Nobody in the street looked up at it. They were too busy counting ha’pence, or coughing, or watching the dusk thicken over the canal. But William saw it, cold and luminous, and felt his own small, furious heart beat against his ribs like a trapped bird.

By Tuesday the newspapers were screaming, black ink smeared across the damp sheets that the boys waved at the corner by the Red Lion. Ultimatum, the headlines cried; Dreadnoughts steaming out of Portsmouth; the German fleet massing in the grey wash of the North Sea. Men gathered under the gas lamps, their faces hollowed by the flickering yellow light, talking of honour and Empire with the same bewildered heat they brought to a dogfight. William walked among them with the revolver heavy in his coat pocket, its cold snout knocking against his hip-bone at every stride. Five pounds it had cost him—all the savings scraped together for a parlour suite he would never buy, for curtains Nettie would never hem. How absurd it was, he thought, catching his reflection in the draper’s darkened window, this little man in a frayed collar carrying death in his serge pocket while emperors shuffled armies across the map of Europe! Yet the two things were woven from the same coarse thread. The world was a boiler stoked too high, trembling in its rivets, ready to burst; and he was a spark inside it, hot and blind. He saw Nettie’s throat, white against the dark green of the lanes behind the Manor; he heard Verrall’s laugh, careless and bright as a sovereign spun on a counter. Let them have their war, he muttered, turning down toward the station where the trains clanked and hissed in the dark. Let the whole rotten edifice of banks and ironworks and drawing rooms come down in ash. He had his ticket in his waistcoat, and his ticket was to the sea.

The sea was not like Staffordshire; it had no memory of soot or wages. At Shaphambury the air tasted of salt and bruised thyme, and the cliffs fell away in great chalky folds toward a tide that washed the shingle with a slow, indifferent hush. William crouched behind a clump of gorse on the headland, his boots white with dust, watching the terrace of the little hotel below. There they were. Even at this distance, he knew the tilt of Nettie’s straw hat, the way she rested her hand on the balustrade as if the whole bright afternoon belonged to her. Beside her, Verrall leaned back in a wicker chair, white-flannelled, turning the pages of a newspaper—no doubt reading of the ships, of the mobilisation, of the great storm gathering beyond the Channel—and then he looked up and said something that made Nettie laugh. The sound floated up the cliff, thin and silver, and pierced William like a needle. How could she laugh, when his own chest felt stuffed with cinders? Above the horizon, though the sun had scarcely set, the green star had swollen into a pale, trailing plume, casting a strange, unearthly sheen upon the water, as though someone had spilled emerald oil across the waves. The gulls wheeled through that eerie twilight, crying sharply, and along the esplanade a lamplighter paused, his pole lifted, staring out to sea. In a moment, William told himself, he would walk down the chalk path. He would stand between the wicker chairs. He would show them that a man from the Potbanks could not be brushed aside like a beetle from a sleeve.

Night came not with darkness, but with a luminous, creeping pallor. William descended the path between the tamarisks, his hand buried in his pocket, his fingers curled tight around the chequered grip. On the terrace, the Chinese lanterns had not been lit; Nettie and Verrall stood together by the low stone wall, looking out at the eastern sky, where the comet now hung like a vast, incandescent lily unfurling its petals over the sea. She wore a shawl over her shoulders, and Verrall’s arm was lightly around her waist. “Nettie,” William said. His voice sounded thin, scraped raw by the train journey and the long hours in the gorse. She turned, her hand flying to her mouth, her eyes widening in the greenish gloom; Verrall stepped half a pace forward, his jaw tightening, yet even in his face there was a strange, slack bewilderment, as if he could scarcely remember why one man should hate another. William drew the pistol. The metal gleamed pale jade in the comet’s light. He meant to speak—to utter all the choked, bitter sentences he had rehearsed between Clayton and the coast—but a sudden hush had fallen over the world. The tide seemed to pause between one wave and the next. From the east rolled a faint, sweet mist, smelling not of salt or coal, but of crushed leaves after rain, cool and penetrating. It touched his forehead; it filled his lungs. The fury inside him, that tight, blackened knot he had carried for months, seemed to loosen and uncoil like smoke in open air. His arm grew heavy, impossibly heavy; the pistol slipped from his fingers and struck the gravel with a dull, meaningless clack as the green sleep washed over them all.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was up, and the world looked as though it had been washed in cold spring water. Every blade of grass on the cliff, every pebble on the terrace, stood out with an astonishing, quiet distinctness, free of shadow, free of haze. He sat up slowly, expecting the familiar ache in his temples, the sour weight of yesterday’s despair; instead, there was only lightness, a curious transparency of mind, as if someone had thrown open all the windows in a shuttered room. Beside his knee lay the revolver. He picked it up by the barrel, turning the absurd contraption of springs and lead over in his palm. What a strange, cramped little dream he had been living in! He saw himself back in the Staffordshire kitchen, nursing his grievance like a sore tooth, confusing love with ownership, confusing his own bruised pride with the destiny of the world. How small it all seemed, and how pitiful—not wicked, merely blind, like a moth beating against a dirty pane. Down by the water’s edge, two figures were walking slowly along the wet sand. He knew them at once, yet the sight brought no spasm to his throat, no heat behind his eyes. Behind them, the sea lay flat and bright as beaten silver, and far out, a steamship moved peacefully across the horizon, trailing no black plume of war, only a thin white wake. William set the revolver down upon the stone wall—someone would throw it in the sea, or melt it into a doorknob—and walked down the steps toward the shore.

They heard his footsteps on the shingle and turned, neither starting back nor hastening to speak, for the old haste, the old defensive armour, had fallen away in the night. Nettie’s face was pale and very clear in the morning light; she looked at William with a deep, unhurried tenderness that had nothing in it of pity, and nothing of fear. “William,” she said, and held out both her hands. He took them, feeling the warmth of her skin, marveling at how simple it was to stand here without demanding anything, without wishing to bruise or possess. Verrall stood a little apart, his white flannels crumpled from the dew, looking out toward the town where the church bells had begun to ring—not an alarm, not a summons to battle, but a slow, bewildered pealing that drifted across the roofs. “The postmaster came down just now,” Verrall said quietly, his voice stripped of its old, careless ring, sounding merely human, merely young. “The telegrams have stopped. The fleets are turning back in the Channel. Nobody knows quite how to begin the old life again.” They walked together along the margin of the tide, where the foam hissed softly around their boots and sank into the sand. William thought of his mother’s kitchen, of the red brick rows of Clayton, of the smoke that would surely lift now, as men looked up from their ledgers and their lathes into this extraordinary morning. Nothing was settled—not Nettie, not Verrall, not the long work of rebuilding a soot-stained world—yet as the light broadened over the sea, binding the three of them in one quiet, luminous space, it seemed to William that for the first time in his life he was truly awake.
