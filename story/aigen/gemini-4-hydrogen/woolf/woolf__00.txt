The smoke of the Five Towns hung low, a greasy pallor smeared across the afternoon, and William Leadford walked through it with his hands clenched deep in his pockets, feeling the cold, blunt nose of the revolver against his knuckles. Everything was grit and cinder; the very air tasted of sulphur and boiled cabbage, of his mother’s anxious sighing in the front room, of the strike that had left the kilns cold and the men lounging at the street corners with hollow, furious eyes. And Nettie—Nettie had gone. She had taken her straw hat with the blue ribbon, she had folded her little gloves, and she had stepped out of this blackened valley into the soft, cushioned world of Edward Verrall. It was intolerable, William thought, kicking a loose piece of slag into the gutter; it was an outrage written across the whole filthy sky. For what was Verrall? A young gentleman with soft hands and a motor-car, who had looked at Nettie across the garden wall as one looks at a ripe peach, and plucked her. While he, William, had only his books, his ragged socialism, his fierce, starving love. Above the jagged silhouette of the bottle-ovens, the afternoon was already darkening, though it was scarcely four o’clock, and there, hanging just above the railway embankment, was the comet. It was no longer a mere smudge for astronomers to squabble over in the penny papers; it was a pale, greenish eye, cold and indifferent, watching the soot drift over Staffordshire.

Three hundred miles away, where the chalk cliffs crumbled into a silver-grey sea, Nettie sat by the open window of the bungalow and watched the tide draw back across the shingle. The room smelled of lavender and freshly ironed linen, a clean, unhurried scent that still surprised her every morning when she woke. On the veranda outside, Edward was tapping his cigarette against a saucer, humming a little tune from a London revue, his white flannel trousers gleaming in the queer, washed light. He was so easy, so wonderfully light upon the earth; nothing clung to him, neither soot nor guilt nor the heavy, suffocating dread of tomorrow. Yet, as Nettie smoothed the silk of her skirt over her knees, a sudden shiver passed through her, like a cold draught under a door. She thought of William’s dark, narrow face, the way his eyebrows drew together when he spoke of justice, of the rich, of the coming smash. She had pitied him, and then she had feared him, and then, when Edward’s motor-car had hummed softly at the end of the lane, she had simply run. Was it wrong? The sea made a long, shushing sound upon the stones, indifferent as time itself. Looking up, she saw the strange green star brightening above the horizon, casting a faint, uncanny sheen across the white tablecloth, across Edward’s careless hand, making even this safe, bright room seem fragile as a blown glass bubble.

Meanwhile, the newspapers rustled in every railway carriage from Euston to the coast, crisp and frantic with the news of war. Men in bowler hats snapped their pages, leaning forward to tap one another on the knee—ultimatums, they said; the North Sea; the fleet mobilized at Spithead. A terrible, jerky excitement seemed to have seized the whole island, like the rattling of a loose window in a rising gale. William, sitting in the corner of a third-class carriage with his collar turned up, stared out at the flying hedgerows and felt the world’s madness beating in unison with his own. Let it burn, he thought bitterly; let the ironclads smash one another to scrap iron in the grey water; let the whole rotten edifice of masters and clerks, of Verralls and Leadfords, come crashing down! Outside, the twilight was deepening into something altogether unnatural. A vast, luminous fan of emerald vapor now spanned half the heavens, dimming the familiar constellations, bathing the signal-boxes, the quiet pastures, the distant spires of cathedral towns in a cold, phosphor glow. In Whitehall, clerks hurried down stone corridors with bundles of telegrams; in Berlin, boots rang upon the cobbles. The telegraph wires hummed beside the track, carrying panic from shire to shire, and in the third-class carriage, a stout woman beside William crossed herself and whispered that it was the end of the world, while the green light pooled like water upon the floorboards at their feet.

He found them at dusk, behind a hedge of tamarisk where the salt wind rattled the dry leaves. Through the gap, the bungalow lawn looked like a stage set beneath an eerie lime-light: the grass was an unearthly, vivid jade, and Nettie’s white dress seemed to float above it, ghostly and luminous. Edward Verrall was laughing, tossing a pebble from hand to hand, his face upturned toward that prodigious green glory in the sky. A sudden, blinding rage surged up in William’s throat, choking him. They were so clean, so untouched, playing their little games while the world choked on coal-dust and marched to slaughter! He pushed through the hedge, the twigs snapping loudly, and stepped onto the grass. Nettie gasped, her hand flying to her mouth; Verrall stiffened, stepping instinctively in front of her. "Leadford," Verrall said, his voice tight, trying to sound like a gentleman even here, at the end of things. William drew the revolver; the metal felt greasy and hot in his palm. He raised it, aiming at the smooth, white space of Verrall’s shirt-front, wanting to tear a hole in his certainty, in Nettie’s betrayal, in the whole cruel order of things. But as his finger tightened on the trigger, the wind stopped dead. A heavy, sweet, unfamiliar fragrance—like crushed fern and cold rain—rolled down from the darkening sky, and the green light thickened, wrapping them all in a sudden, impenetrable silence.

It came softly, without thunder, without the crack of doom—only a vast, emerald mist drifting across the face of the earth, settling into every hollow and crevice. Upon the bungalow lawn, the revolver slipped from William’s unresisting fingers and vanished into the damp grass; he sank to his knees, and beside him, Nettie and Verrall sank too, folded together into the same deep, merciful tide. Out in the North Sea, where the great grey warships lay with their searchlights probing the dark and their gunners waiting by the breeches, the green vapor crept over the decks, and men leaned quietly against the cold steel rails and closed their eyes. In Staffordshire, the soot-blackened streets grew still; William’s mother, sitting by a dying grate with her shawl pulled tight, let her head fall gently back against the antimacassar, her anxious brow smoothing out as if a cool hand had passed across it. In London, the printing presses slowed and stopped; the shouting newsboys sank down upon the steps of St. Paul’s; the diplomats slept with their pens still resting upon their treaties. Time, which had been rushing forward like an express train toward a broken bridge, simply uncoupled its carriages and came to a halt. All night the green cloud lay upon the sleeping world, washing through the cramped, angry chambers of the human mind, dissolving the tiny, bitter knots of pride and terror, of ownership and envy, until nothing remained beneath the stars but a vast, breathing stillness.

When William opened his eyes, the sun was already high, and the world was utterly, astonishingly clear. He lay upon his back in the wet grass, looking up at a sky of such pure, translucent blue that it seemed he had never truly seen the heavens before, only a smudged imitation of them. Beside his outstretched hand lay the revolver; he looked at it with a faint, puzzled curiosity, as one might look at a strange beetle or a piece of broken crockery, wondering why any creature should ever have carried so heavy and ugly a thing. He sat up. A few paces away, Edward Verrall was sitting with his knees drawn up, staring out toward the sparkling, empty sea, his white flannels stained with earth and dew. Nearer still, Nettie was rising to her feet, brushing a blade of grass from her sleeve, her face pale and serene, washed clean of yesterday’s terror. Their eyes met—William’s, Nettie’s, Edward’s—and for a long moment no one spoke. There was nothing to forgive, nothing to possess; the fierce, narrow walls that had divided them, built of money and misery and pride, had melted away in the night like frost under a warm sun. "Are you hurt?" Verrall asked, his voice quiet and entirely simple, stripped of its old, careless veneer. "No," William said, rising to his feet, feeling the salt air rush into his lungs like cold water. "No. Look at the morning." And together, the three of them stood upon the cliff’s edge, watching the gulls wheel through the luminous air, while far away, across the quiet sea, the great ships turned harmlessly toward home.
