In those final, fevered weeks before the Great Change, the industrial blight of Staffordshire seemed to fester beneath a sky grown subtly and terribly inimical. I was then a mere clerk of negligible means, William Leadford by name, condemned to drudge amid the soot-choked potteries and the cyclopean slag-heaps of Clayton, where the blast furnaces belched their sulphurous prayers to an indifferent heaven. Poverty had etched its bitter geometry upon my soul, yet I had clung to one fragile, luminous delusion: the affection of Nettie Stuart. Now, even that meagre beacon was guttering out in the gathering gloom. Everywhere, the daily journals shrieked of mobilization, of ultimatums exchanged between crumbling empires, and of Teutonic armadas massing in the grey North Sea. Europe was sliding with dreadful, somnambulistic certainty into an abyss of mechanized slaughter. Yet it was not the clamour of earthly generals that chilled my marrow as I trudged homeward through the cindered lanes, but the silent, bloated thing hanging in the nocturnal zenith. Night by night, the Comet swelled—a monstrous, gaseous leviathan trailing a tail of sickly, phosphorescent verdigris across the constellations. Its light was neither solar nor lunar, but an alien, bilious luminescence that bleached the brick terraces into the semblance of a forgotten necropolis. Even the blackened canals, sluggish with industrial filth, seemed to mirror that baleful emerald eye as though the earth itself were infected by its gaze. While my fellow townsmen brawled in the taverns over phantom borders and national honour, I stared upward in nameless dread, sensing that the universe was preparing some vast, unutterable mockery of our petty human furies.

The madness that gripped the continent soon found its mirror in the narrow, squalid chambers of my own brain. On a Tuesday thick with coal-smoke and the distant rumour of declared war, I learned that Nettie had fled. She had abandoned our humble promise for Edward Verrall, a scion of the local gentry whose insolent motor-car had long haunted our grimy streets like an emissary from some gilded, unattainable sphere. In my pocket, the crumpled note she had left behind burned like a scrap of poisoned parchment, its hasty ink-strokes mocking my years of patient, unrewarded toil. A cold, reptilian fury coiled within my breast, displacing all gentler memories. I did not weep; rather, I descended into a subterranean cellar of the mind where only vengeance held sway. From a pawnbroker in a crooked alley near the canal—a withered creature who muttered incessantly of the green star's malignity—I purchased a heavy revolver and a box of cartridges. The metal felt obscenely cold against my palm, a talisman of definitive ruin. All around me, the townsfolk were succumbing to a parallel delirium; crowds marched beneath flickering gas-lamps, singing bloodthirsty anthems and waving flags, their faces ghastly in the emerald sheen of the approaching celestial visitor. They sought the blood of foreigners across the Channel, just as I sought the blood of Verrall and the faithless girl who had spurned my devotion. Boarding the midnight train southward, I sat in a corner of the rattling carriage, clutching my hidden weapon while the English countryside slipped past in eerie, greenish silhouette, every gnarled oak and hedgerow twisted into shapes of silent accusation.

By the following evening I had tracked the fugitives to Shaphambury, a bleak coastal settlement where the chalk cliffs drop sheer into the churning waters of the Channel. Here, the atmosphere was heavy with a peculiar, electric stagnation, as though the very air awaited a cataclysmic verdict. The Comet now dominated half the firmament, a terrifying, nebulous vortex whose emerald tendrils seemed to brush the horizon. Beneath its necrotic glare, the sea heaved like a cauldron of dark oil, and far out upon that troubled expanse, I beheld the silhouettes of dreadnoughts—great iron leviathans gliding without lights toward some appointed rendezvous of mutual annihilation. Earlier that afternoon, a distant, rhythmic throbbing had shaken the shingle, the unmistakable thunder of naval artillery heralding the outbreak of the European war. Yet the townspeople along the esplanade scarce heeded the gunfire; they stood in silent, huddled knots, staring upward with slack jaws at the monstrous green apparition overhead. I moved among them like a ghost, my hand locked upon the butt of the revolver in my coat pocket. My mind was a single, jagged point of malice. I found their trail at a secluded bungalow perched near the cliff's edge, its whitewashed walls stained a ghostly chartreuse by the celestial light. Through a gap in the tamarisk hedge, I saw Verrall's silhouette crossing a lit window, and a tremor of ecstatic hatred shook my frame. Somewhere behind those drawn curtains, Nettie was laughing or whispering, utterly oblivious to the doom—both earthly and celestial—that now converged upon that solitary dwelling.

I crept through the damp garden, my boots sinking into the soft turf, until I stood before the open French windows of the parlour. Inside, a single oil-lamp burned, though its warm amber flame seemed pitifully overwhelmed by the encroaching green radiance pouring in from the sea. Nettie stood beside the hearth, her face pale and strangely drawn, while Verrall paced the carpet with a newspaper crumpled in his fist, speaking in low, agitated tones of the coastal bombardment. Stepping across the threshold, I levelled the revolver at his chest. Nettie's stifled cry shattered the stillness, and Verrall froze, his aristocratic composure dissolving into sudden, animal terror. "You have stolen all that was mine," I hissed, my voice scarcely recognizable as my own, ragged and hollow as wind through a tomb. My finger tightened upon the trigger, eager to add two more corpses to the millions already consecrated to Europe's madness. For a single, suspended heartbeat, we three stood motionless as sculpted figures in a frieze, illuminated only by that descending, otherworldly luminescence. Yet even as the hammer began to rise, a soundless concussion shook the foundations of the earth. The sky beyond the window erupted—not with flame, but with a blinding, cascading veil of emerald vapour that spilled downward from the stratosphere like a ruptured sea. A rushing wind, preternaturally cold and redolent of ozone and crushed herbs, swept through the room, extinguishing the oil-lamp in an instant. Far out in the Channel, the distant booming of the dreadnoughts ceased with abrupt, uncanny finality.

I attempted to fire, yet my muscles seemed encased in lead, and my lungs involuntarily drank deep of the descending green miasma. A sensation of indescribable, terrifying strangeness seized my consciousness—not the agony of suffocation, as I had half expected, but an overwhelming, cosmic vertigo. The walls of the bungalow seemed to dissolve into transparent geometry, and I collapsed upon the floorboards beside Verrall and Nettie, who had likewise crumpled into motionless heaps. In that profound, unnatural stupor, my mind was laid bare to the vast, indifferent gulfs of space. I perceived, with a clarity so acute it bordered on madness, the utter, laughable squalor of human existence. I saw the empires of Europe—their frantic generals, their smoking factories, their blood-soaked trenches—as mere anthills squabbling in the mud beneath an infinite, alien cosmos. And my own consuming jealousy, my murderous pilgrimage from the grime of Staffordshire, appeared to me as a hideous, ape-like tic, a grotesque remnant of bestial antiquity. The green vapour coursed through my veins like a frigid, purifying ichor, scouring away the ancient, ancestral fevers of the blood. There was no warmth in this revelation, no benevolent deity whispering of forgiveness; rather, it was the cold, absolute illumination of an alien chemistry that had casually, by sheer cosmic accident, rewritten the frantic neurology of mankind. Outside, the wind died into a breathless, crystalline hush, as though the entire globe lay embalmed within an emerald sphere of dreamless sleep, suspended far from the reach of its own ancient, self-inflicted torments.

When at length I opened my eyes, the monstrous comet had passed beyond the rim of the world, leaving behind an atmosphere of preternatural, crystalline purity. A pale dawn was breaking over the Channel, and the great ironclad fleets lay silent and motionless upon the glassy water, their guns forever cooled. I rose slowly to my feet, amazed to find my limbs light and my brain devoid of its lifelong, festering bitterness. Beside the hearth, Verrall and Nettie were stirring, waking as I had woken from a century of dark, brutish slumber. When our eyes met, there was neither hatred in my gaze nor terror in theirs, only a solemn, humbled recognition of our shared fragility. I walked to the window and let the heavy revolver drop harmlessly into the dew-drenched grass outside—a relic of an extinct, barbaric aeon. Nettie approached me, and though I knew she would never be mine in the old, possessive sense, the knowledge brought no pang, only a quiet, spacious melancholy. Soon we would return to Staffordshire, where the blast furnaces stood cold and the chimneys no longer fouled the heavens, to join the bewildered remnants of humanity in building a saner, quieter world. Yet as I looked up at the fading stars, I could not repress a shiver of cosmic awe; for we had not conquered our own savagery, but had merely been tamed, like beasts in a cage, by the indifferent breath of a wandering star. And in that vast, unfamiliar silence, we stood together upon the threshold of an uncharted age, forever estranged from the blood-stained gods of our ancestors.
