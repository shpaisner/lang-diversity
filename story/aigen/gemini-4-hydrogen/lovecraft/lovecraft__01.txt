In the soot-choked abysses of Staffordshire, where the monstrous brick kilns belch their eternal, sulfurous incense into a weeping sky, I first felt the creeping madness of the heavens. My name is William Leadford, a creature of meager wage and bitter blood, condemned to toil among the clattering looms and grime-encrusted hovels of the Four Towns. Yet it was not merely the crushing weight of penury that soured my soul during those final, fevered weeks of the old world; it was the hideous certainty of Nettie’s apostasy. For months I had clung to her pale, fragile beauty as a drowning man clings to a splintered spar, only to learn that she had fled into the gilded embrace of Verrall—that decadent scion of the manor-house, whose very name tasted of aristocratic rot. And all the while, above the jagged silhouettes of the pit-heads, the Comet waxed in nocturnal splendor. At first it had been a mere pinprick of sickly, necrotic jade among the constellations, ignored by all save the wild-eyed astronomers. Soon, however, it uncoiled across the zenith like a luminescent serpent from the outer gulfs, casting a faint, corpselike pallor upon the slag-heaps. Looking upward from my squalid attic, I felt a nameless, primordial dread—a whisper in the blood that this celestial intruder was no mere wandering stone, but the herald of an ancient, uncomprehending doom. My aging mother coughed her slow, rhythmic litany in the chamber below, oblivious to the green shadow creeping across her counterpane, while I paced the floorboards in a delirium of wounded pride and cosmic foreboding.

By the time I resolved upon pursuit, the whole of Europe had succumbed to a parallel, daemoniac frenzy. As I walked the narrow, cobbled thoroughfares of Clayton, the very air seemed charged with an electric, miasmic hysteria. Newsboys shrieked of ultimatums and mobilized legions; in the fetid taverns, drunken colliers pounded their tankards and roared for Teutonic blood, their eyes reflecting the jaundiced emerald glow that now pierced even the thickest coal-smoke of midday. It was as though the approaching star had stirred some dormant, atavistic sediment at the bottom of the human psyche, dragging civilization backward toward the primal mire. I was no exception to this universal contagion. A cold, murderous clarity possessed me, masquerading as righteous vengeance. With the last of my hoarded shillings—money meant for winter coal and bread—I entered a shadowy pawnbroker’s den and purchased a heavy, six-chambered revolver. The oily iron felt strangely warm against my palm, like a living, venomous thing. I cleaned its cylinder with trembling, soot-stained fingers, picturing Verrall’s arrogant throat and Nettie’s treacherous tears. Let the kings and ministers hurl their ironclads against one another in the North Sea; my own private Armageddon awaited me upon the southern coast, whither the fugitives had fled. I boarded the midnight train at the junction, clutching the weapon in my coat pocket, while the monstrous green plume in the sky seemed to lean downward, watching my infinitesimal, hate-blinded journey with cold, geometric malice.

The railway journey toward the sea was a veritable phantasmagoria of collective terror. Our carriages were packed to suffocation with pale, sweating refugees fleeing nebulous rumors of continental invasion, alongside hollow-eyed youths in khaki marching toward the waiting charnel-houses of France. Beyond the grime-streaked glass, the English countryside lay drowned in an unnatural, phosphorescent twilight. The comet now usurped half the firmament, its feathery, venomous tail sweeping across the stars like the shroud of a dead god, bathing hedgerows, church spires, and sleeping hamlets in a uniform, ghastly verdancy. When at length I descended at the desolate coastal station of Shaphambury, the night air struck me with a peculiar, metallic tang, heavy with ozone and the distant stench of cordite. Out beyond the jagged shingle, the North Sea heaved beneath the green glare like a cauldron of oil. Twice, as I trudged along the windswept cliffs, a dull, subterranean shudder vibrated through the chalk beneath my boots—the distant, rhythmic booming of naval artillery, where dreadnoughts clashed blindly in the dark. The world was tearing itself apart in a paroxysm of simian fury, yet I cared nothing for empires or sinking fleets. My mind was a narrow, fevered tunnel, illuminated solely by the image of a solitary seaside bungalow among the dunes, where my betrayers crouched in what they fondly imagined was a sanctuary from both the war and my wrath. A thin, clammy mist curled inward from the surf, clinging to my threadbare coat like spectral fingers as I crested the final ridge and beheld the faint, amber glow of their lamp.

I crept through the whispering marram grass like a thing accursed, my boots sinking into the damp, shifting sand. Through the salt-crusted panes of the bungalow, I beheld them: Verrall, leaning casually against a bookshelf with an air of languid, patrician indifference, and Nettie, seated by the hearth, her face upturned to his in a posture of fragile devotion that drove a white-hot spike of agony through my breast. Above the slate roof, the sky had ceased to be merely night; it had become a swirling, suffocating vortex of luminescent green, pressing down upon the earth with an almost physical weight. The sea had fallen eerily silent, as if paralyzed by the descending immensity. Driven by a madness older than reason, I hurled myself against the frail wooden door, bursting into the lamplit room with the revolver leveled before me. Nettie’s shriek pierced the stifling air; Verrall sprang backward, his aristocratic pallor deepening to the hue of chalk as he confronted the wild-eyed spectre from the Staffordshire pits. I opened my mouth to vomit forth a torrent of curses, to pronounce their doom in the name of every starved, downtrodden wretch who had ever been robbed by his kind. Yet before a single syllable could escape my parched throat, the atmosphere itself seemed to curdle and thicken, and a strange, sweetish vapor began to seep through the shattered doorframe like an invisible tide.

It was the breath of the abyss, the alien ichor of the comet’s tail finally mingling with the frail envelope of our atmosphere. My finger convulsed upon the iron trigger, yet the hammer refused to fall—not from any mechanical fault, but because a sudden, crushing lethargy had seized my very marrow. A blinding, noiseless cataract of emerald fog rolled over the dunes, engulfing the bungalow in an impenetrable, glowing miasma. I gasped for air, only to draw into my lungs an unearthly, pungent essence that burned like frost and smelled of forgotten millennia. The revolver clattered from my nerveless grasp onto the floorboards. Beside the hearth, Verrall sank to his knees, clutching his throat, while Nettie crumpled like a broken lily. Darkness—not the gentle dark of sleep, but a vast, crystalline void—swallowed my senses. In that terrible delirium, the narrow walls of my ego were violently torn away. I saw myself, and all of humankind, as a swarm of blind, venomous insects crawling upon a speck of cooling mud, shrieking over scraps of territory and mates while the infinite, star-strewn gulfs looked on in pitiless majesty. The petty, festering sores of my jealousy, the ancestral hatreds of class, and the blood-lust of warring nations dissolved like cobwebs in a furnace, burned away by the cold, incomprehensible chemistry of the cosmos. I felt my mortal brain stretched upon a rack of stellar light, forced to endure a terrifying, lucid sanity that stripped away every comforting illusion of human pride, until at last even thought itself surrendered to the silent, emerald tide.

When consciousness returned, the green phantasmagoria had vanished, leaving the world washed in a pale, silver-grey light of preternatural clarity. I lay upon the wooden floor, breathing deeply of an air so pure and keen it felt as though I had never truly drawn breath before. Slowly, I pushed myself upright, my limbs trembling, yet strangely light—as if I had sloughed off a diseased, ancestral skin during that long nocturnal swoon. Across the room, Nettie and Verrall were stirring. When our eyes met, I braced myself for the familiar surge of bile and murderous rage, yet none came. I looked upon Nettie’s tear-streaked face and saw not a faithless idol, nor a possession stolen, but merely another bewildered, fragile creature awakened from a hideous nightmare; I looked upon Verrall, and saw neither master nor rival, only a fellow mortal shivering in the cold dawn of a transformed universe. Without a word, I stooped, retrieved the heavy revolver, and walked out through the broken doorway. Outside, the sea rolled gently beneath an empty, cerulean sky, its waters calm and indifferent. Far to the north, the thunder of the naval guns had ceased entirely. Along the wet sands, fishermen and soldiers alike stood in silent, scattered groups, staring blankly at the horizon. I hurled the iron weapon far into the foaming surf, knowing with a chill, solemn certainty that humanity had been forever altered—not by its own virtue, but by the cold, inscrutable mercy of the stars.
