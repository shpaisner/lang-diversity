It was in the black, soot-choked desolation of Staffordshire that I first beheld the emerald portent, hanging like a festering sore above the belching chimneys of the pot-banks. My name is William Leadford, though that meager designation scarce mattered amid the crawling, subterranean misery of Clayton and Swathinglea, where men toiled like blind grubs beneath a sky forever bruised by furnace-smoke. Poverty had gnawed at my vitals since childhood, breeding a sullen, feverish bitterness that matched the sickly madness seizing the wider world. Each evening, as I trudged past the jagged slag-heaps and stagnant canals, the newsboys shrieked of mobilized legions, of ironclad fleets steaming through the North Sea, and of Europe plunging headlong into an abyss of mechanized slaughter. Yet even the dread tidings of impending war paled beside the nameless terror growing in the heavens. Night after night, the comet swelled—no mere celestial wanderer of benign aspect, but a bloated, gibbous specter of livid green, casting a corpse-like luminescence over the squalid brick terraces. My fellow clerks and the grimy pitmen spoke of it in hushed, superstitious whispers, sensing in its cold radiance an alien malignancy that mocked our petty human empires. I, however, scarcely heeded the cosmic omen at first, for a darker, more intimate poison was consuming my reason—a monomaniacal fury born of betrayal, which soon eclipsed even the spectral star. In my cramped garret, by the guttering tallow candle, I nursed my grievances until my mind became a labyrinth of dark, obsessive corridors, blind to the green doom seeping through my cracked windowpane.

The focus of my wretched existence had been Nettie, whose fragile beauty seemed the sole uncorrupted flower blooming amid the ash-heaps of Staffordshire. Yet even she had succumbed to the rot of our stratified, merciless age. I learned with a shock that nearly shattered my sanity that she had fled—eloped in secret with Edward Verrall, the idle, aristocratic scion of our local gentry, whose smooth features and effortless wealth I had long loathed with an almost religious hatred. The knowledge struck me like a physical blow, unleashing a primordial, murderous frenzy within my breast. In a dingy pawnshop in the vilest quarter of the town, I parted with my last hoarded shillings to procure a heavy, nickel-plated revolver and a box of cartridges. The cold iron felt strangely comforting against my palm, an anchor of mortal vengeance amidst a universe spinning toward chaos. As I boarded the rattling train southward to pursue the fugitives, the comet’s baleful glare flooded the carriage windows, turning the faces of my fellow passengers into ghastly, greenish masks. They babbled incessantly of ultimata and burning border towns, blind to the fact that the green star had now outshone the moon, its monstrous tail sweeping across a third of the firmament like a luminous, toxic shroud. My own mind was wholly fixed upon Verrall’s throat, yet a creeping, subconscious dread whispered that my petty drama of jealousy was unfolding beneath the gaze of something vast, cold, and unimaginably ancient.

By the time I reached the bleak coastal flats of Shaphambury, the continent of Europe had erupted into a conflagration of steel and blood. The telegraph wires hummed with monstrous rumors of sunken dreadnoughts and shattered fortresses, while the seaside resort lay half-deserted, its tawdry bungalows huddled like frightened sheep against the encroaching tide. I wandered the desolate shingle in a state of waking nightmare, the revolver heavy in my coat pocket, my eyes scouring the mist-shrouded dunes for the guilty pair. Above the black, oily rollers of the sea, the comet loomed in terrifying majesty—a vast, swirling maelstrom of sickly emerald fire that seemed to press down upon the earth’s atmosphere. The very ocean appeared poisoned by its reflection, churning with a phosphorescent, unnatural sheen that recalled the legends of drowned, cyclopean cities. I found the track of Verrall’s motor-car in the damp sand near the lonely cliffs beyond the town, and my heart hammered a frantic, bestial rhythm against my ribs. Around me, the few lingering townsfolk moved like somnambulists, staring upward with slack jaws and hollowed eyes, as if the celestial visitor were already leaching the will from their marrow. A strange, metallic tang hung upon the salt wind, prickling my nostrils and inducing a faint, dizzying nausea. Every shadow cast by the gorse bushes seemed elongated and distorted, twitching with a spectral life of its own beneath that blasphemous, verdant sky, yet my mania drove me onward through the gathering twilight.

I cornered them at last upon a lonely stretch of road winding above the chalk cliffs, where the gnarled hedgerows whispered in the rising gale. Nettie’s cry of terror rang out shrill and thin against the booming surf as I stepped from the shadows, the revolver leveled at Verrall’s pale, startled face. He thrust her behind him with a desperate, aristocratic bravado that only inflamed my homicidal rage, his eyes wide as he beheld the madness gleaming in mine. I squeezed the trigger, expecting the deafening bark of cordite and the warm spatter of blood, yet the hammer fell with a dull, useless click upon a faulty cap. Before I could cock the weapon anew, the heavens themselves seemed to tear open in silent, suffocating cataclysm. From the bloated green star poured a descending curtain of luminous, jade vapour—a thick, swirling miasma from the outer void that swallowed the cliffs, the sea, and the sky in an instant. It struck my lungs not with fire, but with an icy, aromatic sweetness that paralyzed every muscle in my frame. I saw Verrall stagger and collapse onto the flinty road, his hands clutching his throat, while Nettie sank beside him like a wilted blossom. My own knees buckled beneath an overwhelming, hypnotic lethargy. As the green fog enveloped me, extinguishing the stars and silencing the distant roar of the surf, I felt my consciousness unraveling into a cold, infinite darkness, utterly stripped of human will.

How long I lay entombed within that luminous, unearthly stupor, I cannot reckon, for time itself seemed to dissolve within the alien chemistry of the comet’s breath. When my eyes finally unclosed, the world had undergone a transformation so profound and uncanny that it chilled my very soul. The suffocating green mist had dissipated, leaving behind an atmosphere of preternatural, crystalline clarity, yet the sky remained a pale, haunting shade of aquamarine, devoid of any familiar warmth. I rose stiffly from the damp earth, my fingers still curled around the rusted iron of the revolver, and stared at the weapon with a sudden, dizzying revulsion—not of remorse, precisely, but of absolute alienation, as though it were the relic of some extinct, bestial species. A few paces away, Verrall and Nettie were stirring from their torpor. When our eyes met, there was no trace of the terror, hatred, or feverish passion that had bound us together the night before. Instead, I beheld in their gazes a terrifying, glacial serenity—a vast, hollow lucidity that mirrored the cold void of the heavens. We spoke in hushed, strangely measured tones, our petty grievances, social barriers, and mortal lusts utterly scourged from our minds by the celestial gas. It was as though an unseen hand had reached into the wet clay of our brains and rewritten our very nature, leaving us calm, rational, and horribly devoid of the dark, chaotic fires that had once made us human.

Together, the three of us walked back toward Shaphambury in a silence broken only by the gentle, rhythmic lapping of the altered sea. As we entered the town, we discovered that the Change had spared none. Men and women stood in the streets in quiet, orderly throngs, gazing upon one another with that same eerie, dispassionate benevolence. A crumpled newspaper lay in the gutter, its bold headlines shrieking of the war in Europe—yet even as I read of the million-man armies, a passing telegraph-boy casually remarked that the guns had fallen silent across the continent, that soldiers had abandoned their trenches in a sudden, inexplicable trance of brotherhood. The world was at peace, yet as I looked at Nettie’s tranquil, vacant smile and Verrall’s placid brow, a cold, creeping horror settled permanently into the recesses of my newly clarified mind. Mankind had not conquered its savage nature through wisdom or grace; we had been domesticated in a single night by a stray, indifferent exhalation from the cosmic abyss. The comet had already begun to recede into the black gulfs between the stars, leaving behind a race of serene, obedient puppets whose very thoughts had been sterilized by an alien miasma. I cast my revolver into the sea, knowing we would never kill again, yet trembling inwardly at what vast, unknowable purpose might next drift down from the watching void to reshape our helpless clay.
