It was amidst the foul, soot-choked abysses of Staffordshire, where the great conical kilns of the Potteries belch their eternal, sulfurous incense toward a blighted heaven, that the twin madnesses of my life first took root. I was then a wretched, hollow-cheeked clerk, chained by penury to a warped desk in Clayton, breathing the acrid coal-smoke that clings like a funeral shroud to those hideous industrial valleys. Yet even in that Tartarus of blackened brick and slag, there bloomed one fragile, anomalous flower: Nettie Stuart, whose pale, haunting visage had become the sole altar of my febrile devotion. When the tidings reached me—whispered with cruel, knowing leers in the dingy alehouses—that she had fled my side for the gilded embrace of Edward Verrall, scion of the hated, parasitic gentry, a dark and ancient venom flooded my veins, awakening beasts of primordial malice I had not known slumbered within my breast. I wandered out upon the desolate slag-heaps at midnight, my soul convulsed with a murderous, ancestral rage, and it was then that I first beheld the Thing in the sky. Hanging low above the jagged, cyclopean silhouette of the foundry chimneys was a strange, portentous star—not of the honest, fiery hue of terrestrial flames, but of a sickly, unearthly emerald. Even as I cursed Nettie’s name and swore a blasphemous oath of vengeance to the indifferent night, that luminous speck seemed to pulse with a cold, alien malignancy, as though some vast, cosmic eye had opened in the outer void to witness the petty agonies of mankind.

In the weeks that followed, a singular and terrifying parallel developed between the festering of my own monomania and the feverish delirium of the outer world. From a cobwebbed, ill-lit pawnshop in Swathinglea, I procured a heavy, antiquated revolver—an ugly talisman of cold iron whose weight in my pocket brought a grim, maniacal solace to my shattered nerves. By day, I performed my meager duties in a daze of homicidal anticipation; by night, I devoured the screaming broadsheets that spoke of Europe’s inexorable drift toward the abyss of war. Mobilization was the word upon every trembling lip; great fleets of ironclad dreadnoughts were steaming into the gray, mist-shrouded North Sea, while millions of men along the Rhine sharpened their bayonets in a blind, collective lunacy. And overhead, night after night, the emerald comet swelled to monstrous, unimaginable proportions. No longer a mere star, it had become a vast, smudged phantasm of spectral, baleful green light, trailing a nebulous, feathery plume across half the firmament. Its ghastly radiance turned the squalid streets of Staffordshire into a subterranean dreamscape, casting long, distorted shadows that seemed to writhe with a sinister life of their own. Neighbors gathered in frightened, whispering knots upon the cobblestones, their faces bathed in that corpse-like verdigris pallor, muttering of divine wrath and the end of days, while I merely clutched my hidden weapon and smiled a terrible, secret smile.

At length, through a bribed and trembling servant, I learned their hiding place: the quiet, cliff-girt coastal hamlet of Shaphambury, far to the south, where Verrall sought to conceal his stolen prize amidst the salt winds and chalky downs. I boarded the midnight express from the Midlands, sharing the dim, rattling carriage with a company of wild-eyed youths bound for the coastal garrisons. All along the iron road, England lay gripped in a grotesque, martial hysteria. At every wayside station, torchlit crowds roared discordant patriotic anthems, their voices cracked with a manic, bloodthirsty fervor that echoed the dark tempest within my own breast. Yet whenever I pressed my fevered brow against the soot-streaked windowpane, the roar of the mob seemed to dwindle before the silent, cosmic terror of the heavens, which appeared to mock our mortal quarrels. The comet now dominated the zenith, a vast, cyclopean eye of luminous green mist whose sheer, terrifying magnitude dwarfed the puny Earth beneath it. Its unholy light drenched the rolling hills and sleeping villages in an eerie, phosphorescent twilight, robbing the night of its natural darkness. When I finally alighted at Shaphambury, the air was strangely still and heavy, laden with an unfamiliar, metallic tang. Behind me, the distant telegraph wires hummed with the madness of impending slaughter; before me, the dark sea murmured beneath a sky that had grown wholly, terrifyingly alien, as if the planet itself had drifted into some forbidden quadrant of the cosmos.

I found them at the hour when the world seemed to hold its breath, walking together along the lonely, wind-scoured crest of the chalk cliffs above the restless Channel. Against the luminous, emerald-tinted horizon, their two silhouettes appeared small and fragile, wholly oblivious to the stalking Nemesis behind them. I crept across the coarse, dew-drenched turf, my boots silent upon the chalk, every nerve in my body taut as a snapping wire, my mind wholly consumed by the red mist of my obsession. As I drew within twenty paces, Nettie turned, and in the ghastly, spectral glow of the comet, I saw her face—pale, startled, and infinitely remote. Verrall stepped instinctively before her, his aristocratic features twisted into a mask of sudden comprehension and alarm. With a guttural, inhuman cry, I wrenched the heavy revolver from my coat and leveled its dark barrel at his heart. Time itself seemed to coagulate in that hideous instant; I felt the cold, greasy iron against my sweating palm, saw Nettie’s mouth open in a soundless scream, and heard the distant, booming thunder of the surf below. Yet before my trembling finger could complete its fatal contraction, the entire firmament above us erupted into a silent, blinding conflagration. A colossal curtain of shimmering, verdigris vapor detached itself from the comet’s blazing nucleus and swept downward across the stars, engulfing the cliffs, the sea, and the trembling Earth in a silent, terrifying deluge of unearthly, incandescent green.

No sound accompanied that cosmic inundation, save a faint, high-pitched whistling in the upper air, like the spectral sighing of winds across forgotten, lifeless moons. Instantly, a dense, luminous fog of emerald gas enveloped the cliff-top, blotting out the sea and reducing Verrall and Nettie to vague, wavering phantoms within the mist. I braced myself for the agony of suffocation, expecting the alien miasma to sear my lungs like vitriol; instead, as I drew a ragged, involuntary breath, I inhaled a vapor of impossible, terrifying sweetness—cool, crystalline, and redolent of vast, empty spaces beyond the stars. My finger froze upon the trigger. A profound, paralytic lethargy stole through my limbs, heavy as lead yet strangely devoid of pain, and the iron revolver slipped from my nerveless grasp to vanish into the damp grass. Through the swirling green luminescence, I saw Verrall stagger and sink slowly to his knees, while Nettie collapsed beside him like a broken reed. Then my own knees gave way, and I fell supine upon the turf. Yet as my mortal body lay locked in that uncanny, cataleptic stupor, my mind did not perish; rather, it seemed to tear free from its narrow, squalid prison of Staffordshire clay. Floating through abysses of cold, luminous clarity, I felt the ancient, festering tumors of my hatred, my jealousy, and my petty human pride dissolve like smoke in the pure, indifferent radiance of the void.

When consciousness returned to my scattered faculties, the green miasma had vanished, leaving behind an atmosphere of such piercing, unearthly transparency that every distant headland and wave seemed etched in diamond. A pale, washed dawn was breaking over the Channel, free of smoke, free of cloud, and steeped in a silence so absolute it bordered on the unnatural. I rose slowly to my feet, feeling neither stiffness nor fatigue, only a strange, hollowed-out serenity that was almost frightening in its completeness. At my feet lay the rusted, ugly revolver; I looked upon it as an archaeologist might regard the flint axe of some extinct, bestial hominid, utterly unable to fathom the dark, bloody delirium that had placed it in my hand. A few paces away, Verrall and Nettie were stirring amidst the wet clover, rising with the same bewildered, quiet grace. When our eyes met, there was no terror in Nettie’s gaze, no defiance in Verrall’s, and in my own breast, not a vestige of the murderous, consuming fire that had driven me across England. We stood together upon the silent cliff, three changed and uncanny beings awakened into a world stripped of its ancestral madness. Somewhere beyond the horizon, I knew, the waiting armies of Europe were laying down their gleaming rifles in the same terrible, luminous clarity—and as I gazed out over the quiet sea, I shuddered slightly at the vast, incomprehensible strangeness of the dawn that awaited mankind.
