In the fuliginous abyss of the Staffordshire Potteries, where the belching kilns of Clayton and Swadlincote rear like blasphemous altars against a perpetually bruised sky, I, William Leadford, nurtured a madness darker than the coal-smoke that choked my lungs. My chamber was a cramped, sordid box of peeling plaster and dampness, yet it was not the grinding penury of my station that corroded my reason. It was the spectral image of Nettie—pale, elusive Nettie—who had cast aside my desperate, subterranean devotion to flee with that gilded scion of the squirearchy, young Verrall. For weeks a febrile delirium had gripped my brain, fed by the whispering malice of neighbors and the hideous, unearthly radiance that now nightly stained the northern heavens. Even the astronomers at their distant, trembling lenses could scarcely disguise their terror of that approaching wanderer from the outer void. It hung above the slag-heaps like a malignant, emerald eye, swelling with each passing dusk, bathing the soot-blackened brickworks in a sickly, phosphorescent sheen that no terrestrial fire had ever cast. Across the narrow sea, the newspapers shrieked of mobilizing armies, of Teutonic dreadnoughts steaming through the fog, and of empires poised upon the brink of mutual slaughter; yet to my disordered mind, the impending doom of Europe was merely a distant echo of the cataclysm within my own breast. I sat beside my cracked window, watching the green star climb above the chimneys, and swore a silent, gibbering oath that neither Verrall’s wealth nor the gathering apocalypse of nations should shield them from the reckoning I intended.

By the third night of my obsession, the emerald intruder had grown to an appalling magnitude, casting two-pronged, unnatural shadows across the blighted moorland as I quitted Staffordshire forever. In my coat pocket rested a heavy, cheap revolver, purchased from a trembling pawnbroker in Burslem whose eyes held the same haunted, animal dread I saw reflected in every face upon the streets. Men spoke in hushed, frantic tones of the ultimatum delivered in Berlin, of reserves called to the colors, and of a strange, metallic taste upon the evening breeze, yet none dared look long at the sky. My journey southward was a waking nightmare through a kingdom sliding into bedlam. Upon the clattering trains, soldiers in coarse khaki sang drunken, defiant anthems that died away into uneasy silence whenever the carriage windows revealed that vast, luminous plume sweeping across the zenith. At London, the great termini swarmed with weeping women and wild-eyed clerks clutching extras that proclaimed the first naval engagements in the North Sea. I moved among them like a revenant, untouched by their patriotic frenzy, driven solely by the cold, geometric certainty of my hatred. By twilight of the fourth day, I reached the coastal cliffs of Shaphambury, where rumor placed the fugitives. The sea below was no longer blue, but lay like a sheet of oiled, necrotic glass under the towering green specter of the comet, while far out beyond the horizon, the muffled, rhythmic thudding of heavy ordnance announced that the ancient madness of Europe had at last erupted in fire and iron.

Shaphambury lay half-abandoned, its stuccoed boarding-houses and shuttered pavilions wearing the aspect of a necropolis beneath the baleful celestial glare. The summer visitors had fled inland at the first thunder of the Channel guns, leaving behind only a handful of dazed townsfolk who huddled in doorways, staring upward with slack, terror-stricken jaws. I prowled the deserted Esplanade, my fingers wrapped tightly around the cold iron grip in my pocket, scanning every shadow for the hated silhouette of Verrall. The air had grown extraordinarily still and oppressive, laden with a faint, sweetish odor resembling crushed almonds or some unknown nocturnal blossom, which seemed to prickle at the base of the skull. From the darkened pier came the sound of frantic hammering as two fishermen sought to launch a skiff into the stagnant, glowing surf, their voices shrill with a nameless panic. I ignored them, turning instead toward the lonely, wind-scoured chalk cliffs where the grander villas stood isolated above the sea. There, behind the wrought-iron gates of a secluded garden, I caught the sudden gleam of a lantern and heard the unmistakable murmur of Nettie’s voice. A surge of hot, primordial fury, indistinguishable from the blood-lust now raging across the Belgian plains, flooded my veins. I vaulted the low stone wall, creeping through the dew-soaked shrubbery like some nocturnal predator, while overhead the comet’s vast, bifurcated tail seemed to sag downward, as though the very firmament were preparing to disgorge its alien substance upon the cowering earth.

I found them standing upon a stone terrace overlooking the luminous abyss of the sea—Verrall tall and rigid in his evening attire, Nettie clinging to his arm, her face upturned toward the monstrous green canopy that now usurped half the sky. When I stepped from the shadows of the laurel bushes and leveled the revolver, Verrall gasped and thrust the girl behind him, though his aristocratic composure crumbled instantly before the dual terror of my weapon and the looming heavens. Nettie uttered a single, piercing cry—not of recognition, I realized with a pang of bitter lucidity, but of sheer, helpless dread at the madness blazing in my eyes. I opened my mouth to pronounce their doom, to scream out the accumulated grievances of my squalid, trampled life, when a blinding, silent flash of emerald light rent the upper atmosphere. Simultaneously, the distant booming of the naval guns ceased as if severed by a cosmic blade. Down from the zenith rushed a dense, rolling curtain of pale green vapor, swallowing the stars, engulfing the cliffs, and wrapping the terrace in an eerie, luminous fog. My finger tightened upon the trigger, yet a sudden, leaden paralysis crept through my limbs. The sweetish miasma filled my nostrils—cold, penetrating, and horribly alien—and as the pistol slipped from my nerveless grasp to clatter uselessly upon the flagstones, I saw Nettie and Verrall sink slowly to their knees, their faces slackening into expressions of vacant, unearthly wonder before darkness claimed my senses.

Of the hours that followed, no terrestrial tongue can furnish an adequate chronicle, for my consciousness was unmoored from its narrow, bestial anchorage and cast adrift amidst gulfs of unthinkable serenity. I lay upon the cold stone of the terrace, yet I felt neither chill nor pain, only the inexorable dissolution of every petty rage and squalid memory that had once constituted William Leadford. It was as though the green vapor were a subtle, non-material reagent, scouring the labyrinthine chambers of the human brain with an alien chemistry born in the sunless voids between the stars. In that luminous trance, I perceived the earth not as the proud stage of empires and lovers, but as a fragile, dust-mote sphere spinning blindly through an indifferent cosmos. I saw the great ironclads upon the sea drifting silent and harmless, their crews slumbering beneath the emerald shroud; I saw the blood-soaked trenches of the continent hushed, the frantic fingers of artillerymen relaxing upon their lanyards as the strange miasma seeped into every cellar, palace, and hovel. There was no warmth in this cosmic absolution, no benevolent deity stooping to pardon mankind’s follies—only the cold, vast, accidental touch of a passing celestial entity whose very exhalations rewrote the fevered architecture of our nerves. When at last the green fog began to thin, curling upward like incense toward the retreating star, I knew with a chilling, crystalline certainty that the old race of men had perished in their sleep, and that whatever awoke would wear our flesh yet think with alien clarity.

I opened my eyes to a dawn of unbearable, diamond-like brilliance. The sky was a pale, washed azure, purged of smoke and cloud, and the distant comet had dwindled to a faint, receding spark in the west. Slowly, I rose from the flagstones, marveling at the lightness of my limbs and the terrifying silence that hung over Shaphambury. Nearby, Verrall was stirring, sitting up with a bewildered gaze fixed upon his own trembling hands, while Nettie knelt beside him, her eyes wide and strangely luminous. I looked upon them—the woman I had sworn to slay, the man I had hated with every poisoned fiber of my being—and felt nothing. No surge of jealousy, no lingering ember of wounded pride, not even the faintest tremor of desire stirred within my breast. It was as though a surgeon of infinite skill had excised the very glands of human passion, leaving behind a vast, echoing cathedral of pure, indifferent thought. Verrall met my gaze without fear or arrogance; Nettie looked upon me as one might regard a fellow survivor of some nameless shipwreck upon an unknown shore. Together, without a word of accusation or forgiveness, we walked to the parapet and looked out over the quiet sea. Far on the horizon, a thin plume of black smoke rose from a burning warship, the last dying remnant of the old world’s frenzy, already fading into the immense, immaculate morning. We were at peace, yet as the cold wind touched my brow, I shuddered to realize how utterly we had been unmade.
