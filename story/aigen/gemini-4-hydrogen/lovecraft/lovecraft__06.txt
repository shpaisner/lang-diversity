It was in the fuliginous, soot-choked squalor of Staffordshire that my reason first began to fray beneath the twin pressures of earthly betrayal and celestial portent. By day I toiled amidst the belching chimneys and slag-heaps of Clayton, a mere cipher in that industrial abyss, nursing a bitter, impoverished existence that seemed ordained by some malignant demiurge. Yet even that grey misery had held a solitary star—Nettie, whose fragile beauty had briefly illuminated my wretched hovel. When she fled into the decadent embrace of Edward Verrall, scion of the local gentry, a cold, murderous madness took root within my breast. How I loathed that sleek, aristocratic usurper, and how I cursed the rigid, rotting hierarchy that permitted him to pluck my sole possession like a weed from the mire! Yet my private agony was soon mirrored by a darker dread brooding over the whole of Europe. In the dingy taprooms and upon the rain-slicked cobblestones, men whispered hoarsely of mobilizing legions, of ironclad leviathans steaming through the North Sea, and of an inevitable cataclysm of blood and fire. And above it all, piercing the greasy pall of coal-smoke each dusk, hung the nameless intruder from the outer void. At first it was merely a faint, sickly speck of verdigris light near the constellation of Gemini, ignored by all save the most feverish astronomers; yet night by night it swelled, casting an unnatural, corpselike luminescence upon the slag-heaps, as though some vast, unblinking eye were opening in the gulf beyond the stars.

Driven by a delirious obsession that eclipsed even my fear of the looming European conflagration, I resolved to hunt the fugitives down. From a shadowy pawnbroker in a labyrinthine alley of Swathmore I procured a cheap, heavy revolver—a crude talisman of iron and lead that felt strangely warm against my palm as I boarded the southward train. Throughout that nightmare journey, England seemed poised upon the very brink of dissolution. At every station, frantic crowds surged around newsboys screaming of ultimatums, of Teutonic dreadnoughts massing in the grey mists, and of vast armies rattling their sabres along the Rhine. The passengers within my carriage spoke in hushed, hysterical tones, their faces gaunt and terrified, yet their eyes drifted endlessly toward the darkening windows. For the comet had grown monstrous. No longer a mere point of light, it now unfurled a vast, spectral plume of emerald vapour across the zenith, dimming the familiar constellations with its cold, alien radiance. I sat hunched in the corner, my fingers curled tightly around the pistol in my coat pocket, impervious to the collective panic of nations. What mattered the slaughter of millions when my own soul was consumed by a far more intimate venom? I pictured Nettie laughing beside Verrall in some sequestered coastal retreat, and a red haze swam before my eyes, mingling horribly with the sickly green glare that bled through the rattling glass of the carriage.

I reached the seaside hamlet of Shaphambury as a premature, unearthly twilight settled over the chalk cliffs. The sea below was no longer the familiar, turbulent grey of the English Channel, but an oily, undulating expanse that mirrored the bloated green terror in the heavens. A strange, breathless hush hung over the narrow lanes, broken only by the distant, rhythmic throbbing of coastal batteries preparing for the inevitable invasion. The townsfolk had bolted their shutters against the twin horrors of war and the sky, leaving me to stalk the deserted esplanade like a phantom risen from the Staffordshire mines. Following the fragmented rumours I had gleaned from a trembling innkeeper, I turned inland toward an isolated bungalow perched near the edge of the heath. The air had grown inexplicably dense, charged with a faint, metallic tang that set my teeth on edge and made my temples throb with a feverish pulse, echoing the far-off rumble of guns. Looking upward, I recoiled in involuntary awe; the comet now dominated half the firmament, a swirling, cyclopean vortex of luminescent mist whose core burned with an intensity no mortal eye could long endure. Beneath its malignant gaze, the gnarled gorse and hedgerows of the heath assumed grotesque, elongated shapes, swaying without wind as if in silent homage to an approaching master. I drew the revolver from my pocket, its oiled barrel gleaming faintly emerald, and crept toward the lighted windows of the bungalow.

Through the parted lace curtains of the parlour, I beheld them—a tableau of hated tranquility amidst a crumbling world. Verrall sat lounging in a wicker chair, his handsome, arrogant profile bathed in the warm yellow glow of an oil lamp, while Nettie stood near the hearth, her pale face upturned with an expression of mingled tenderness and vague alarm. The stark contrast between that cosy domestic amber and the eldritch green hell brooding outside the glass inflamed my madness beyond endurance. Pushing open the unlocked garden gate, I strode onto the gravel path, my heavy boots crunching loudly in the unnatural stillness. They heard me; the French windows swung wide, and Verrall stepped out onto the veranda, his eyes narrowing as he recognized my gaunt, soot-stained figure emerging from the emerald shadows. "Leadford?" he breathed, half in disbelief, half in aristocratic disdain. Before he could utter another syllable, I raised the trembling weapon, aiming squarely at his heart, while Nettie let out a choked, terrified cry behind him. My breath tore raggedly from my throat as I prepared to snuff out his pampered existence. Yet in that supreme instant of murderous triumph, my finger froze upon the trigger. A sudden, deafening roar—not of terrestrial artillery, but of a rushing, cosmic wind—descended from the upper atmosphere. The sky seemed to tear open, and a blinding cascade of luminous green vapour spilled down over the cliffs, swallowing the stars, the sea, and the very breath in our lungs.

It was no mere earthly fog that enveloped us, but a thick, iridescent miasma drawn from the uncharted abysses between the worlds. As it swept across the veranda, transforming the solid timber and stone into phantom silhouettes, I dropped to my knees, the heavy revolver clattering harmlessly onto the flagstones. A peculiar, sweetish aroma—unlike any flora of this planet—flooded my nostrils, seeping deep into my lungs and coursing through my veins like liquid fire. I tried to cry out, to curse Verrall, to cling to the familiar, bitter anchor of my hatred, yet my mind was abruptly unmoored. The petty boundaries of my consciousness dissolved beneath an overwhelming, alien tide, and the ancestral malice of humanity seemed to evaporate like smoke in a gale. Around me, through the swirling emerald haze, I saw Verrall slump against the wooden pillar, while Nettie sank softly to the floor beside him, both overcome by the same irresistible stupor. Above our prostrate forms, the distant thrum of mobilizing fleets and the gathering thunder of European war faded into an infinite, mocking silence. My vision fractured into vast, impossible geometries of pale green light, wherein the whole history of mankind appeared as a brief, feverish twitch upon a speck of cooling dust. Then, gently and inexorably, the luminescent mist closed over my senses, and I drifted into a dreamless, timeless void.

I awoke not to the blackness of death, but to a dawn of terrifying, crystalline clarity. The emerald mist had vanished, leaving the morning sky a vast, immaculate dome of pale azure, purged of every cloud and smoke-stain. Slowly I pushed myself up from the dew-drenched flagstones, astounded by a profound, almost unnatural lightness in my limbs. The feverish ache that had haunted my chest since childhood was gone; more unsettling still, the dark, venomous knot of hatred within my brain had been utterly excised, as though some celestial surgeon had cauterized my soul while I slept. Beside me, Verrall stirred and sat up, gazing around with wide, bewildered eyes. There was no arrogance left in his bearing, nor any trace of fear when his gaze met mine. Together we turned toward the open doors, where Nettie was emerging into the pure, silent sunlight. We stood in a strange, wordless communion, three fragile remnants of a species abruptly awakened from a long, bestial nightmare. In the distance, where the warships had lurked in the Channel mists, no guns sounded; I knew, with a sudden, chilling certainty, that across all the blood-hungry capitals of Europe, men were waking to the same vast, incomprehensible sanity. Yet as I looked upon Nettie’s tranquil face and upward into the empty, indifferent heavens, a quiet dread lingered in my breast—for we were no longer truly ourselves, but altered creatures, reshaped forever by an alien breath from beyond the stars.
