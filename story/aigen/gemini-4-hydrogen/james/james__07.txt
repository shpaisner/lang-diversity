It was not, William Leadford had long since concluded, that the universe owed him a gentler lodging, yet the sheer, grey accumulation of Staffordshire—its soot-laden air, its mean, bricked horizons, the small, anxious economies of his mother’s parlour—had come to press upon his spirit with the weight of a personal affront. He sat by the narrow window in the thickening dusk, a cheap, ink-stained volume of social philosophy neglected upon his knee, and watched the sky above the potteries. For weeks now, the newspapers had been full of a twin distraction: the vast, inexorable drift of the European powers toward an armed collision, and the steady, almost theatrical approach of a pale green star that hung nightly in the firmament like an uninvited witness. To William, however, both the distant mobilization of fleets and the celestial portent were merely vast, impersonal metaphors for the quiet catastrophe of his own existence.

What occupied him, with a sharpness that made his chest ache, was the altered quality of Nettie’s silence. It had been three days since she had last met him by the canal, and in the interval her image had grown, in his restless imagination, at once more luminous and more unbearably remote. He was, he knew only too well, a clerk with frayed cuffs and a bitter tongue, whose whole fortune lay in the fierce, unlovely pride of his intellect; whereas she possessed that rare, effortless bloom which seemed to ask for nothing less than the world’s most delicate setting. As the green light of the comet began to assert itself above the chimney-stacks, touching the smoke with a faint, sickly iridescence, William felt a sudden, chilling premonition that the small, cherished edifice of their understanding was already in ruins.

The confirmation, when it arrived the following afternoon, wore the banal, humiliating guise of an envelope left upon her uncle’s mantelpiece. William had walked the three miles to Checkshill in a state of suspended dread, only to find the old man staring at the hearth with a face of helpless, bewildered propriety. Nettie was gone. She had not merely stepped out for the air, nor vanished into the commonplace refuge of a relative’s house; she had, as the brief, trembling lines of her note made terribly plain, placed herself under the protection of Edward Verrall. The name struck William with the force of a physical blow, bringing with it the whole hateful vision of Verrall’s world—the motor-car humming along the dusty lanes, the easy, sunlit impunity of wealth, the casual condescension with which a gentleman might stoop to gather what a poor man had spent years reverently courting.

He did not linger to offer the old man any hollow consolation; he turned back into the lane with a strange, cold lucidity settling over his faculties. That they had gone south, toward the coast—toward Shaphambury, where Verrall’s people kept a house above the sea—he pieced together from a dozen half-remembered remarks Nettie had let fall in happier weeks. Before the evening train departed, William made a single, deliberate detour to a pawnshop in the lower town. The heavy, antiquated revolver he slipped into his coat pocket was, he dimly recognized, a clumsy, almost theatrical instrument for a man of his professed enlightenment, yet its cold weight against his hip offered a perverse, steadying comfort. It represented the one argument left to him that Verrall’s money could not effortlessly smile away.

The journey southward was a long, rattling immersion in a fever that seemed to belong as much to the nation as to himself. In the crowded third-class carriage, men in dusty broadcloth leaned over the evening editions, their voices raised in anxious, fragmentary debate over the latest ultimatum from the continent, the calling up of reserves, the sudden paralysis of trade. William sat wedged in the corner, his eyes fixed upon the darkening fields rushing past the pane, scarcely hearing their talk of cruisers and artillery. What was the collision of empires, he asked himself with a bitter, inward smile, beside the private treachery that had hollowed out his life? Yet even as he nursed his grievance, he could not wholly escape the sense that the world outside was growing unnaturally tight, as though every human arrangement were straining toward some imminent, irrevocable snap.

Above the low hills of the midlands, the comet had risen higher, no longer a mere point of curiosity, but a broad, luminous fan of emerald fire that drenched the twilight in an unearthly sheen. The faces of his fellow passengers, caught in the pale glare through the dusty glass, looked strangely haggard, like figures glimpsed in a fever-dream. William’s hand crept repeatedly to his pocket, his fingers tracing the chill, corrugated grip of the pistol. He had no clear vision of what he meant to do when he found them; he knew only that he could not bear to remain a silent, discarded figure in the background of their happiness, and that the sheer, suffocating pressure of his humiliation demanded some final, unmistakable reckoning.

Shaphambury, when he reached it late that night, presented a spectacle of indifferent, gilded leisure that only sharpened his sense of outrage. The sea lay dark and vast beneath the green-lit sky, breaking in long, whispering lines of phosphorescence against the shingle, while along the promenade the great hotels glowed with warm, amber windows, indifferent alike to the rumours of war and the strange light overhead. William, dusty and unshaven, paced the gravel walk like a ghost at a banquet, his eyes scanning the shadowy terraces for a glimpse of a white dress or the careless, assured silhouette of his rival. The air was remarkably still, heavy with the scent of salt and the faint, sweetish odour of the sea-thrift along the cliffs.

It was from a loitering porter near the Grand Hotel that he gleaned, through a few guarded, awkward inquiries, the direction of his quarry. A gentleman answering to Verrall’s description—a young man with a motor-car and a striking, pale-faced companion—had engaged rooms at the smaller, quieter inn perched above the western cliffs. William thanked the man with a curt nod and turned away toward the steep path that climbed the downs. As he ascended, the lights of the town fell away beneath him, and the great, silent expanse of the English Channel opened out to his left, gleaming faintly under the immense, spreading plume of the comet. His breath came short, not merely from the climb, but from the terrifying proximity of the moment toward which every bitter hour of his life seemed to have been converging.

He found them not at the inn, but upon the turf of the cliff-top itself, where they had evidently wandered to watch the extraordinary spectacle of the night. They stood a little apart from the path, their figures etched with startling clarity against the luminous emerald mist that now seemed to cling to the horizon. Nettie was leaning slightly against Verrall’s arm, her face upturned toward the sky, and even in the strangeness of the light William could see the soft, troubled curve of her cheek. At the sound of his heavy, uneven footsteps on the gravel, they turned together, and for a long, suspended instant no one spoke. It was Verrall who first recovered his composure, stepping forward with an air of mild, slightly irritated inquiry, as though he were confronting an importunate beggar.

"Leadford?" Verrall murmured, his voice carrying that effortless, modulated ease which William had always detested. "What on earth brings you down here at this hour?"

William did not answer him directly; his eyes were fixed upon Nettie, who had shrunk back a pace, her hands clasped tightly against her breast. "You didn't think, perhaps," William said, his voice trembling despite his fierce effort to keep it level, "that I should let you vanish without a word? Without so much as an accounting?"

"William, please," Nettie whispered, her tone edged with a frightened pity that stung him far worse than Verrall’s disdain. "There is nothing to account for. I couldn't—I couldn't bear the life there any longer. It was suffocating me."

"And so you chose this," William cried, his hand plunging into his coat pocket and emerging with the clumsy, glinting weight of the revolver. Verrall stiffened, his smooth mask dropping away into a sharp, calculating alertness, while Nettie gave a low, choked gasp. Yet even as William raised his arm, the gesture felt strangely hollow, as though he were an actor reciting lines in a language he had half forgotten.

Before he could steady his trembling wrist, a singular change passed over the night. It began not as a sound, but as a sudden, profound alteration in the quality of the air—a thick, sweetish coolness that seemed to descend from the great green canopy overhead, wrapping the cliffs, the sea, and the three silent figures in an impenetrable hush. William felt his lungs draw in a breath that carried the faint, bewildering fragrance of bruised herbs and distant rain. His finger, resting upon the trigger, grew curiously heavy; the fierce, knotted tension that had sustained him from Staffordshire to the coast began, with an alarming and irresistible gentleness, to slacken.

"What is that?" Verrall’s voice sounded thick, stripped of its accustomed assurance, as he swayed slightly upon the turf. "The air—it’s stifling."

William tried to speak, to summon back the righteous fury that had driven him up the steep path, but the words dissolved before they reached his lips. A vast, luminous drowsiness was stealing through his limbs, not like the heavy stupor of exhaustion, but like the sudden, quiet opening of a door in a darkened room. He saw Nettie sink slowly to her knees upon the damp grass, her face turned upward with an expression of pure, childlike wonder rather than fear, and beside her Verrall dropped heavily onto one arm, his eyes closing as if against a too-brilliant light. The revolver slipped from William’s unresisting fingers and fell silently into the turf. As he himself sank down beside them, his last conscious impression was of the immense, green-drenched sky leaning gently over the quiet earth, and of a vast, unseen tide of peace washing silently over the distant, waiting guns of Europe.

When William opened his eyes, the green glare had vanished, and the morning lay over the downs in a wash of pale, crystalline gold. For several moments he did not stir, content simply to lie upon the cool grass and marvel at the extraordinary lightness of his own spirit. It was as though he had awakened from a long, cramped illness during which he had been entirely preoccupied with the petty discomforts of a darkened room, only to find the shutters flung wide to an immense, sunlit landscape. He sat up slowly, brushing the dampness from his sleeve, and his gaze fell upon the revolver lying a few feet away in the grass. He looked at it without a flicker of recognition or remorse—it seemed merely an absurd, rusted relic of a world that had ceased to exist.

A few paces off, Nettie and Verrall were also stirring, rising to their feet with the slow, bewildered grace of sleepers waking in an unfamiliar country. There was no awkwardness in the silence that held them as their eyes met, none of the old, jagged embarrassment of class or wounded pride. Verrall looked at William—not with the patronizing tolerance of the night before, nor with alarm, but with a quiet, grave attentiveness, as if seeing for the first time a fellow traveller who had shared the same perilous crossing.

"You came a long way, Leadford," Verrall said gently, his voice stripped of every trace of its former artifice, leaving only a simple, unadorned humanity. "And we—we were very blind."

Nettie stepped forward, her face still pale in the morning light, yet luminous with a new, untroubled candour. She did not look at William with guilt, nor at Verrall with clinging dependence, but seemed to hold them both in a gaze of deep, serene affection. "Everything looks so small now," she murmured, gesturing faintly toward the town below and the wide, empty sea beyond. "All that we were so afraid of—all that we fought against. How could we ever have been so unkind?"

William rose to his feet, feeling the cool sea wind against his forehead, and looked from one to the other. The fierce, possessive ache that had consumed him for months had vanished so completely that he could scarcely recall its shape; in its place there remained only a vast, quiet tenderness, a sense that the world had been washed clean of its old, suffocating barriers, and that whatever awaited them beyond the sunlit cliffs, they would meet it not as rivals or prisoners of their past, but in the clear, spacious freedom of the dawn.
