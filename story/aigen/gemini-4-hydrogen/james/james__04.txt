It was not, William Leadford had come to feel with an acuteness that seemed almost to divide his consciousness in two, merely the brute fact of Nettie’s flight that pressed so intolerably upon his spirit; it was rather the dreadful, the quite unanswerable lucidity with which he now perceived the smallness of his own poor stage. Sitting in the narrow, soot-stained parlor in Clayton, while his mother’s kettle hissed with a kind of domestic indifference on the hob, he turned over for the hundredth time the exquisite, the maddening simplicity of what had occurred. Nettie had gone—had slipped from the grimy, cramped circumference of their Staffordshire world as effortlessly as a bird from a cage whose door had been left, through some fatal oversight of his own, unlatched. And she had gone with young Verrall, whose father’s great house sat upon the hill above the valley like an emblem of everything William had been taught, by his books and his bitter, solitary evenings, to despise, yet whose effortless grace he could neither imitate nor forgive. Above the slag-heaps and the clustered chimneys, visible even through the yellow haze of the afternoon, hung the strange green star—a pale, portentous smudge in the upper air that the newspapers had begun to discuss with an uneasy mixture of scientific detachment and vulgar dread. William, however, scarcely spared it a glance; his own private firmament had already collapsed, and in the pocket of his cheap serge coat his fingers closed, with a cold, steady persistence, around the heavy iron of the revolver he had purchased in Swathinglea.

The journey southward in the third-class carriage offered him, through its very monotony of rattle and smoke, a curious interval in which to nurse his dark, unyielding intention. Opposite him, two commercial travelers in glossy hats leaned toward one another over the spread sheets of the morning journals, their voices rising and falling in a fretful duet concerning the European crisis. Europe, it appeared, was drifting with a terrible, somnambulistic certainty toward war; there were dispatches from the Channel, rumors of ultimatums, and a general, feverish sense that the old, comfortable equilibrium of nations was about to shatter like a dropped glass. William listened to their agitated conjectures with a faint, disdainful curl of his lip. What were their fleets and their frontiers to the immense, silent devastation that had been wrought within his own breast? Yet, as the train tore through the green, unblemished midlands—so unlike the scarred, blackened earth of his native district—he felt a strange kinship between the madness of the statesmen and the cold, rigid purpose that kept his hand pressed against his coat. He had become, he told himself, a mere instrument of retribution, a solitary figure moving steadily against the current of a frivolous world. Even when the travelers paused in their political alarm to peer nervously through the dusty pane at the widening, emerald-tinted glare of the comet, now unmistakably a presence in the deepening twilight, William remained fixed in his corner, guarding his small, ferocious secret as though it were the single real thing left in a universe of shadows.

Shaphambury, when he emerged into its bright, salt-scented air the following morning, struck him immediately as an affront—a place contrived exclusively for the ease of people who had never known the weight of an unpaid bill or the taste of coal-dust in the throat. The white terraces curved along the bay with an air of serene, hereditary entitlement, and the sea itself lay flat and glittering under a sky that seemed, for all its summer brilliance, subtly infected by the pale greenness of the approaching star. William walked the promenade with his collar turned up, acutely, painfully aware of the shabbiness of his boots and the dusty stiffness of his provincial attire. It was not long before he saw them. They were descending the steps of the Grand Hotel, and the sight of them—so framed by the gleaming stucco and the potted palms—caught him with a physical shock that left him breathless against the iron railing. Nettie wore a dress of pale, floating muslin he had never seen, and she was laughing, her head tilted back with an abandon that seemed to him, in that hideous instant, the very portrait of treachery. Beside her, Verrall leaned slightly inward, speaking with an easy, indolent familiarity, his hand resting lightly upon the crook of her parasol. The spectacle of their harmony—so complete, so effortlessly immune to the squalor from which William had come—filled him with a despair more sharp than anger. They were, he saw with a terrible clarity, inhabitants of another element altogether, and he stood outside the glass, a dark, dusty ghost whom they had already forgotten.

He followed them until the promenade gave way to the lonely, turf-covered slopes of the eastern cliff, where the town’s genteel murmur faded behind the rhythmic sighing of the tide. It was there, in the gathering dusk, that he finally stepped from the shadow of a gorse-bush to bar their path. Nettie’s small, sharp cry of recognition seemed to hang for a moment in the quiet air, while Verrall, after a single startled step backward, assumed an attitude of cool, slightly impatient inquiry. "Leadford?" the young man said, his voice carrying that effortless, modulated calm which William had always hated more than any insult. "What on earth brings you down here?" William could scarcely speak; the rehearsed sentences of accusation, the grand, bitter speeches he had composed in the Staffordshire dark, dissolved into a dry constriction of his throat. He drew the revolver from his pocket, holding it awkwardly, almost apologetically, yet with a desperate rigidity of his arm. Nettie pressed her hands to her mouth, her eyes widening not merely at the weapon, but at the gaunt, wild misery of his face. Before Verrall could move or William’s trembling finger could find its resolve, a sudden, distant clamor drifted up from the town below—the thin, frantic cries of newsboys echoing along the sea-front, carrying the single, terrible word of war. Simultaneously, as if in answer to that human folly, the sky above the headland seemed to open in a vast, silent sheet of emerald fire, bathing the three of them in a light so unearthly and intense that the pistol in William’s hand appeared suddenly absurd, a toy of rust and iron beneath an indifferent heaven.

In the confusion of that blinding illumination, William had somehow turned and fled—not from Verrall’s advancing step, nor even from Nettie’s frightened, imploring call, but from the intolerable, sudden weight of his own ridiculous violence. He wandered blindly along the high, wind-swept downs until the lights of Shaphambury vanished behind the swell of the land, leaving him alone with the sea and the prodigious, silent spectacle overhead. The comet now occupied half the vault of heaven, a vast, feathery fan of luminous green that cast no shadows, yet lent to every blade of grass and chalky ridge a strange, phosphorescent distinctness. As the night deepened, a curious phenomenon began to manifest itself along the coast: a soft, pale mist, neither cold nor damp, rose gently from the horizon and drifted inland across the turf. It carried an odor faintly sweet, like crushed leaves or distant summer rain, and as William inhaled it, standing rigid and breathless by the cliff’s edge, he felt a singular, unhurried slackening of his tightly wound nerves. Down in the channel, the distant, winking signal-lights of the warships—those grim, restless sentinels of the newly declared war—faded one by one into the thickening, luminous haze. His knees gave way beneath him, not in terror, but in an overwhelming, luxurious fatigue, as though he had been carrying an immense, pointless burden for years and had only now been permitted to lay it down. Stretching himself upon the cool, fragrant grass, William closed his eyes, and the fierce, narrow little drama of his jealousy dissolved into a sleep as deep and dreamless as the sea.

When he woke, the world was bathed in a morning light of such extraordinary, washed lucidity that he lay for several minutes without moving, merely drinking in the sheer, unclouded beauty of the sky. The green glare was gone; in its place arched a pale, delicate blue, infinitely high and serene, as if the atmosphere itself had been cleansed of some ancient, invisible grime. William sat up slowly, bracing himself against the familiar return of his misery—the gnawing, bitter ache of Nettie’s betrayal, the hot resentment of Verrall’s privilege—yet, to his profound astonishment, the pain did not come. He searched the chambers of his own mind as a man might search a darkened house after a storm, only to find every window flung wide to the morning air. The revolver lay beside him in the dew-wet grass, looking curiously small and pathetic, like a discarded relic of some barbarous childhood; he picked it up by the barrel and tossed it over the cliff’s edge, watching without regret as it vanished into the white foam far below. What had happened to him, he wondered, as he rose and brushed the chalk from his shabby trousers? He remembered every detail of his Staffordshire life—the soot, the cramped parlor, his mother’s anxious eyes, his own fierce, cramped pride—and he saw them now not with anger, nor even with shame, but with a vast, quiet pity. It was as though a smoky pane of glass, through which he had squinted all his days, had been shattered overnight, leaving him face to face with an immense, unexplored reality.

Walking back into Shaphambury, he found the town wrapped in an unfamiliar, gentle hush. Doors stood open along the terraces, and little groups of townspeople and guests lingered on the pavements, speaking together in low, wondering voices, their faces marked by a quiet, bewildered radiance. The frantic posters announcing the war still clung to the hoardings, yet nobody glanced at them; the very notion of nations hurling iron at one another across the sea seemed, in that clear morning, as distant and incomprehensible as a bad dream. On the gravel walk before the Grand Hotel, William encountered Nettie and Verrall. They were standing together near the balustrade, looking out across the tranquil bay, and when they turned at his approach, there was neither fear in Nettie’s eyes nor condescension in Verrall’s—only a deep, quiet recognition, as of travelers who had survived the same strange voyage. "William," Nettie said softly, stepping toward him with a grave, unhurried grace that seemed far lovelier than her nervous laughter of the day before. "We were coming to look for you." They stood together for a long, unembarrassed moment, and William felt, with a quietness that was almost joy, the final dissolution of every barrier that had ever stood between them. He saw that he did not possess Nettie, just as Verrall did not possess her, and that the fierce, mean little kingdom of his former desire had been swept away to make room for something infinitely more spacious. "There is so much to undo," Verrall murmured, looking out toward the glittering water, "and so much, I suppose, to begin." William nodded, smiling faintly in the clear sun, as the three of them turned together toward the waking town.
