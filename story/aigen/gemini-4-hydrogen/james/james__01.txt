It was in the grey, unlovely bosom of Staffordshire, amid the perpetual exhalation of the kilns and the sullen, soot-begrimed geometry of the pit-heads, that William Leadford first felt the double pressure of his fate. He was a young man whose poverty had not so much dulled his perceptions as sharpened them to an almost morbid edge, so that every cinder crunching beneath his worn boots, every cramped ledger-page he turned in his dismal clerkship, seemed a deliberate slight offered by the universe to his capacity for feeling. Of late, however, his private grievance had found a strange, vast accompaniment in the heavens. Night after night, above the red glare of the blast-furnaces, a new luminary had hung—a pale, greenish-amber portent that swelled with an uncanny, silent persistence, as though some celestial eye were leaning closer to inspect the squalor of the Four Towns. Nor was the earth itself less agitated. Along the blackened streets, the evening sheets cried of ultimatums, of armoured trains steaming toward continental frontiers, of a Europe drifting, with the blind, pompous fatality of an old tragedy, toward war. Leadford, walking homeward through the acrid dusk, regarded both the star and the newspapers with a bitter, personal irony. What, after all, were the quarrels of emperors to a man whose own small kingdom had just been shaken to its foundations? For Nettie—whose bright, timid grace had seemed the single flower rooting in that slag-heap of a world—had grown of late curiously distant, her glances drifting past his shoulder toward a horizon he could neither afford nor name.

The certainty, when it arrived, did not strike him with the bluntness of a blow so much as it unfolded before him like a terribly legible document he had long dreaded to read. Nettie had gone; and she had gone—the detail possessed for Leadford’s lacerated pride an almost unbearable symmetry—with Edward Verrall. It was not merely that Verrall was rich, though the young gentleman’s effortless command of leisure, of clean linen, of carriages that rolled silently past Leadford’s dusty trudging, formed the very texture of the injury. It was rather that Verrall wore his advantages with an unconscious, sunlit grace that made Leadford’s own fierce, self-taught earnestness appear, even to himself, slightly ridiculous. In the small, stuffy parlour where Nettie’s mother wept into a damp handkerchief, Leadford stood rigid, taking in the pitiful little note left upon the mantelpiece—a scrap of paper full of breathless, half-formed apologies that only confirmed how completely the girl had been dazzled out of her own sphere. He said very little; his silence, indeed, had the density of something compressed under great weight. Later that evening, in a dim side-street shop whose window offered tarnished watches and second-hand instruments of desperation, he laid down his last sovereigns for a small, cold revolver. To possess it gave him, for the first time in days, a curious, formal composure. If the world’s great powers were preparing to settle their wounded honour with iron and fire, he would not be behindhand in the terrible etiquette of the hour.

The southward train bore him away from the smoke, though not from the fever that seemed to have seized the entire island. Opposite him in the third-class carriage, two commercial travellers bent over a spread newspaper, their voices dropping into that hushed, relishful gravity with which men discuss a distant conflagration they expect to watch from a safe distance. Fleets were coaling; reserves were summoned; the great, intricate machine of European civilization was grinding into a posture of mortal offence. Leadford looked out at the flying hedgerows of the Midlands, seeing in every quiet meadow a strange, borrowed stillness, as if the countryside itself were holding its breath. As twilight deepened, the green comet rose once more above the darkening fields, no longer a mere speck of curiosity, but a luminous, feathery fan that cast a faint, unearthly pallor across the carriage glass. He felt the weight of the revolver in his coat pocket—a hard, reassuringly definite fact amid so much vague, drifting apprehension. They had gone, he had learned through a clerk’s indiscretion at the station, to Shaphambury on the coast, where Verrall’s people maintained a discreet, sea-facing villa. Leadford pictured them there with an acute, tormenting precision: the white tablecloth, the silver, the murmured laughter over wine, the soft, protective cushion of money drawn between Nettie and the grime she had abandoned. Yet beneath his rage there stirred a colder, more desolate perception—that even if he struck them down, he could never quite extinguish the memory of how easily she had preferred that sunlit ease to the dark, demanding hunger of his own devotion.

Shaphambury, when he stepped from the terminus into the salt-scented dusk, presented a spectacle of nervous, holiday-clad suspension. Along the esplanade, the string of coloured lamps trembled in the sea-breeze, and beneath them moved a crowd unusually silent, pausing in knots around the newsboys whose hoarse cries of war and ultimatums cut across the gentle, rhythmic sigh of the tide. Out over the Channel, the comet hung prodigious—a vast, pale-emerald apparition whose light seemed to mingle with the water, turning the breaking foam to a ghostly, phosphorescent silver. Leadford walked the promenade with his collar turned up, a spare, threadbare figure moving like an uninvited shadow among the flannelled loungers and the rustling silks of evening dresses. It did not take him long to find the house; it stood slightly apart on the rising cliff-road, its French windows open to a small, walled garden that looked down upon the sea. From the shelter of a tamarisk hedge, he saw them. They were sitting on the terrace after dinner, the warm glow of a shaded lamp behind them, the green starlight before. Verrall was leaning back in a wicker chair, a cigarette glowing faintly between his fingers, his posture eloquent of a serene, unhurried possession; while Nettie, wrapped in a light shawl, sat near him, her face turned toward the sea with an expression of quiet, almost breathless wonder. Watching them, Leadford felt the whole edifice of his injury rise up within him, vast and cold. They were so terribly at peace, so insolently sheltered from the storm gathering over Europe and the darker storm he carried in his own breast.

They came down the cliff-path half an hour later, drawn, no doubt, by the strange, compelling beauty of the night. Leadford stepped out from the shadow of the gorse just where the path narrowed above the shingle. Nettie saw him first; she stopped with a small, choked intake of breath, her hand flying to her throat as though she had encountered not a man, but the embodied reproach of her own past. Verrall, after a single startled instant, placed himself slightly before her—not with melodramatic bravado, Leadford bitterly observed, but with the instinctive, practiced poise of one accustomed to dealing with awkwardness. “Leadford,” Verrall said quietly, his voice carrying a note of genuine, if slightly fatigued, concern. “You shouldn’t have come here. There is nothing to be gained by this.” Nettie’s eyes, wide in the emerald gloom, fixed upon Leadford’s face with a pleading, terrified pity that stung him far worse than hatred. “Willie,” she whispered, “oh, Willie, go home.” The words, so small and familiar, broke the last fragile barrier of his restraint. He drew his hand from his pocket, the dull metal catching a pale gleam from the sky. “Home?” he repeated, his voice trembling with the accumulated humiliation of months. “You’ve left me none, Nettie. And as for you—” He levelled the barrel toward Verrall’s white shirt-front, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. Yet even as his finger tightened, a singular, sweet-scented coolness seemed to drift up from the sea, a faint, greenish mist that curled softly around their ankles and dimmed the sharp outline of Verrall’s face.

It was not like fog, which chills and blinds, but rather like a gentle, luminous exhalation of the earth itself—a subtle, aromatic vapour that crept across the shingle and rose along the cliff-face with an almost tender deliberation. Leadford blinked, his arm growing strangely heavy, as if the iron of the revolver had suddenly been weighted with lead. Across the bay, the distant murmur of the town—the shouted headlines, the restless shuffle of anxious crowds—seemed to sink into a profound, cushioned hush. Verrall did not flinch; instead, he swayed slightly, passing a hand across his brow with a bewildered, half-smiling sigh, while Nettie sank softly to her knees upon the turf, her eyes closing as though an intolerable burden had been lifted from her shoulders. Leadford tried to hold his anger, to summon back the sharp, sustaining edge of his grievance, yet the emotion slipped from his grasp like water through open fingers. A marvellous, lucid drowsiness was stealing through his veins, dissolving the knotted bitterness around his heart. He saw, in one last, extraordinarily clear flash of perception before his knees gave way, that the green mist was not merely enveloping them upon this narrow English cliff, but was pouring silently over the entire spinning globe—over the armoured trains halted at the frontiers, over the admirals on their bridges, over the emperors in their gilded rooms. Then the turf rose gently to meet him; the pistol dropped from his relaxed hand into the grass; and beside the girl he had meant to punish and the man he had meant to kill, William Leadford fell into a deep, dreamless, and utterly unburdened sleep.

When he woke, the world appeared to have been washed in a clearer, more generous light. Above the Channel, the sun had risen into a sky of pale, translucent gold, and the green vapour had vanished, leaving only a faint, intoxicating freshness in the air, like bruised mint after rain. Leadford sat up slowly, looking first at the small revolver lying harmlessly among the daisies, and then at his two companions, who were stirring upon the grass beside him. There was no terror in Nettie’s face as she met his eyes—only a quiet, wondering candour, as if she were seeing him for the first time without the distorting glass of his poverty or her own vanity. Verrall, too, rose to his feet, brushing the damp earth from his coat with an air that had lost every trace of its former effortless superiority; he looked, Leadford thought, extraordinarily human, slightly dishevelled, and profoundly humble. “I am sorry, Leadford,” Verrall said, and the words carried no longer the smooth tone of the gentleman, but the unadorned gravity of one man speaking to another. “We were all blind, I think—blind and terribly cruel.” Leadford stood up, feeling in his chest an unfamiliar spaciousness, as though a tight iron band had snapped in the night. Looking down toward Shaphambury, he saw people emerging into the sunlit streets, pausing to speak to one another with gentle, astonished gestures, the abandoned newspapers fluttering unheeded in the gutters. The war, he knew, would not happen; the old, cramped world of grasping pride and jealous possession had dissolved. He picked up the revolver, walked to the cliff’s edge, and tossed it far out into the bright, indifferent sea; then he turned back to Nettie and Verrall, ready, at last, to begin.
