It had come to William Leadford, in the small, soot-stained back room that overlooked the cinder-heaps of Clayton, with the peculiar, almost insolent neatness of a blow delivered in the dark. He sat for a long time with Nettie’s folded note between his fingers, turning it over as if the cheap, cream-laid paper might yet yield some hidden margin, some mitigating postscript that his first, stunned perusal had missed. There was, of course, nothing of the sort; there was only the bare, breathless declaration that she had gone with Edward Verrall, and that William must try—the phrase struck him as a masterpiece of unconscious cruelty—to forgive her. Outside, the Staffordshire dusk was thickening, though not with the honest, familiar blackness of the furnaces; a strange, pale, greenish lustre seemed to hang in the upper air, a faint, spectral reflection of the great comet that had, for weeks past, been climbing the northern sky. Beside his elbow lay the evening paper, its columns swollen with the furious rhetoric of an impending war—Ultimatums, Mobilisations, the ironclads steaming through the North Sea—yet to Leadford, in the narrow, suffocating theatre of his own humiliation, the convulsions of Europe appeared merely as a noisy, irrelevant accompaniment to his private ruin. He had loved her with a fierce, possessive concentration that had been, in its way, his sole refuge from the grinding poverty of his clerkship; and that she should have been gathered up, with so effortless a grace, by the young gentleman from the Hall, seemed to him an outrage not merely upon his heart, but upon the very decency of the universe.

By the following afternoon, the small, heavy object in his coat pocket—purchased from a pawnshop in Swadlincote with the last of his meager savings—had ceased to feel like an alien weight and had become, instead, the very centre of his physical consciousness. The train that bore him southward, toward the distant Norfolk coast where he had traced them through a careless word dropped by Verrall’s groom, rattled through a countryside strangely hushed beneath the afternoon glare. In the third-class carriage opposite him, two commercial travellers sat with their newspapers spread wide, debating, with an air of solemn, proprietary relish, the certainty of a general conflict before the week was out. Leadford listened to them without speaking, his eyes fixed upon the flying hedgerows, struck by the grotesque irony of their preoccupation. They spoke of national honour, of violated borders, of the inevitable reckoning with an insolent rival, and it occurred to him, with a bitter, lucid clarity, that the whole world was, at that precise moment, nursing a grievance as blind and as murderous as his own. The comet, though invisible in the bright, brassy daylight, seemed already to press upon the atmosphere, lending a feverish edge to every voice. Yet, when he touched the cold iron through the worn cloth of his pocket, he felt no kinship with the blustering patriots across from him; his own purpose was, he told himself, altogether cleaner, altogether more intimate—a matter of simple, terrible justice between a man who had nothing and a man who had taken the single thing he possessed.

Shaphambury, when he stepped out upon its neat, gravelled platform in the late afternoon, presented to his darkened spirit an aspect of almost unbearable serenity. It was a place, he perceived at once, constructed entirely for the ease of those who had never known the smell of wet coal-smoke or the indignity of a frayed cuff; its white-painted villas faced a sea of astonishing, tranquil blue, and the very air carried a crisp, saline sweetness that seemed to mock the grime he had brought with him from the Midlands. He made his way along the esplanade with the stiff, watchful gait of an intruder, his gaze sweeping the sunlit terraces of the Grand Hotel, until, with a sudden, sickening contraction of the heart, he saw them. They were sitting at a small wicker table near the balustrade, partially screened by a striped awning, and the sheer, effortless harmony of their attitude struck him like a physical blow. Verrall, in his pale flannels, leaned forward with a look of amused, proprietary tenderness, while Nettie—a Nettie transformed, he noted with a pang, by a broad-brimmed hat of fine straw and a gown of some soft, floating muslin—laughed up at him, her hand resting lightly upon the table. They looked, to Leadford’s aching eyes, not like two guilty fugitives, but like figures belonging by natural right to that bright, leisured world, as if their flight had been nothing more sordid than a charming excursion. He turned away before she could glance toward the railing, his fingers clenching in his pocket, overwhelmed by the cruel realization that his very presence there was an absurdity, a dusty blot upon their sunlit picture.

He did not approach them that evening, held back less by any failure of nerve than by a strange, fastidious desire to choose his moment with a certain grim propriety. Instead, he wandered out beyond the town, climbing the steep, grassy shoulder of the cliffs as the light faded, until the esplanade and its twinkling lamps lay far below him like a scatter of discarded jewels. Above the dark horizon of the sea, the comet had risen in terrifying splendour. It was no longer a mere distant smear of light, but a vast, luminous fan of pale emerald, stretching halfway across the zenith and casting upon the turf, upon his own shabby hands, upon the silent, heaving water below, an unearthly, spectral glow. Leadford sat upon a wooden bench, watching it, and felt, for the first time, a curious, cold detachment steal over his frenzy. He turned the whole question of Nettie’s treachery over in his mind, examining it from every angle with a painful, almost scholarly precision. Had she ever truly understood what she meant to him, or had she merely accepted his devotion as one accepts the warmth of a fire, only to step away when a brighter, more commodious hearth was offered? He thought of the war that was, even now, perhaps beginning in the distant cities of Europe, of the millions of men marching under that same livid sky, and he wondered whether the approaching star was not, in truth, some immense, indifferent eye, looking down upon the madness of the earth. Yet, when he rose at last in the chill hours before dawn, his resolve had only hardened into a cold, metallic certainty.

He found them shortly after sunrise, on a lonely stretch of the shingle where the cliffs curved inward, hidden from the town and its waking windows. They had come down early to walk by the water, and when Leadford stepped out from behind a cluster of dark groynes, blocking their path, the silence between them was broken only by the gentle, rhythmic hiss of the retreating tide. Nettie’s small, sharp cry of recognition seemed to hang in the still, strangely heavy air, while Verrall, after a single, startled glance, stepped instinctively forward, placing himself slightly before her with an air of stiff, gentlemanly composure. Leadford did not speak; he felt that words had long since lost their currency between them, and he simply drew the revolver from his pocket, holding it at his side, yet raised enough for its meaning to be unmistakable. For a long, suspended moment, they stood thus, three figures caught in an absurd, tragic tableau beneath a sky that had begun to turn a singular, opaque shade of green. Verrall’s face was pale, yet his eyes met Leadford’s with a steady, almost contemptuous inquiry; Nettie stood motionless, her hand pressed to her throat, looking at William with an expression in which terror was strangely mingled with a kind of helpless pity. Then, even as Leadford felt his thumb tighten upon the hammer, a vast, silent wall of mist—thick, luminous, and smelling faintly of some unknown, sweetish spice—rolled swiftly across the water, enveloping them in a sudden, profound darkness. Leadford tried to raise his arm, to shout, to cling to his rage, but his knees gave way beneath him, and he sank down upon the pebbles into an abyss of dreamless sleep.

When consciousness returned to him, it came not with the jolt of an ordinary waking, but like the slow, luminous opening of a window in a long-shuttered room. Leadford lay upon his back on the cool shingle, gazing up into a sky of the purest, most crystalline blue he had ever beheld, washed clean of the livid greenish haze of the night before. For several minutes he did not move, content merely to breathe the air, which seemed to possess an extraordinary, intoxicating lightness, as though every particle of dust and bitterness had been purged from the atmosphere. Gradually, he turned his head. A few paces away, his revolver lay half-buried in the pebbles, its dark iron looking curiously small, almost childish, in the bright morning light. Beyond it, Verrall and Nettie were still sleeping, side by side upon the stones, their faces pale and relaxed, stripped of all the tension and artifice that had marked them only hours—or was it centuries?—ago. Leadford sat up slowly, and as he did so, he experienced a sensation so profound, so entirely novel, that he could only stare at his own hands in silent wonder. The dark, suffocating knot of jealousy and wounded pride that had formed the very core of his being for weeks past had simply vanished. He looked at Nettie—at the curve of her cheek, the tumbled strands of her hair—and felt no stab of possessive anguish, no desire to punish; he felt instead a vast, almost impersonal compassion for her, and, stranger still, for the young man beside her. It was as if he had stepped out of a smoky cellar into the open day, marvelling that he had ever mistaken its shadows for the whole of life.

Presently, Verrall stirred, sat up with a bewildered frown, and looked about him, his eyes resting first upon the revolver in the shingle and then upon Leadford’s quiet, attentive face. A moment later Nettie also woke, rising slowly to her knees, her gaze moving from one man to the other with a dawning, incredulous awe. Neither of them spoke of fear, nor did Verrall make any movement toward the weapon; it seemed as if the unspoken conventions of yesterday had dissolved as completely as the green mist itself. "We have been asleep," Verrall murmured at last, his voice stripped of its former easy arrogance, like that of a man remembering a long-forgotten truth. "And the comet," Nettie added softly, looking up at the empty, radiant sky, "it has passed." Leadford rose to his feet, brushing the sand from his worn trousers, and looked down at them with a faint, grave smile. He felt no impulse to utter reproaches, nor did he expect any from them; there was nothing left to forgive, because the very notion of ownership—of one soul laying violent claim to another—now appeared to him as a pathetic, vanished delusion. "I came here to kill you," Leadford said simply, not with shame, but with the quiet wonder of a man recounting a stranger’s dream. Verrall nodded slowly, his eyes clear and steady. "I know," he replied. "And I suppose, back there in the world, they are still trying to kill each other—whole nations of them, marching in the dark." They stood together on the bright shore, three small figures facing the sparkling sea, united by a lucid certainty that the dark age of their old selves—and of the world’s blind fury—had ended, and that whatever awaited them beyond the cliffs would require a new, and far gentler, language.
