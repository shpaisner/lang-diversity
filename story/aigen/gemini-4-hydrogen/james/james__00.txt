It was not merely the soot of Swathinglea that settled upon William Leadford’s spirit during those suffocating weeks of late summer, though the grime of the Potteries—infiltrating, as it did, the very creases of his cheap serge waistcoat and the margins of his ledger—served well enough as the outward figure of his condition. Rather, it was the exquisite, almost intolerable sharpness with which he perceived his own insignificance against the vast, indifferent canvas of things. In the cramped parlour behind his mother’s tiny shop, where the scent of damp wool and boiled cabbage hung perpetually in the dusk, he would sit by the narrow window and watch the new star—the green comet, as the papers had begun, with a kind of nervous jocularity, to call it—hanging low above the smoking chimneys. It had the look, to his fevered fancy, of a cold, emerald eye regarding the squalor of his defeat. For Nettie was gone. She had not, indeed, been torn from him by any vulgar catastrophe; she had simply drifted, with a grace that seemed to him the very perfection of cruelty, into the orbit of Edward Verrall. That young gentleman, whose leisure was as boundless as William’s hours at the colliery office were pinched, had needed scarcely a fortnight of afternoon walks and murmured pleasantries to accomplish what William’s years of dogged, silent devotion had failed to secure. The brief note she had left—all timid evasions and delicate regrets—lay folded in his pocket like a warrant, and as he stared at the luminous smudge in the heavens, he felt the slow, dark gathering of a resolution that was, in its own miserable way, his sole remaining claim to dignity.

The revolver, when he came at last to purchase it from a dusty pawnbroker in Hanley, weighed less in his palm than he had somehow anticipated, yet it possessed, for all its cheap nickel plating, an extraordinary power of simplifying his thoughts. With the weapon tucked into the lining of his greatcoat, he boarded the southward train on a Friday afternoon, leaving his mother’s bewildered, tearless farewell behind him like a chapter he had neither the patience nor the courage to reread. The third-class carriage was stifling, thick with the odour of stale tobacco and the rustle of evening newspapers. Across from him, two stout commercial travellers were engaged in a heated, almost gleeful dissection of the latest dispatches from the Continent: there had been an incident in the North Sea, a mobilization along the Rhine, an exchange of ultimatums so curt and uncompromising that war, they agreed, was now merely a matter of hours. William listened to their booming certainties with a strange, detached sympathy. It seemed to him entirely natural, indeed almost reassuring, that the great capitals of Europe should be nursing their wounded pride toward an irrevocable explosion. He, too, was an aggrieved power; he, too, had suffered an infringement of his rightful territory that no diplomatic formula could mend. As the train hurtled past the flat, darkening meadows of the Midlands, the green comet rose once more above the horizon, visibly larger now, trailing a faint, phosphorescent plume across the twilight, as though the sky itself were leaning down to witness the general madness.

By the time he reached Shaphambury, the air had altered from the coal-smoke of the north to a salt-laden brilliance that made him acutely, painfully aware of his frayed cuffs and travel-stained boots. Everything in this seaside refuge seemed arranged to celebrate an indolence he had never known: the whitewashed villas perched above the promenade, the scent of tamarisk and warm gravel, the unhurried murmur of voices from shaded verandas. It required no great ingenuity to discover where Verrall had taken her; a discreet inquiry at the stationer’s, coupled with a shilling he could ill afford, directed him to a secluded bungalow overlooking the shingle cove. Lingering behind a low stone wall choked with sea-thrift, William watched them for nearly an hour before dusk. They sat together on the terrace, Nettie in a pale muslin frock he had never seen, her dark hair loosened by the breeze, and Verrall leaning back in a wicker chair with that effortless, sunlit composure which William had always hated more than any active malice. They were not even speaking much; their silence was the silence of people who had nothing to prove, nothing to defend, no anxious account to render to the morrow. That, more than any embrace, pierced him to the quick. To be so utterly excluded from the mere ease of their existence—to stand in the dust of the lane like a beggar peering through a lighted window—filled him with a cold, methodical fury. He waited until the lamps were lit inside and the two figures rose to wander down the narrow cliff path toward the water.

He intercepted them where the path flattened onto the coarse shingle, beneath a sky that had grown eerily luminous with the comet’s immense, greenish arc. Nettie saw him first; she stopped so abruptly that her hand flew to her throat, though she uttered no cry. Verrall, turning with a slight, quizzical frown, took in William’s gaunt figure and dusty coat with a polite bewilderment that was almost worse than hostility. “Leadford?” he murmured, stepping half a pace in front of her, as if to shield her from an unpleasantness of the merely social sort. “I scarcely—is something amiss at home?” The sheer, unassailable propriety of the question struck William like a blow across the face. “You have a remarkable way,” William heard himself say, his voice thin and trembling in the salt wind, “of supposing that nothing can be amiss unless it concerns your own convenience.” Nettie took a quick step forward, her eyes dark with a mixture of pity and dread. “Willie, please,” she began, “I wrote to you—I tried to make you see—” “You made me see everything,” William interrupted, his fingers closing tightly around the cold grip inside his pocket. “You made me see what a man of my sort is worth when a gentleman takes a fancy to what belongs to him.” Verrall’s jaw tightened, yet his tone remained maddeningly level, even gentle. “She belongs to no one, Leadford. Surely you must see that to follow us here, in this fashion, can only distress her.” The reasonableness of it, the smooth, impregnable fortress of their mutual understanding, drove the last vestige of hesitation from William’s mind.

He drew the revolver with a sudden, clumsy violence, though even as the barrel caught the pale green shimmer from the sky, he was dimly aware that he did not truly wish to kill them—he wished only to shatter, once and for all, the invisible glass that separated his suffering from their serenity. Nettie gave a sharp, muffled gasp and clutched Verrall’s sleeve; Verrall himself did not flinch, though the colour drained swiftly from his face, leaving him looking unexpectedly young and vulnerable in the spectral light. “Put that down, you fool,” Verrall breathed, taking a measured step forward. “Don’t move,” William cried, his own voice cracking strangely against the hush of the tide. He raised the weapon, sighting along the cheap metal toward Verrall’s breast, his thumb drawing back the hammer with a dry, metallic click. At that precise instant, however, the air around them underwent an inexplicable, velvet transformation. It was not a wind, for the dry grasses on the cliff remained motionless, yet a vast, cool tide of pale emerald vapour seemed to well up from the sea itself, swallowing the horizon, muffling the distant wash of the surf, and filling their nostrils with a faint, indescribable sweetness, like crushed mint and rain-washed stone. William’s arm grew abruptly heavy, as though weighted with lead. He saw Nettie’s knees buckle gently, saw Verrall reach out to catch her only to sink beside her onto the shingle, his eyes closing without fear or struggle. A profound, dreamless languor dissolved the tension in William’s fingers; the revolver slipped from his grasp, clattering faintly against the pebbles as he, too, subsided into the luminous dark.

When William opened his eyes, the morning had already broken, yet it was a morning such as he had never in all his twenty-four years beheld. The sky above the Channel was neither harsh nor blinding, but possessed a lucid, crystalline depth, as though some ancient film of dust and smoke had been washed forever from the atmosphere. Sitting up upon the chill pebbles, he looked down at his own hands, then at the cheap revolver lying a yard away in the damp seaweed, and he experienced a sensation of the strangest, most liberating astonishment. It was not merely that his rage had vanished; it was that the entire architecture of his former misery—the cramped pride, the jealous counting of slights, the sour conviction that his life was a small, cheated thing—had become wholly unintelligible to him. He felt as one might feel upon waking from a long, suffocating fever into a cool, high-ceilinged room. Looking back at the William Leadford who had boarded the train at Swathinglea with murder in his coat pocket, he felt neither horror nor self-pity, only a quiet, wondering compassion for a creature so blinded by the dark. The sea before him was a vast, silver-green mirror, utterly calm; the comet itself had faded into the clear blue of day, leaving behind it this inexplicable grace. He picked up the revolver by the barrel, walked a few paces to the edge of the tide, and tossed it far out into the gentle, lapping water, watching the small splash with a smile of complete, unburdened peace.

When he turned back toward the shingle bank, Nettie and Verrall were already stirring, blinking at the radiance of the morning with the same bewildered, gentle candour he had just discovered in himself. There was no awkwardness in their meeting, none of that strained theatricality which only last night had seemed the sole language possible between them. Nettie stood up, brushing the sand from her muslin skirt, and looked at William with eyes so clear and unafraid that he felt a sudden, quiet warmth in his chest. “Willie,” she said simply, “we were all of us mad yesterday, weren’t we?” “I think we were asleep,” William answered, and found that he meant it with every fibre of his being. Verrall approached them, his coat rumpled, his usual studied nonchalance replaced by an earnest, almost boyish humility. He extended his hand—not with the condescension of a gentleman pardoning an inferior, but with the frankness of a fellow-survivor—and William clasped it without hesitation. “I am sorry for my part in the blindness,” Verrall said quietly. “And I for mine,” William replied. As they walked together up the cliff path toward the bungalow, a postman on a bicycle paused at the gate above them, calling out in a voice filled with wondering excitement that the morning wires had brought extraordinary news: the fleets in the North Sea had turned back without firing a shot, the armies along the Rhine were laying down their rifles, and all of Europe seemed to have woken from its murderous dream. William listened, looking out over the glittering, pacified sea, and knew that his own long war—with Nettie, with Verrall, with the soot and bitterness of his own heart—had ended in the same quiet dawn.
