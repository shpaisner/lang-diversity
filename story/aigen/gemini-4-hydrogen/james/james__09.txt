It was not merely that Nettie had vanished from the narrow, soot-stained theatre of their common existence, though that alone might have sufficed to darken the already sufficiently dismal skies of Staffordshire; it was rather the intolerable, the almost exquisite refinement of her desertion that kept William Leadford pacing the cinder-paths above the potteries. He was a young man whose poverty had never quite succeeded in dulling his consciousness, and this very lucidity—this unfortunate gift for perceiving the exact degree of his own insignificance—now turned inward like a blade. All about him, the kilns belched their sullen, perpetual incense into the twilight, while high above the murky horizon there hung, with an air of detached and luminous irony, the strange green star that had of late set the newspapers to chattering. William, however, had little patience for celestial portents. His universe had contracted to the humiliating compass of a single betrayal, to the image of a girl whose small, bright beauty had seemed, for one brief season, the solitary justification of his cramped and arduous days. That she should have preferred the effortless, the almost insolently graceful Verrall was a fact that William’s mind turned over and over, seeking some flaw in the logic of it, some crevice through which his wounded pride might still force a claim. Yet there was no crevice; there was only the cold, unyielding surface of a social order that had, as it were, simply plucked her from his grasp without even the courtesy of a struggle.

He had, in the extremity of his isolation, acquired a small, inexpensive revolver—an object whose blunt, metallic finality offered a curious comfort to his bruised imagination. It lay now in the pocket of his shabby coat, a secret weight that seemed to restore to him some measure of the consequence he had lost. Verrall, he knew, belonged to that fortunate tribe for whom the world was an arranged and deferential garden; the young gentleman had never been required to count his coppers, nor to inhale the sulphurous breath of the Midland furnaces, nor to feel, in every fibre of his being, the silent, crushing pressure of an inferior station. That Nettie had gone with him to the coast—to some breezy, expensive haven where the air smelled only of brine and leisure—was a certainty William had pieced together from a dropped envelope and a servant’s careless hint. He did not deceive himself as to the figure he would cut in such a setting. He saw himself already, with a sharp, painful detachment, as the gaunt, ill-dressed intruder, the discordant note in their harmonious idyll. Yet it was precisely this discrepancy that hardened his resolve. If he could not command their respect, he could at least shatter their serenity; he could compel them, for one terrible, suspended moment, to reckon with the reality of his existence. Behind him, his mother’s small house crouched in the gathering gloom, a monument to patient, uncomplaining defeat, and William swore to himself, with a quietness more dreadful than any oath, that he would not inherit her submission.

The journey southward on the following afternoon was accomplished amidst a curious, feverish agitation that seemed to have infected the very upholstery of the third-class carriage. Europe, it appeared, was at last sliding down the long, greased incline toward war; the morning editions, rustled by anxious clerks and red-faced commercial travellers, screamed of ultimatums, of mobilized legions, of ironclads steaming through the grey reaches of the North Sea. William listened to their disjointed, belligerent murmurings with a cold, superior detachment. What were their empires and their treaties to him, whose own small kingdom had already been partitioned without his consent? Yet as the train hurtled past quiet pastures and into the deepening dusk, he could not wholly escape the sense that his private desperation was somehow in league with the larger madness of the hour. When he stepped at last onto the platform of the seaside terminus, the air was mild and salt-laden, a startling contrast to the acrid fog he had left behind. Looking upward, he saw that the green luminary had grown monstrous, swelling into a pale, emerald blur that seemed to drain the surrounding stars of their accustomed fire. Down the gas-lit promenade, newsboys were running, their shrill cries cutting through the polite murmur of evening strollers. The crisis, they shouted, had broken; the guns were speaking across the water. William merely thrust his hand deeper into his pocket, his fingers closing around the cold iron, and turned his steps toward the cliff-side hotels.

He found them, after an hour of patient, bitter reconnaissance, emerging from the lighted portico of the Grand Hotel, and the sheer, unstudied ease of their appearance struck him almost as a physical blow. Verrall wore evening dress with the negligent grace of one to whom such attire was a second skin, while Nettie—his Nettie, though she seemed already translated into a finer, more elusive element—walked beside him in a pale cloak that caught the strange, greenish radiance of the night. They were conversing in low, amused tones, their heads inclined toward one another with an intimacy so complete, so exquisitely insulated from the crude anxieties of the world, that William felt himself shrink into a mere phantom watching from the shadows of the tamarisk bushes. There was in Nettie’s carriage no trace of the constrained, slightly defiant girl who had walked the Staffordshire lanes; she had, with an adaptability that William found both marvelous and unforgivable, absorbed the very rhythm of Verrall’s leisure. They paused near the balustrade overlooking the dark sea, and Verrall gestured upward toward the vast, glowing comet that now dominated the eastern sky. Even at that distance, William could hear the light, silvery ripple of Nettie’s laughter—a sound so devoid of guilt, so serenely indifferent to the ruin she had left behind her, that the last vestige of William’s hesitation evaporated. They were turning now toward the quieter path that wound along the headland, away from the bright windows and the distant, muffled uproar of the town.

The headland was deserted, save for the three of them and the vast, unnatural illumination that poured from the heavens. Far below, the sea broke in long, phosphorescent lines, while from the town behind them came the faint, intermittent clamour of bells and shouting—the distant, hysterical chorus of a nation plunging into slaughter. William followed at a measured pace, keeping to the soft turf beside the gravel path so that his heavy boots might make no sound. His heart beat with a slow, suffocating regularity, yet his mind had never been clearer; he felt himself to be the sole sane instrument in a world given over to folly and betrayal. When Verrall and Nettie halted at the edge of the down, where a wooden bench faced the open channel, William stepped out from the shadow of a gorse bush and stood directly in their path. Nettie’s small, sharp intake of breath was audible even above the sighing of the wind; Verrall, after a single startled glance, drew himself up with that instinctive, infuriating composure of his class. "Leadford?" the young gentleman murmured, his tone conveying less of fear than of a fastidious distaste for an unseemly intrusion. William did not answer immediately; he drew the revolver from his pocket, watching the pale green light glint along its barrel. He meant to speak—to articulate, at last, the whole accumulated weight of his wrong—yet as he opened his lips, he became abruptly aware that the air around them had grown strangely thick, heavy with a faint, sweetish odour that seemed to numb the very springs of his anger.

It was as though an invisible, emerald curtain had been drawn across the world, muffling at once the distant cries of the war-maddened town and the sharp, agonizing tension between the three figures on the cliff. William tried to raise his arm, to force his fingers to tighten upon the trigger, yet a profound, inexplicable languor was stealing through his limbs. Before his eyes, the neat, arrogant silhouette of Verrall seemed to waver; the young gentleman raised a hand to his forehead, swayed slightly, and sank without a word onto the damp turf beside the bench. Nettie gave a low, bewildered cry—not of terror, William dimly perceived, but of a sudden, overwhelming weariness—and crumpled gracefully beside him, her pale cloak spreading like a fallen wing upon the grass. William stood for a moment longer, swaying in the thick, luminous mist that now enveloped the headland, a mist that tasted faintly of almonds and summer rain. His fierce, narrow grievance, which had sustained him across half of England, seemed abruptly to slip from his grasp, dissolving into the vast, chemical hush that was settling over the earth. The revolver dropped from his nerveless fingers, striking a stone with a dull, insignificant clink. He sank to his knees, his consciousness unravelling softly, thread by thread, until there remained only a sense of immense, tranquil suspension, as if he and his enemies—and indeed the whole warring, frantic world—were being gently lowered into an unplumbed sea of sleep.

When William opened his eyes, the world had been washed clean of its old, familiar murk. The morning sun stood high above a sea of astonishing, crystalline clarity, and the air he drew into his lungs was sharper, sweeter, and infinitely more lucid than any he had ever known. For a long moment he sat upon the grass, looking down at the small, absurd weapon lying beside his knee, and a curious, almost painful sense of wonder took possession of him. It was not that he had forgotten the past—he remembered Staffordshire, the kilns, his bitter poverty, and the feverish journey southward—but he beheld them now as one beholds the shapes of a dream from which one has awakened. A few paces away, Verrall was stirring, sitting up with an expression of bewildered, gentle gravity, while Nettie, leaning upon her elbow, looked from one man to the other with eyes that seemed newly opened to the light. There was no awkwardness between them, none of the cramped vanity that had hitherto governed their lives. William rose, and with a quietness that felt entirely natural, offered his hand to help his rival to his feet. Verrall took it without hesitation, his gaze meeting William’s in a moment of profound, unspoken recognition—a recognition of their shared, fragile humanity, and of the immense, comical smallness of their former strife. Below them, the town lay silent and bathed in morning gold; the frantic bells had ceased, the rumours of war had evaporated like an evil vapour, and as the three of them stood together upon the headland, looking out across the tranquil water, it seemed to William that life had only just, and with an infinite tenderness, begun.
