It was not merely that the air of Clayton had grown, during those breathless weeks of August, thicker than ever with the soot of the Potteries, nor even that the evening sky had begun to wear, above the silhouette of the bottle-kilns, a strange, pale, emerald stain; it was rather that William Leadford felt himself, in the very marrow of his impecunious being, to have been pushed to the absolute rim of endurance.

His mother, moving with her accustomed, apologetic hush between the scullery and the narrow table, offered him the silent tribute of a sympathy she dared not utter, knowing too well how fiercely his pride—that fierce, brittle pride of the clever young man condemned to twelve shillings a week and a ledger—would have resented the lightest touch of pity. For Nettie was gone. She had slipped from the dull, respectable cage of her father’s cottage not with the dramatic crash of a tragedy, which William might have met with a corresponding grandeur of despair, but with the smooth, almost insolent ease of a creature who had simply found a warmer climate. And the climate, as all the world of Swathinglea knew by now, had a name: it was young Verrall, the son of the great house, whose motor-car had been seen, on two successive Sundays, lingering with an air of negligent proprietorship near the edge of the common. William sat by the window, his elbows upon the oilcloth, and watched the green star swell in the twilight. That it was a comet, a wanderer of prodigious and indifferent sweep, the halfpenny papers assured him daily, sandwiched between their shrill, terrified prophecies of a European war; yet to William, in the narrow chamber of his humiliation, the celestial visitor seemed to hang there solely as a cold, ironic witness to his own eclipse.

The little revolver he had purchased in Birmingham lay heavy against his thigh, an object at once absurdly theatrical and terribly concrete, as though he had borrowed a property from some melodrama only to find it had grown, in the heat of his pocket, into a principle of justice. He had discovered their refuge—Shaphambury, on the eastern coast, a place of salt breezes and whitewashed terraces where, as he bitterly imagined, the rich went to be fanned by an obsequious sea.

On the morning of his departure, the station platform at Clayton was crowded with men whose faces wore a curious, glazed excitement; they held open newspapers whose headlines screamed of ultimata, of the German fleet steaming through the grey North Sea, of mobilization and the imminent crash of empires. William, boarding the third-class carriage with his small, battered bag, felt for these public convulsions a profound, almost weary contempt. What were the jealousies of emperors beside the monstrous, intimate theft that had been practiced upon him? Yet, as the train rattled southward through the parched Midlands, he perceived, with a kind of lucidity that was itself a torture, that he and the nations were sick of the very same fever—a blind, suffocating determination to strike, to shatter, to make some irreparable gesture rather than endure the slow rot of an intolerable arrangement. The green light of the comet, even in the noon glare, seemed to tinge the dust rising from the embankments, lending to the whole anxious island the look of a stage set for an act of violence.

Shaphambury, when he reached it in the late afternoon, presented to his jaundiced eye an aspect of almost insulting serenity. Along the esplanade, gentlemen in white flannel and ladies under pale parasols strolled with the unhurried grace of persons for whom the world had been expressly upholstered, their voices floating across the shingle with a soft, modulated confidence that struck William as a personal affront. He walked among them in his dusty serge suit, conscious of the grease-spot on his lapel and the sharp, angular hunger of his own profile, feeling less like a rival than like a ghost permitted, by some oversight, to haunt a banquet.

Even here, however, the double shadow of the hour had begun to fall. Groups of men lingered by the bandstand, their laughter clipped short as they bent over the evening editions, while out beyond the pier, where the sea lay flat and metallic as a sheet of lead, the eastern horizon was already steeped in that uncanny, emerald luminance. He found the address without difficulty—a neat, gabled villa set back behind tamarisk hedges on the road toward the cliffs. He did not knock; he had no wish to confront a parlor-maid or to rehearse his grievance in a hallway. Instead, he lingered in the salt-scented dusk across the lane, his hand closed around the cold grip in his pocket, waiting with a patience that surprised even himself for the moment when their happiness should step out into the open air.

They appeared at last, not with the furtive haste of culprits, which might have comforted him, but with the easy, murmurous leisure of two people who had nothing in the world to fear save perhaps a chill from the sea-mist. Verrall wore a light overcoat thrown open over evening dress, his fair head bare, his profile turned toward his companion with an attentiveness that was, to William’s fevered scrutiny, infuriatingly tender. And Nettie—ah, Nettie was no longer the girl of the Swathinglea lanes, with her hurried, defensive little laugh and her cheap cotton frock. She moved in a pale, shimmering wrap that caught the greenish twilight, her chin lifted, her whole carriage speaking of a sudden, effortless translation into a sphere where things were soft, and slow, and infinitely forgiving.

William followed them at a distance of fifty paces as they took the cliff path above the darkening water. Every step he took across the short, springy turf was a debate of exquisite bitterness. To shoot Verrall—to blot out that complacent, privileged outline against the sea—would be easy enough; yet a strange, paralyzing lucidity kept his hand in his pocket. He saw, with a clarity that pierced him more sharply than any jealousy, that even if he struck the man down, he could never reclaim the Nettie he had lost, for she had already ceased to exist. What walked before him was a creature who had chosen, with every fiber of her young, hungry nature, the light over the soot.

It was at the crest of the headland, where the path dipped toward a solitary shelter overlooking the bay, that William’s voice broke from him, harsh and unrecognizable even to his own ears. They turned together, startled by the suddenness of his emergence from the gathering gloom, and for a single, suspended instant the three of them stood fixed in a triangle of mute astonishment. Verrall’s hand went instinctively before Nettie, a gesture of protective grace that caused William’s throat to constrict with a final, blinding spasm of hatred. He drew the revolver. He saw Nettie’s lips part, saw the white oval of her face grow rigid with a terror that was not merely for herself, and he felt the cold trigger yield a fraction of an inch beneath his finger.

Yet even as he aimed, the world around them seemed to draw a long, shuddering breath. A vast, silent curtain of green vapor—cool, faintly fragrant, and luminous as pulverized emerald—rolled swiftly in from the sea, swallowing the cliff-edge, the distant lights of the town, and the very stars above them. William tried to shout, to force his will through the sudden, velvet heaviness that invaded his limbs, but the air he drew into his lungs was strangely sweet and dizzying. The pistol slipped from his numb fingers into the grass. He saw Verrall sway, heard Nettie’s faint, bewildered sigh, and then, with a sensation not of falling but of being gently laid down into an immense, forgiving quiet, William sank into the darkness.

When he opened his eyes, the sun was already high, yet the world appeared to have been washed in an element far more clarifying than ordinary daylight. William sat up slowly upon the dewy turf, his hand brushing the little revolver that lay beside him—an object that now seemed to him as quaint, as pitifully irrelevant, as a child’s broken toy. He looked at it for a long moment, marvelling not that he had been prevented from using it, but that he had ever imagined a bullet could mend the intricate, fragile tissue of human affection. A profound, unhurried stillness occupied his mind, as though a smoky lamp that had been guttering there for years had suddenly been extinguished, leaving the chamber open to the morning breeze.

Down in the bay, the sea shimmered with a clear, pale brilliance, and far off along the coast, where yesterday the newspapers had promised the thunder of coastal batteries and the march of armed men, there rose only the peaceful, dispersed smoke of morning fires. He found himself thinking of the great, clumsy empires—of the emperors, the statesmen, the terrified clerks in Clayton—with a sudden, aching tenderness. How absurdly they had all been posturing in the dark, clutching their little grievances, their flags and their revolvers, blind to the simple, generous breadth of the earth! He rose to his feet, brushing the grass from his dusty serge, and turned toward the shelter where two figures were just beginning to stir.

Verrall was the first to rise, supporting himself for a moment against the timber of the shelter before helping Nettie to her feet. When they saw William standing before them in the clear light, there was neither the start of guilt nor the stiffness of social embarrassment; there was only, in the eyes of all three, a quiet, almost solemn wonder, as if they were survivors of a shipwreck who found themselves cast upon the same sunlit shore. It was Nettie who spoke first, her voice stripped of its old defensive sharpness and equally free of its borrowed, fashionable languor.

“Will,” she said simply, “we have been very blind.”

William looked at her—truly looked at her, perhaps, for the first time in his life—and saw not an idol he had been robbed of, nor a prize to be contested, but a young woman whose spirit had sought its own room to breathe, just as his own had suffocated in the soot of Swathinglea.

“We were all in the dark, Nettie,” he answered gently, glancing from her face to Verrall’s, in which the old, negligent arrogance had given place to a look of candid, almost boyish humility.

Verrall stepped forward, not as a patron, nor as a conqueror, but as a man extending his hand to an equal who had shared a terrible peril. “You will walk back with us?” he asked.

William hesitated, looking out over the bright, boundless sea toward the waking world, where so much remained to be unlearned and begun anew. “I will walk with you a little way,” he said, and together, without haste and without fear, the three of them turned their faces toward the town.
