It was not merely that the air of Staffordshire, heavy with the sulfurous exhalations of the blast furnaces, seemed during those weeks to press upon William Leadford’s chest with a quite unprecedented weight; it was rather that the whole cramped, soot-blackened theatre of his existence had suddenly revealed itself as a stage from which the one figure capable of lending it a shred of grace had quietly withdrawn. Nettie was gone. She had slipped from the narrow orbit of their dismal street, from the small, damp parlor where his mother sat eternally mending, and she had done so—this was the exquisite, the intolerable sting—not into some void of mere misfortune, but into the cushioned, sunlit amplitude of young Verrall’s world. Leadford stood by the low brick wall at the end of the lane, his hands thrust deep into pockets that held only a few coppers and a crumpled, twice-read note, and stared upward. Above the ragged silhouettes of the pot-banks, high in the thickened twilight, hung the green star. For months the astronomers had murmured of it, and now it hung there, a pale, prodigious emerald, growing by imperceptible degrees into an object of personal menace. People in the public houses spoke of it in the same breath as the mobilization of the fleets, as though the heavens themselves were conspiring with the chancelleries of Europe to bring about some vast, indiscriminate smash. Leadford felt, in his own tight, bitter breast, a curious sympathy with that impending ruin. Let the powers clash, let the sky fall; what had he left to lose that Verrall had not already, with the effortless insolence of his class, appropriated?

The journey southward, undertaken in the third-class carriage of a rattling, ill-lit train, was for Leadford less a passage through the English shires than a protracted, feverish descent into the darkest recesses of his own grievance. Against his ribs, wrapped in a handkerchief within his pocket, lay the small, cold weight of the revolver he had purchased in Birmingham—an acquisition made with a deliberation so calm that it had almost frightened him. Around him, his fellow travelers rustled the evening editions, their faces, under the feeble oil lamp, stamped with a strange, avid apprehension. Every headline shrieked of ultimatums, of cruisers steaming under sealed orders into the North Sea, of a continent poised upon the brink of an abyss. Yet to Leadford, gazing out at the dark, flying hedgerows, this immense public agitation seemed merely a clumsy, magnified echo of his own private extremity. Europe, like himself, had been pushed beyond the limit of endurance; Europe, like himself, was reaching for its weapon. At each station where the train paused, the green light of the comet—no longer a mere star, but a vast, luminous fan—fell upon the upturned, anxious faces on the platforms, lending them an unearthly, theatrical pallor. He closed his eyes and saw Nettie’s face as he had last beheld it, turned slightly away from him, with that faint, elusive smile he had mistaken for modesty and now recognized as the dawn of her betrayal. He would find them by the sea, where Verrall’s people kept their summer cottage; he would confront them in the full glare of their stolen ease, and make them understand the exact price of a poor man’s pride.

By the following afternoon, Leadford found himself pacing the immaculate, sun-bleached esplanade of the coastal town, painfully conscious of the Staffordshire dust still clinging to the cuffs of his cheap serge suit. Everything here spoke of a leisure so secure, so effortlessly maintained, that his very presence felt like a species of social trespass. White-painted hotels looked out over a sea of polished turquoise; ladies in pale muslins drifted beneath striped parasols, their voices rising in light, unhurried cadences that took no account of coal strikes or impending wars, save as mild tea-table speculation. It was near the stone steps leading down to the shingle that he first saw them, a sight that arrested him like a physical blow. Verrall wore flannels of an immaculate, creamy whiteness, his bare head catching the afternoon sun as he leaned toward Nettie with an air of amused, proprietorial devotion. And Nettie—it was this, perhaps, that pierced Leadford most deeply—was already subtly altered. She wore a straw hat trimmed with a ribbon of pale blue, and as she listened to her companion, she tilted her chin with a little gesture of confident, almost languid grace that Leadford had never once witnessed in the dim, narrow lanes of home. They did not see him; they were enclosed within the invisible, shining bubble of their own sufficiency. Leadford drew back into the shadow of a bathing-machine, his fingers tightening around the hard outline in his pocket. He watched them stroll down toward the quieter stretches of the dunes, and he followed at a measured, relentless distance, his whole being reduced to a single, burning point of injured resolution.

Twilight settled over the coast not with soft English greyness, but with a strange, theatrical suffusion of emerald light that turned the breaking surf to liquid jade and cast long, ghostly shadows across the sand. Leadford had waited until the promenade was nearly deserted, until the hotel lights began to prick the gloom, before he closed the distance between himself and the pair seated in a hollow of the dunes. When he stepped over the crest of the ridge, his boots sinking silently into the loose sand, Verrall was the first to look up, his expression shifting from idle surprise to a sharp, aristocratic annoyance. Nettie’s small cry was swallowed by the vast, hushed murmur of the tide. Leadford did not speak at once; he stood above them, trembling slightly, the revolver drawn and held at his side, drinking in the spectacle of their sudden, helpless exposure. Above his head, the comet now filled the sky—a colossal, silent apparition whose trailing mist seemed almost to brush the dark horizon. Yet even as he raised his arm, even as the rehearsed words rose to his throat, he became aware of an extraordinary alteration in the atmosphere. A faint, penetrating odor, sweeter than anything he had ever known—like crushed thyme and distant rain—drifted across the dunes. A green vapor, luminous and palpable, seemed to descend from the heavens, wrapping the shore in profound stillness. Leadford’s arm grew impossibly heavy; the furious, tight-wound spring within his chest seemed abruptly to slacken. He heard Nettie’s voice, very far away, utter his name without fear, and then, with a sudden, bewildered relief, he sank down upon the cool sand and knew no more.

He woke to a world that appeared to have been washed clean in his absence. The morning light was so exquisitely pellucid that every pebble on the beach, every blade of coarse grass along the ridge, stood out with the precision of a Dutch painting. For several moments Leadford lay motionless, staring up into a sky of cloudless, tender blue from which the green portent had utterly vanished, leaving behind only a memory like that of a fevered dream. When he sat up, he waited for the familiar, gnawing ache of his grievance to reassert itself, for the sour taste of his poverty and his humiliation to flood back into his mouth; yet nothing came. He felt, on the contrary, a lightness of spirit so unprecedented that it bordered on awe. He looked down at the sand beside his knee, where the revolver lay half-buried in the pale grit, its blued barrel dulled by the morning dew. How extraordinarily small, how pathetically clumsy, the thing appeared! That he should have carried it across half of England, that he should have imagined a few grains of lead could mend the fractured dignity of a human soul, struck him now as an aberration so grotesque that he could scarcely recognize the man who had planned it. He rose to his feet, brushing the sand from his trousers, and breathed deeply of the salt air. It was as if a thick, soot-stained pane of glass had been lifted from before his eyes, allowing him, for the first time in his life, to perceive the sheer, ungrudging beauty of the earth without the distorting shadow of his own wounded ego.

A few yards away, in the shallow hollow of the dunes, Verrall and Nettie were also stirring. Leadford watched them as they sat up, blinking in the bright, astonishing morning, and he felt no impulse either to advance in anger or to retreat in shame. Verrall’s white flannels were rumpled and stained with sand, his hair disordered, yet as his eyes met Leadford’s, there was in his gaze neither the haughty resentment of the previous evening nor the slightest trace of fear. Instead, there was a look of profound, almost childlike perplexity, as though he were struggling to recall the rules of a game he no longer wished to play. Nettie, too, looked across at Leadford, and in that quiet, unguarded moment, he saw her as she truly was—not the treacherous angel of his dark imaginings, nor the polished young lady of the esplanade, but simply a frightened, hopeful girl who had reached out clumsily toward a brighter life. She rose slowly, smoothing her skirt with trembling fingers, and walked toward him, Verrall following a pace behind. None of them spoke for a long minute; the silence between them was no longer heavy with unspoken accusations, but open, spacious, and strangely gentle, like the wide, empty shore stretching away on either hand. When Verrall finally extended his hand—not with the patronizing gesture of a gentleman humoring an inferior, nor with the nervous haste of a man seeking to buy off a threat, but simply as one bewildered creature greeting another in the dawn—Leadford took it without hesitation, struck by the simple, undeniable warmth of their common humanity.

They sat together upon a bleached length of driftwood, facing the calm, shimmering expanse of the Channel, and began at last to speak. There was neither dramatic reckoning nor facile reconciliation, for the old terms of their conflict—the jealousy, the class pride, the fierce, possessive claim Leadford had once mistaken for love—seemed to have evaporated in the night like the green mist itself. Leadford spoke first, his voice sounding oddly clear in the quiet air, confessing with a simplicity that astonished him the dark, murderous errand that had brought him south. Nettie listened with her hands clasped in her lap, her eyes fixed upon the horizon; when she answered, it was without the defensive coquetry she had worn of late, acknowledging her own vanity, her own breathless flight from the ugliness of Staffordshire, with an unguarded candor. Verrall, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, murmured that he had scarcely known himself until that moment—that his whole life had been a kind of sleepwalking through inherited privileges. Far out upon the water, a great grey cruiser lay motionless against the morning haze, its guns silent, its smoke-stacks cold; for across the sea, as they would soon learn, the armies had woken to the same clarity, and the emperors and ministers were staring at their maps in sudden, helpless shame. Leadford looked from the quiet ship to the faces of his companions, realizing that he had lost Nettie forever in the old sense, yet gained something infinitely larger. The world before them remained fractured, soiled by ancient folly, and there would be much, he knew, to rebuild; but as the sun climbed higher over the unruffled sea, he felt within himself, for the first time, the quiet, unshakeable patience to begin.
