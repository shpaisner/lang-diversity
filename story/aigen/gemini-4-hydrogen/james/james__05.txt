William Leadford stood at the back gate of the narrow, soot-darkened garden in Swathinglea, his gaze arrested by a sky that had, for the past fortnight, ceased to behave with its accustomed Midland sobriety. A singular, pale viridity, the unmistakable exhalation of the approaching comet, hung above the pottery kilns and the black ridges of the Five Towns, lending even to the meanest brick wall a strange, theatrical pallor. He held in his hand a folded copy of the Daily Post, whose columns, dense with the portents of a European rupture, seemed to him merely the larger, louder echo of his own private ruin. For it had come to him, with a sharpness that made his breath catch, that Nettie was slipping beyond the modest perimeter of his claim. She had not, of course, offered him any crude dismissal; she possessed too delicate an instinct for the oblique, too genuine a reluctance to wound him in the open. Yet there had been the Sunday afternoon at Clayton, the sudden, unannounced appearance of young Verrall from the great house at Checkshill, and the manner in which Nettie’s eyes had lifted—not with the shy, dutiful deference she kept for William’s earnest talk of wages and justice, but with a bright, startled wonder that left him standing, as it were, outside a lighted window. The newspapers spoke of ultimata and mobilized fleets; William, turning the damp sheet in his stained fingers, felt that his own small kingdom was already under siege, and that the green star above the chimneys looked down with an intolerable, indifferent calm.

The confirmation, when it arrived on the following evening, possessed none of the dignity of a tragic revelation; it was, rather, a matter of hurried whispers in a cramped parlour smelling of damp wool and boiled cabbage. Nettie’s mother, Mrs. Stuart, stood by the mantelpiece with her apron twisted about her red knuckles, her eyes darting everywhere save toward the young man who had come to demand his right. Nettie had gone—that much, at least, was extracted from the poor woman’s fluttered evasions—and she had not gone alone. A carriage had been seen at the turning of the lane; a portmanteau had been packed in the grey of the morning; and the name of Verrall, though Mrs. Stuart could scarcely bring herself to pronounce it without a nervous glance at the ceiling, hung between them like the smoke of an extinguished lamp. William listened with a stillness that surprised even himself, though beneath his rigid waistcoat his heart beat with a thick, suffocating violence. What pierced him was not merely the loss of the girl, whose soft, bewildered beauty he had so long regarded as the one redeeming ornament of his pinched existence; it was the exquisite insolence of the arrangement. Verrall had wealth, leisure, the effortless grace of a class that took what it fancied as naturally as it breathed the air, while he, William Leadford, was left to digest the bitter comedy of his own devotion. As he stepped out into the street, where the green twilight lay like stagnant water along the cobbles, a cold, methodical clarity settled upon his mind.

By the time he had pawned his silver watch and acquired, from a small, greasy shop in Hanley, the heavy, antiquated revolver that now weighed down the pocket of his coat, the world beyond Staffordshire seemed to have entered into a conspiracy with his own fever. The train that carried him southward toward the coast was packed with men whose faces, illuminated by the sickly radiance filtering through the carriage windows, wore an expression of distracted, quarrelsome anxiety. Everyone talked of the war—of cruisers already steaming through the mist of the North Sea, of statesmen who had ceased to pretend that reason might still prevail—yet William sat in his corner wrapped in a fierce, impenetrable solitude. He had discovered, through a chance word dropped by a Checkshill groom, the name of the seaside hamlet to which the fugitives had repaired: Shaphambury, a cluster of wooden bungalows perched above the shingle, far from the smoke and the prying eyes of the Midlands. He rehearsed, with a precision that was almost luxurious, the scene he intended to enact upon the sands. He would not bluster; he would not plead. He would simply present himself before them as the embodied consequence of their lightness, forcing Verrall to see, for one brief, terrible instant, that a poor man’s honour was not a toy to be tossed aside in the grass. Overhead, as the train rattled through the darkening countryside, the comet had grown to a vast, luminous fan of emerald fire, casting long, unnatural shadows across the fields.

Shaphambury, when he reached it in the late afternoon, appeared to have been abandoned to the sea wind and the uncanny light. The little promenade was nearly empty, save for a handful of anxious visitors staring seaward, where, it was rumoured, the distant, muffled thud of naval guns had been heard at noon. William walked along the ridge of pebbles with his hand thrust deep into his pocket, his fingers curled about the cold iron of the revolver, his eyes scanning the row of white-painted cottages that faced the tide. It was at the furthest end of the line, where the gorse crept down to meet the shingle, that he saw them. They were sitting upon a low timbered verandah, so close together that their silhouettes, outlined against the pale, greenish-gold of the western sky, seemed almost to merge into a single, untroubled form. Nettie was laughing—a light, silver sound that carried across the beach and struck William like a physical blow. Verrall leaned back in his wicker chair, his fair head tilted, offering her a cigarette with that same negligent ease that had, for days, haunted William’s waking nightmares. The sheer, unthinking beauty of their contentment, set against the vast, menacing backdrop of a world on the brink of slaughter, filled him with a sudden, dizzying nausea. He did not hesitate; he stepped off the path onto the loose stones, his boots crunching loudly in the quiet air, determined to shatter their little paradise before the sun went down.

They heard him before he reached the steps, and the transformation in their attitude—the abrupt arrest of Nettie’s laughter, the swift, protective stiffening of Verrall’s shoulders—gave William a moment of dark, intoxicating triumph. He stood at the edge of the sand, breathing hard, the revolver already drawn and gleaming faintly in the strange, emerald dusk. "You thought you had left it all behind," he said, his voice sounding thin and unfamiliar in his own ears, as though another man were speaking through his throat. Nettie gave a small, stifled cry, rising from her chair with her hands pressed to her mouth, while Verrall stepped forward, his face pale but remarkably composed. Yet even as William raised his arm, aiming not at the girl he had loved, but at the young gentleman whose very existence seemed an insult to his own, an extraordinary thing occurred. A scent—faint at first, like crushed almonds and wet meadow-grass—drifted in from the sea, accompanied by a silence so profound that the distant wash of the waves appeared to cease. The green light in the sky deepened, thickening into a luminous, velvety mist that wrapped the verandah, the beach, and the three figures in a sudden, breathless calm. William’s arm grew intolerably heavy; the anger that had sustained him for days seemed to dissolve like frost in a warm room. He saw Verrall’s knees buckle, saw Nettie sink gently back into the wicker chair, and before he could even wonder at the meaning of it, he himself slid forward onto the cool, yielding shingle, losing all sense of his grievance in a deep, dreamless sleep.

When he opened his eyes, the morning had come, though it was a morning unlike any he had ever beheld in his twenty-four years of life. The air was of an exquisite, crystalline purity, washed clean of every trace of coal-smoke and sea-fog, and the sky arched above him in a vast, untroubled dome of pale sapphire. For several minutes William lay quite still upon the pebbles, conscious of a sensation so novel that he could scarcely name it: it was as if a heavy, rusted chain, which he had worn about his chest for so long that he had mistaken it for a part of his own anatomy, had been silently unfastened. He sat up and looked at the revolver lying beside his knee. The sight of the clumsy, black instrument filled him not with shame—for shame itself seemed a cramped, unnecessary thing—but with a quiet, wondering pity for the desperate boy who had carried it across England. A few paces away, upon the verandah, Verrall was stirring, passing a hand across his brow with a look of bewildered lucidity, while Nettie sat leaning against the railing, her dark hair loosened about her shoulders, gazing out toward the sea with eyes that held no trace of yesterday’s terror. There was no sound of distant guns; the horizon lay empty and serene. It occurred to William, with a clarity that needed no argument, that the old world—with its fierce little prides, its jealousies, its frantic preparations for murder—had simply ceased to be, and that they were all, in some strange, unimagined fashion, beginning again.

He rose to his feet, leaving the weapon where it lay in the sand, and walked slowly up the wooden steps of the verandah. Neither Nettie nor Verrall shrank from him; instead, they turned toward him with an openness of regard that made any formal greeting superfluous. "You came a long way, William," Nettie said softly, and there was in her voice no note of apology, no tremor of the old, frightened evasion—only a deep, quiet recognition of what he had suffered. William stood beside the railing, looking from her face to Verrall’s, and saw, for the first time, the young man not as an enemy or a thief, but as a fellow creature, curiously young and rather bewildered by the sudden transparency of their lives. "I came to kill you," William replied, stating the fact with a gentle, almost conversational simplicity, as though they were discussing a journey undertaken in another life. Verrall nodded slowly, without flinching, his gaze meeting William’s with a candour that bridged, in an instant, the gulf of birth and fortune that had separated them since childhood. "I know," Verrall murmured. "And I took what was yours without a thought of what it cost you." They stood together in the bright, clean morning, the three of them bound by a strange, luminous sympathy that had nothing to do with possession or victory. Far off, beyond the quiet downs, the towns and capitals of Europe were waking to the same clear light, their armies laying down the instruments of an ancient madness; and William, looking at Nettie’s quiet hands, felt that he had, after all, lost nothing that was truly his, and gained a world he had never known how to imagine.
