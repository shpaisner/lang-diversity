The Five Towns lay under their own smoke that autumn, as they had lain for a hundred years, and the smoke was no more troubled by comets than the men beneath it. William Leadford, who painted flowers on cheap tea-cups at Swinnerton's pot-bank for eighteen shillings a week, had no leisure to look at the sky. He looked at the clock. At six it let him out, and at ten past six he was at the corner of Heath Street with his collar brushed, waiting for Nettie Willersley, as he had waited every evening since the spring.

"She's a bit of a lady, that girl," said Parload, who clerked at the gasworks and kept a telescope in his back bedroom. "You'd best mind how you hold her."

"I hold her as well as any man," said Leadford.

"That's what the pot says to the potter," said Parload, and he went off to his stars, for he was a man who had a fixed idea and was therefore happy.

The papers that month printed little about Leadford's trouble and a great deal about the comet. It was a new one, they said, a wanderer out of the dark beyond Neptune, trailing a tail of strange green fire. They printed, too, a great deal about the Germans and the Russians, and about the new ironclads, and about Balkan frontiers no Staffordshire man could pronounce. Leadford read none of it. He read Nettie's face, and in October it began to say things he did not like.

Her father kept the lodge-gates at Lord Verrall's, and young Edward Verrall, who had been to a university and had never in his life been asked to wait for anything, took to walking through the park at the hour when Nettie fetched the milk. Leadford knew it. A man who loves knows these things as a dog knows rain. He said nothing, because he was proud, and because he had nothing to offer against a motor-car except a grievance.

On a wet Saturday he went to the lodge with a bunch of chrysanthemums that had cost him a shilling. Old Willersley met him on the step with a face like a shut door.

"She's gone," he said. "Went Thursday, with the young master, by the London train. Left a note. I'd have thought she'd have the decency to write to you."

"Where?"

"London. Abroad, maybe. Gentlemen go abroad." The old man looked at the flowers. "You'd better give those to the dustman, lad."

Leadford did not give them to the dustman. He walked back across the park in the rain, and at the lodge gate he dropped them in the road and set his heel upon them, one after another, very carefully, as a man kills beetles. Something that had been good in him, and not very strong, went out with them. Something else came in and sat down, and it did not talk. It only waited.

"You're not yourself," said Parload.

"I'm exactly myself. That's the trouble."

They were in the back bedroom, among the brass tubes and the smell of paraffin. Parload had the telescope out of the window, pointed at a place in the sky where, to Leadford's eye, there was nothing but the wet dark and a single dirty star.

"There," said Parload. "Look. That's the head. And that line, that's the tail. Do you know what the spectroscope says of the tail? Cyanogen, they think. And something else, a line nobody can place. A new gas, Leadford, a green gas." He stopped, remembering the other man was there. "She'll pass close in a fortnight. Closer than anything on record."

"Will it kill us?"

"Nobody knows."

"Good," said Leadford. "Then it'll be fair."

He put his eye to the glass and saw a small pale smudge, quite without malice. It seemed to him a poor sort of thing to be afraid of.

Three days later he drew eleven pounds, which were all his savings, from the Post Office. In a pawnshop in Hanley he bought a revolver that had belonged, said the pawnbroker, to a gentleman, and six cartridges that had belonged to nobody. He did not say what he wanted them for. He had not decided. It was enough that they lay in his pocket and were heavy.

He found them through Nettie's sister, who had a postcard from Wales. It cost him two days and a third-class ticket, and everywhere he went, the country seemed to be holding its breath.

At Crewe the platform was full of men with cardboard boxes and anxious wives; reservists, a porter told him, called up that morning. At Shrewsbury a newsboy was crying something about an ultimatum. In the carriage a commercial traveller in hardware explained to the company that there would be no war, since the bankers would never permit it. A clergyman said there would be war, and that it would be a judgment. A little old woman with a basket said nothing, but held her basket tighter.

"And what d'you say, young man?" demanded the traveller.

"I say it's no business of mine," said Leadford.

"It'll be everybody's business before Christmas."

Leadford looked out at the wet hills going by, and at the sky, where by day there was nothing and by night the comet hung, grown now to a great pale feather across the stars. He thought: they are all frightened of the wrong thing. The war was a long way off. Verrall was in a house in a valley eleven miles from the railway, and that was near enough to hold in his two hands.

He walked the eleven miles in the dusk, with the road shining under a sky the colour of a bruise. The villages he passed were shut. In one a knot of men stood outside a public-house and spoke low, and one of them, a Territorial in half a uniform, was being treated by the rest as something between a hero and a corpse. At a crossroads a motor-lorry went by full of soldiers who sang, and the singing did not sound like joy.

By nine he was at the gate of a small stone house with a lamp in the window. He could see, through the glass, a table laid for two, and a woman's shoulders, and the back of a man's head; and the shoulders were Nettie's, and the head was Verrall's, and they were laughing at something.

Leadford leaned against the wall. He took out the revolver and looked at it, and it was an ugly thing, black and a little greasy. He found he was not angry. He had expected to be angry. He was merely tired, and very clear, and he understood that in a moment, when he had pushed open the gate, there would be no way back to the pot-bank and the clock and the corner of Heath Street. The road behind him was already closing, like water.

He put his thumb on the hammer.

And then the light changed.

It came from the sky, softly, as milk comes into tea: first a tinge in the east, then a stain of green that spread without haste across the whole vault of heaven, until the hills, the wet road, the stone wall and his own hand were all lit with the colour of young beech leaves. There was no wind. A dog began to howl in the valley, and stopped. Far off, down the road, the singing of the soldiers broke in the middle of a line, and nothing took its place.

"Parload," whispered Leadford, absurdly, "you were right."

The air had a taste, sweetish and clean, such as he had never met in the Potteries. He drew it in because he could not help it, and it went down into him like cold water into a scorched pipe. The revolver was very heavy. He thought, dimly, that he ought to be afraid; but the fear, when he reached for it, was not there. It had gone out of the world, as the war had gone, and the pawnshop, and the clock. His knees folded gently. He sat down against the wall with the pistol across his lap, and in the lighted window he saw Nettie's head go down upon her arms, and Verrall's after it, as children's heads go down in school on a hot afternoon.

The green deepened. The stars were drowned in it. Leadford's last thought, before the quiet took him, was a small and astonished one, that he had never in his life been so comfortable, and that someone, somewhere, ought to be told.

Then the road, the hills, and the sleeping island lay together under the sea-coloured light, and nothing in all England stirred but the slow, bright, indifferent tail of the comet, sweeping over them like the hem of a great robe.
