The kilns of Swadlincote stood along the ridge like a company of fat black bottles, and from every one of them went up a smoke that told the sky what the Potteries thought of it. William Leadford, nineteen years old and a draughtsman's clerk at eighteen shillings the week, walked under that smoke each evening with his boots full of marl and his head full of Nettie Stuart. That was the whole of his wealth, and he had never reckoned it small.

On the Thursday he came up Trubshaw Lane as usual, and the gardener's cottage had its blinds down in the daylight. Old Stuart sat on the step with his pipe unlit. He did not look up.

"She's gone, lad," said he. "Went by the half-past-ten, with a bag. There was a motor waiting at the turnpike. You'll know whose."

Leadford knew. There was one motor in the county that stood so, with its engine muttering like a gentleman too well-bred to complain. It belonged to young Verrall of the Verrall Works, who had hands as white as a girl's and a way of saying my dear fellow to men who earned their bread.

He did not say anything. A man seldom does, at the first stroke. He stood and looked at the cabbages, very straight and green in their rows, and thought how orderly the world was in the places where it did not matter.

Over the Bursley roofs, in the thin yellow of the dusk, hung a smudge no bigger than a thumbprint. The papers called it the Comet. Leadford had read about it that morning, with interest. He read about it now without any.

His mother kept the kitchen as the Potteries keep everything: scrubbed, small, and loud with the clock. She had the tea on the table and the news in her face, for the whole street had it before the kettle boiled.

"Ye'll eat," she said.

"I'll not."

"Ye'll eat, Will. A broken heart's a poor excuse for a hollow belly. Your father had both, and I buried him with the one and the other."

He ate. It was bread and dripping, and it tasted of ash. Through the wall came the Pomfrets' gramophone, and through the window came the voices of the men going to the Crown, and the voices were not talking of Nettie. They were talking of Germany.

"They'll have us in it by Michaelmas," said one, in the flat certain tone of a man who has read half a leader. "Dreadnoughts, they call 'em. Ships by the dozen, and every one a hole in the Treasury."

"Let 'em come," said another. "I've a boy in the Staffords."

"Then you've a boy in the grave, Sam Bickerton, if the Kaiser has his way."

Leadford listened with the dull attention of a man at the bottom of a well. The whole of Europe, it seemed, was arming itself and sharpening its tongue, and the whole of England was reading the sporting page. Nobody had thought to ask what any of it was for. He had no answer either. He only knew that somewhere a motor was running south on a good road, and that he was not in it.

He had met her first at the chapel bazaar, where she sold lavender bags, and had been a fool within the quarter-hour.

Nettie Stuart was not clever. Let that be said plainly, since the lovers of the world never will say it. She was quick, and light, and honest in her own fashion, and she wanted things. That was all her sin. She wanted a fire that drew properly, and a frock that did not come out of a catalogue, and a life with the corners of it lifted. Leadford had offered her a terrace house in Hanley and a lifetime of Sundays.

"It's not that I don't care for you, Will," she had told him once, by the stile. "It's that you'd be so sure of everything. You'd be right, and I'd be tidy, and we'd both be dead by forty."

He had laughed. He had thought it a pretty thing to say.

Verrall had offered a different arithmetic. He had a car, and a villa at Torquay, and a laugh like a man who had never once been told no. He did not have a plan beyond Tuesday. Leadford, sitting on the edge of his bed with the window open and the smudge in the sky growing a shade brighter, saw it all, and saw also that seeing did not help. A clear eye is a poor plaster.

On the Saturday he went to Hanley and bought a revolver.

The man who sold it was a pawnbroker with a cough, and he asked no questions, because the Potteries were that month full of men who needed pistols and had no one to tell why. It was a small ugly thing, a Belgian make, and the cartridges came in a twist of newspaper. Leadford carried it home against his ribs as one carries a hot coal, wondering at its weight.

At the works that Monday the foreman found him staring at a drawing of a saggar-kiln as though it were a letter in Greek.

"Leadford. Wake up, man. There's work."

"Yes, Mr. Hulme."

"And hark. Verrall's father was here this morning, face the colour of a boiled ham. Says the boy's taken himself off with some girl and he'll stop the allowance. I tell thee what, they'll be hard-put." Hulme chuckled, and then, seeing the clerk's face, stopped. He was not a stupid man. "Aye. Well. Get on with it."

Leadford got on with it. His pencil drew the kiln true to a sixteenth of an inch, and every line was straight, and none of it meant anything. When the whistle blew he walked out past the ovens with the small hard weight against his ribs and looked up. The Comet was no longer a thumbprint. It was a pale feather laid across the evening, and under it the whole county seemed to wait, as a dog waits for a voice it half knows.

That night he climbed Mow Cop to be alone with it.

It is a cold hill, with a ruined castle upon it that was never a castle but a rich man's joke, and from the top you may see half of Staffordshire laid out like a map of a disaster: the red glow of the furnaces, the chains of lamps along the roads, the black humps of the slag. He sat on a stone with his hands between his knees. Above him the Comet had thrown its tail clear across a third of the heavens, and the stars shrank away from it.

He had read in the Signal that men of science did not think it dangerous. He had read in the Sentinel that the Archbishop thought it a warning. He had read in the Clarion that it was a symbol, and that after it the wage-slaves would arise. Every paper had made it mean what the paper wanted. Leadford looked at it, and it meant nothing at all. It hung there, and went on being huge and indifferent, and that, strangely, was a comfort.

"You'll not stop for me," he said aloud, "and I'll not stop for you."

Far below, a train screamed, going south.

He took out the revolver, turned it over once in the glimmer, and put it back. Then he began to go down, stumbling over the tussocks, towards the station and the last train, with eleven pounds of savings in his pocket and no notion in his head except a name and a seaside town.

The third-class carriage smelt of damp wool and oranges. Leadford sat in the corner with his cap over his eyes and heard the wheels say the same thing over and over: Torquay, Torquay, Torquay. At Crewe two soldiers got in, singing, and behind them a woman with a basket, who told the compartment that her brother in Hamburg had written that all the men there were being drilled in the streets. Nobody replied. The soldiers stopped singing, and for a while the only sound was the engine.

Towards dawn the train stood a long while at some junction in the Midlands, in a fog the colour of dishwater. Leadford let down the window. The air was strange. It was neither cold nor warm, but had a taste in it, a faint sharpness like the smell of a struck match a long way off. The porter on the platform had stopped with his lantern half-raised, looking up, and his mouth was open.

Leadford looked also.

The Comet had grown overnight into something that no longer belonged to the sky. It had a head now, wide and pale, and the edges of it were stained a clear and gentle green, such as he had seen only once, in the heart of a flame where a copper salt had fallen. The fog burned thin beneath it. Every roof, every wet rail, every face on that platform was touched with the same cold colour, and the faces were all of one expression.

"That's not right," said the porter softly. "That's not right at all."

Leadford put his hand upon the revolver, and found, to his surprise, that it was no longer the heaviest thing he carried. The train jerked, and moved. He sat back and watched the green light come in at the window and lie across his knees like a hand, and he went on towards Nettie, and towards Verrall, and towards whatever the morning was going to make of them all.
