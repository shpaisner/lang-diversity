The bottle-ovens of Clayhanger stood up against the evening like a row of fat black bottles set out to cool, and William Leadford, with his ledger shut and his pen wiped, watched the smoke go up from them into a sky that had a stranger in it.

The stranger had come a month ago, a smudge no bigger than a thumbprint on a window. Now it hung low in the east after sundown, a pale, whiskered thing, and the papers at the pot-bank counted its miles as the Almighty counts sparrows. The papers counted other things too. There were so many Dreadnoughts on the one side and so many on the other, so many army corps on the Rhine, so many words in so many telegrams that meant nothing and were meant to. The Potteries read them over their tea and said it would never come to a fight. They said it as men whistle in a churchyard.

"Comin' down to the Cross Keys, Will?" said Parsons, the glazer, clapping on his cap.

"No."

"Thee'll sit there and sour like a jug o' milk."

"Then I'll sour," said Leadford, and Parsons, who was kind in the way of men who have never had much to be unkind about, went off whistling.

Leadford stayed. He was twenty-two, with a clerk's stoop and a labourer's wrists. His wage was eighteen shillings, and nothing in the world was his but a good head for figures and a hurt that sat under his ribs like a swallowed stone. The stone had a name, and the name was Nettie.

She had gone on a Tuesday. There was no scene in it and no scandal that the street could chew on. There was only the cottage in Bursley Lane with the blind half down, her father sitting in the kitchen with his hands hanging between his knees, and a note in the clock-case that said, in her round school hand, Don't look for me, Will. I couldn't bear to be poor always.

Under the note, in the same hand, a second line, as if she had hesitated and then been honest: He has a motor.

It was a motor that had done it. Leadford knew the make of it, for he had seen it twice in the High Street, a long grey thing that purred like a well-fed cat and scattered the hens. In it, lounging with one gloved hand on the door, had sat Mr. Verrall of the Hall, who had never in his life been hungry and had never needed to be clever. A man of that kind does not take a girl as a thief takes a purse. He holds out his hand with the rings on it, and the girl goes to it as water goes downhill.

Leadford put out the gas, locked the office, and stood a while on the step.

The comet had grown a shade brighter. He looked at it with a dull, level hatred, as if it too were a thing that had come from outside to carry off what was his.

Her father, old Stewart, had been a gardener at the Hall before his back gave out, and he still smelt of earth and sacking and the sourness of old age. He sat in the lean-to behind the cottage with a seed-tray on his knees, pricking out lettuce that would never be eaten by anybody he knew.

"I'd have stopped her, Will," he said, "only she'd the look of her mother. When a woman's got that look, thee might as well argue wi' the weather."

"Where is she?"

"I couldn't say."

"Thee could."

The old man's fingers went on pricking at the soil, careful, small, and shaky. "London, they say. He's a flat there. Or it's a house at Brighton. Folk tell me one thing and another." He lifted his faded eyes. "Thee's not to go after 'em, lad. Thee'll do thysel' a mischief and her no good."

"I'll do nobody a mischief," said Leadford, and the lie lay between them like a third person.

He walked home the long way by the canal. The water was the colour of weak tea and carried on its skin a rainbow of oil and a dead cat. He had thought, all those evenings in the lane, that love was a plain, honest article like bread, which a man earned and a woman ate. Now he saw it was a market after all, with prices, and that he had come to it with the wrong coin.

The green light of the comet lay on the water by the bridge. It was faint, but it was there, and it was not the colour of any lamp.

At the Cross Keys the talk that Saturday was all of Germany.

"They'll not dare," said Parsons, with his pot raised. "Not wi' the Navy."

"Navy's a fine thing," said a lean man by the fire, a reservist with his boots polished for no reason. "It's not the Navy that goes into Belgium."

"Belgium!" Parsons laughed. "Who's Belgium to us?"

"Nobody, till somebody walks over 'em. Then they'll be everybody."

The room considered this, and found it distasteful, and turned to the comet, which was easier. A man who had it from his cousin in the Observatory said that the tail of the thing was a gas, and that the Earth would pass through it in a few weeks' time. Another said it was nonsense, and a third said it was in the Bible. The landlord, who had a licence to think of, said it was bad for trade.

Leadford sat in the corner and drank his one half-pint very slowly, and heard it all as a man hears a band playing in the next street. There was something in all this talk, he thought, that matched the thing in his own chest: a general sourness, a feeling of men crowded too close in the dark and fingering their knives. Everybody was afraid of everybody else. Everybody had a grievance and a rival, and nobody had yet been told the right name for either. The world, he thought, was as ready for a bad deed as he was.

"Will's gone quiet," Parsons said. "What's thee think o' the comet, Will?"

"I think," said Leadford, rising, "that if it hits us, it'll save a deal of argument."

They laughed, and he went out into the street while they were still laughing.

Mosenstein kept the pawnshop in Hanley, under the three brass balls, and he asked no questions of any man who laid down the right money in the right manner.

The money was Leadford's savings, four years of them: eleven pounds, six shillings, got together shilling by shilling for a wedding that was now to be spent on a funeral. The revolver lay on a square of green baize like a small, black, sullen animal, and Mosenstein turned it over in his soft hands.

"Webley. Very good. Very sound." He looked up. "You are going to shoot rats, perhaps?"

"Perhaps."

"Rats, yes. There are many." Mosenstein weighed the box of cartridges in his palm. "I will say one word, my friend, and then I say nothing. A man who has bought one of these has bought also a rope for his own neck. Not today, maybe. But the rope is in the parcel."

"Wrap it up."

Mosenstein wrapped it in brown paper and tied the string in a neat bow, as though it were a pair of boots.

Outside it was nearly dark. The comet stood over the chimneys now, no longer a smudge, but a great pale feather, and its colour had changed. A man would have said that someone had hung a green lamp in the sky and forgotten to turn it down. People had stopped on the pavement to look, with their shopping baskets on their arms and their faces tilted up, and the green light turned them all to drowned folk. A child was crying somewhere. A tram clanged. Leadford pushed through them with the parcel under his coat, and not one of them looked at him.

He took the late train to London, third class, with a ticket that cost him another seven shillings out of the little that was left.

The carriage was full of the night's cargo: a sailor asleep with his mouth open, two women with a basket of eggs, a commercial traveller reading the evening paper with his lips moving. The headline said ULTIMATUM. The traveller read it aloud to nobody. "Forty-eight hours," he said. "God help us, forty-eight hours." Nobody answered him. The wheels said it for them, over and over, all the way through Rugby and Bletchley and the black dripping cuttings south of Watford: forty-eight hours, forty-eight hours.

Leadford sat with the parcel on his knees and did not think. He had meant to think, and to plan how he should find the house, what he should say, and whether he should speak at all. But thought would not come. Only pictures came: Nettie's hand on the clock-case, Nettie's blue Sunday hat, Verrall's glove on the door of the motor. And under the pictures ran the one fixed intention, like a rail under a train, that carried him on without his leave.

At Euston the lamps were burning pale and queasy against a sky that was nearly daylight, though it was only three in the morning. The light was green, and it seemed to come from nowhere, to fall from no one quarter, and to cast no shadows. The porters moved about in it slowly, looking up, forgetting their trucks. A policeman stood at the barrier with his helmet pushed back and his mouth a little open, like a man listening for a sound he cannot place.

The house stood on a hill above Hampstead, a villa with a monkey-puzzle on the lawn, and a gate that was not locked.

Leadford had got the address from Verrall's own tailor, by a lie told so smoothly that he wondered at himself. He went up the gravel with the revolver heavy and warm in his coat pocket. The air had grown strange. It was very still, so still that his own boots seemed too loud. It smelt of nothing he knew, a thin sweetness, like crushed leaves or rain on a hot road. Over the roofs the whole sky had turned the green of a bottle held to the sun, and the stars had vanished. The comet itself was gone, melted into the heavens, and the heavens were all comet.

There was a light in the window, and in the window sat Nettie.

She was in a loose white gown, her hair down, her chin on her hand. She was looking out at the green night with a face he had never seen on her, not happy and not sorry, but frightened and awake. Behind her Verrall moved across the room, pale and handsome, and said something over his shoulder.

Leadford drew the revolver. His hand was steady. That surprised him. He lifted it, and sighted along the black barrel at the white blur of Verrall's shirt-front, and drew a long breath, and found that the breath was strange to him. It went down into him like cold water into a parched throat, and the stone under his ribs, that had sat there for three weeks, shifted.

Why, he thought, with a slow and enormous wonder, what a fool I've been.

The pistol sagged. The sweet air rose about him like a tide. He saw the lawn, the monkey-puzzle, the gravel, and the sleeping house, all of them bright and clear and very kind, and he laid himself down beside the path, and the green light closed over him as gently as a hand over a child's eyes.
