The Five Towns lay under their own smoke that autumn, as they had lain under it since before William Leadford was breeched, and the bottle-ovens stood about the clay like a congregation of fat black monks who had forgotten their prayers. Leadford was a clerk at Swinnerton's pot-bank at nine-and-twenty shillings a week, and he had a mind that was too large for the stool he sat on. That is the whole of the trouble with most men, and it is also the beginning of most songs.

He read in the evenings, mostly things that made him angrier than they made him wise. He read that the Comet, which had been a smudge on an astronomer's plate in the spring, was now a thing the newspapers called "the Visitor." He read that Germany had said one thing and France another, and that an Emperor had gone yachting. He read it all with his boots on the fender and his landlady's cat on his knee, and he thought, as the poor think: It will be done to us, and not by us. It always is.

Over every page, whatever the print, he saw Nettie's face.

He had courted her three years, if walking out along the canal on Sundays can be called courting. Nettie Stewart was a grocer's daughter with grey eyes and a way of looking past a man at something better. Leadford saw that look and mistook it for thoughtfulness. He would talk to her of Socialism and the Rights of Labour; she would nod, and tread the towpath, and watch the barges.

"You're always going to do something, Will," she said once, by the lock at Etruria. "You've been going to do it since I was seventeen."

"I'm saving."

"Saving," said Nettie, and laughed. It was not an unkind laugh. It was worse. It was the laugh of a girl pricing a ribbon she knows she will not buy.

He should have taken warning from the lock-gates, which hold back the water only until somebody opens them. But he was twenty-nine, and honest, and slow where it mattered, and he went home that night rehearsing a proposal in four paragraphs. He never delivered it. Another man delivered his in a single sentence, and the sentence had a motor-car in it.

Verrall was the son of a man who owned a good half of the land that the pot-banks stood on. Leadford had seen him a dozen times: a clean, loose-limbed young man with a cigarette and the unhurried voice of one to whom no door has ever been shut. He had come down to look at the works, as young men of his sort will, and he had looked at Nettie Stewart over a counter of tea and bacon, and that was all.

The note was found on a Thursday. It said she was sorry. It said she could not go on being sensible; that she had been sensible all her life, and look at it. It did not say where. Her father read it at the shop door with his apron on, and sat down on a sack of sugar, and did not rise for the better part of an hour.

Leadford read it at the Marquis of Granby, passed to him hand over hand along the bar, and the men about him went quiet in the way that men do when they are sorry and also very glad it is not their own.

"London, they say," said a mould-runner. "Or Paris. They go to Paris."

"There's talk Paris'll not be there to go to, come Christmas," said another. "What with the Germans."

"What with the Comet," said a third, and nobody laughed.

It was a bad winter to be wronged in. The whole world seemed to have been wronged, and to be sharpening itself upon the fact. The papers came out twice a day. Reservists were warned. In Hanley the Territorials drilled in a drizzle with an air of apologising to the rain. Along the Continent, so the leader-writers said, the great Powers were drifting, which is the word men use when they are too frightened to say steering.

And above it all, from about nine at night, hung the Comet, which had put on flesh and colour week by week like a creature feeding. At first it was a pale thumbprint. Now it was a hard, bright wedge with a greenish tinge to the tail, and when the smoke thinned over Shelton the children stood in the road and pointed. The old women said it meant a king dying, which they have said of every comet since Caesar, and have been right often enough to be insufferable.

Leadford did not look at it. He worked, because a man must; he ate, because the body is a fool; and in the evenings he sat with the thing that had grown in him since the note. It was not grief. Grief is soft and wet and washes out. This was dry and angular, and sat in the middle of him like a stone in a boot.

He lost his place in November for a mistake in the invoices, a mistake he made with his eyes open, and in the same week he found out where they were. It was a cousin of Nettie's who told him, and told it badly, from a pity that was half spite: a furnished villa at a place called Burnham, down in the south, a poor sort of hiding for a rich man's son.

He drew out eleven pounds, which was all he had, and went to a pawnbroker in Hanley who asked no questions and gave a revolver and twelve cartridges for three pounds ten. It was a heavy, ugly, honest weapon, a Webley, that had belonged to someone's dead son. It sat in his coat like a second and more truthful heart.

The train south was full of men in new khaki who sang and then did not sing. At Rugby a woman wept on the platform. Leadford sat in his corner with his hand upon the coat and looked out at the dark country wheeling past, and at the Comet which wheeled with it, and which now filled a fifth of the sky.

He thought: I shall say nothing. I shall say her name once, and then I shall do it. It was a poor sort of plan. Most plans of the kind are.

He came to Burnham at dusk on the second day, on foot, through lanes that smelt of wet leaves and woodsmoke, and the villa stood where he had been told: a small square house, a lamp in the lower window, a motor-car cooling under a shed. He stood at the gate a long while. His boots were wet through; he had not noticed.

The light was queer. He had been aware of it for an hour without giving it a name. It was not dusk. It was a clear, thin, unnatural green, such as a man sees at the bottom of a bottle, and it lay on the hedges and on the gate-latch and on the backs of his own hands. He looked up.

The whole sky had become the Comet. It leaned over the world like a great pale flower, and from its edges the green came down in long soft veils that moved. There was no sound at all. Somewhere a dog began to howl, and stopped, and a rook came down out of an elm and sat in the road, quite quiet, with its beak open.

Leadford drew the Webley. His hand was steady and he hated it for being so. Through the window he saw Nettie rise from a chair; saw Verrall lift his head; saw both their faces turn toward the glass, lit green, curiously young, as if someone had taken a hand and smoothed them.

"Nettie," he said, as he had planned.

Then the air changed. It sweetened. It went into his chest like cold water into a thirsty man, and the stone that had sat there so long began, slowly, absurdly, to give. His knees were failing him. The revolver, he noticed, was very heavy, and then it was lying in the wet grass, and he was lying beside it, looking up into the green, thinking quite clearly: Why, I was never so tired in my life.

The last thing he heard was the rook, laughing.
