The Five Towns lie in a bowl of their own smoke, and a man born there learns early that the sky is a thing one sees on Sundays, if at all. William Leadford had been born under the bottle-ovens, in a street of blackened brick where the washing came in dirtier than it went out. At twenty-two he drew nineteen shillings a week as a clerk at Rawdon's Pot-Works, and wrote down other men's crates in a ledger that smelt of clay and damp. He was a thin, sallow, angry young man, who read books he did not understand and believed what they said about the injustice of things.

He had one possession that mattered, and her name was Nettie.

She worked at the decorating-shop, and she had a way of laughing with her head thrown back that made the girls beside her look like cheap earthenware. Leadford had walked out with her through two winters, up the Bank and along the canal, where the water lay thick as treacle and the slag-heaps smoked against the dusk. He had told her that things would mend, that he would rise, that the world was a cheat but a cheat might be beaten at its own game. She had listened, and swung her hand in his, and said nothing at all. He took the silence for assent. That was his first mistake; there were others.

Verrall came in the spring. He was the son of a man who owned things: a house at Clayfield, shares in a colliery, and a motor-car, which in those days was still an insolence. Young Verrall wore a grey suit that fitted him, and he came to the decorating-shop with his father's partner to look at patterns. He looked at Nettie too, a little longer than at the patterns.

Leadford learned the rest as a man learns of a death, by pieces. First there was a gap in the Saturday walk, then a half-lie about an aunt at Hanley, then a morning when her mother stood in the doorway with her apron over her hands and said, "She's gone, Willie. She's left a line."

The line was four sentences long. I am sorry. You are better than me, and that is why it will not do. I am going with Edward. Do not follow.

He read it twice in the street, and the world went quiet round him, as a furnace-room goes quiet when the draught is shut. Then he folded the paper small and put it in his waistcoat, and went to work, and wrote down eleven hundred dinner-plates without an error. That is the way with some men: the worse the hurt, the straighter the figures.

It was that autumn that people began to look up. The astronomers had found a thing in the heavens, a wandering stranger out of the dark beyond Neptune, and the papers gave it columns. Its tail would sweep across half the sky. Some said it would graze the earth, and some said it would strike us; the wiser men said nothing and sold their telescopes' time to the curious at sixpence a look.

Leadford took no interest in it, but the talk was everywhere, in the tram and the tap-room and the chapel porch. And behind the comet, like a second and fouler weather, came the other news. There was a quarrel over a frontier in the east, and a Kaiser who rattled something, and a Tsar who answered. The reservists were being counted. A man at the Wheatsheaf, red-faced and certain, said that a good war would clear the air, and another said that it would clear the pits too, and there was almost a fight about it.

"Let 'em come," said Leadford, from the end of the settle. "Let it all come down together. Comet and Kaiser and the whole rotten shop. I'd not lift a hand to stop it."

They laughed at him. He was known for talking so. But he drank his beer without tasting it, and he thought, A man might use a war. A man might use anything.

On the Saturday he drew his wages and went to a pawnbroker's in Bethesda Street whom he knew by repute. There he bought, for two pounds ten and a lie about rats, a revolver with a worn walnut butt, and a card of cartridges. The pawnbroker, a small yellow man, wrapped it in brown paper as though it were a pair of boots.

"Rats," he said. "Ah. Big 'uns, I dare say."

"Two-legged," said Leadford, and was ashamed of the speech even as he made it, for it was out of a book.

He had learned where Verrall's father had taken a cottage for the young people, who were not married and would not be, since the old man held that his son might be a fool but not a bigamist, and there was a wife somewhere in the case. It lay beyond the hills, near the Welsh border, some seventy miles of slow trains and slower roads. Leadford took a day's leave that he had not been given, and did not tell his mother. She looked at him from the fireside, a grey woman with a face like folded linen, and said only, "Mind thysen, Willie," and he knew that she knew.

He went out into the dark with the thing heavy in his coat. Above the ovens, low in the north-east, hung a faint pale smear, like a thumb-mark on a window.

The train went south by fits. At every junction there were soldiers, boys in new khaki with their kit-bags, and women running beside the carriages with their hands out. A sergeant of the Staffords shared Leadford's compartment, a lean man with a moustache, who slept with his boots braced on the opposite seat. Once, at Stafford, he woke and looked at the sky through the dirty glass.

"There she is," he said. "Look at 'er. Got a green edge on 'er tonight. I don't like it."

Leadford looked, and for the first time he saw. The comet had grown. It stood above the black fields like a drawn sword, with a veil of faint green fire sweeping back from it across a quarter of the heavens, and the stars shone dim behind it. It was very quiet and very large. It made the engine, the carriages, and the little hurrying men seem like the toys they were.

"Wars," said the sergeant, settling his cap. "Comets. Seems like the Almighty's clearing His throat." He slept again.

Leadford did not. He sat with his hand upon the brown paper in his pocket, and told himself over and over what he would say. The words went dry in his mouth, as words will when the thing behind them is only hurt.

He walked the last nine miles. The road climbed through beech-woods and came out upon a ridge, and below him, in a fold of the hills, he saw the cottage: a white square with a thread of smoke, and a lamp in the window. It was near dawn. The whole sky had gone the colour of a bottle held to the light, and the air was strange, sweet and thin, like the air after a thunderstorm.

He went down through the wet grass with the revolver in his hand. A dog barked once and stopped. Somewhere, very far off to the east, he thought he heard guns, a dull stuttering like a cart on a bridge, and then he did not hear it. A kind of hush came over the hills. The birds, which had begun, left off. The green light thickened, and a taste came into his mouth, light and clean, such as he had never known in the Five Towns. His head swam. He thought, It is the comet. It has come to us; and he laughed, and tried to lift the pistol, and found that it was heavier than the world.

He saw the cottage door open. Nettie stood there in a grey shawl, with her hair down, looking up at the sky, and behind her Verrall, white-faced, with his hand on her shoulder. Neither looked at the gun. They looked at the light.

"Willie," she said, and there was no fear in it. "Oh, Willie, look."

He tried to say the words he had carried seventy miles. They would not come. The revolver slid out of his fingers into the wet grass, and he sank down after it on his knees, breathing the sweet air in great gulps, while the green dawn poured over him, and the old hatred went out of his body like a poison drawn from a wound.

His last thought, before the darkness took him, was a small one, and almost kind: What a fool I have been. Then the sky closed over him like water, and the whole world slept.
