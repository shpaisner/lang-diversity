The pot-banks of Staffordshire do not care who loves whom. They smoke at dawn and smoke at dusk, and between the two smokes a man may lose his wife, his wage, and his wits, and the kilns will draw no differently for it. William Leadford knew this as he knew the taste of clay-dust; he had been born to it in a street where the slate roofs ran black and shining to the canal, and where the sky was a thing seen by the inch.

He was nineteen when he first walked out with Nettie Reedworth, who had a quick brown eye and a way of tilting her head at a man as though he were a sum she meant to do properly. He was twenty-two when he began to understand that she did not mean to do it for him.

"You're always going to be somebody, Willie," she said, on the canal bank, a Sunday in March. "And I'm always going to be waiting for it."

"It'll come."

"So does the winter," said Nettie. Then she laughed, and he laughed, and neither thought it a joke.

He worked for a pittance among the saggars, carried and stacked and sweated, and at night read such books as a poor man can borrow, which are mostly the wrong ones. They taught him that the world was unjust. They did not teach him what to do about it, and so the knowledge sat in him like a bad tooth, aching quietly, waiting for a cold wind.

The cold wind was named Verrall.

He was the son of a house on the hill, with a motor-car, a soft voice, and the kind of hands that have never been introduced to a shovel. He came to the pottery works on some business of his father's, saw Nettie at the counter among the cups, and looked at her as men of his sort look at anything they have not yet owned.

Leadford saw the look. He told himself that he had not, which is how a man begins to lie to himself in earnest.

For three weeks Nettie was late to the canal bank. For a fourth she did not come at all. On the fifth Sunday Leadford climbed the stair to her mother's rooms and found her mother sitting very straight beside a cold grate, with a letter on her knee and her mouth shut like a purse.

"She's gone," said the old woman. "With him. South, they say. You'll not do anything foolish."

"Why should I?"

"Because you've that look. Your father had it."

He read the letter. It was kind, which was the worst of it. I could not bear a life of waiting, Willie. You will understand some day. He folded it small and put it in his waistcoat, and went down the stair into the street, and the kilns were roaring at the sunset like beasts being fed.

There was one man in Clayton who might have talked sense into him, and that man was Parload, who kept books by day and kept the sky by night.

Parload lived in a loft over a chandler's shop, where a brass telescope the length of a cart-shaft poked through a hole in the slates. He was thin, stooped, and cheerful as only a man can be who has something larger than himself to worry about.

"Come and look," said he, when Leadford came up the ladder with his face set hard. "Never mind her. Look."

Leadford looked, to be rid of him. He saw a smear of pale light, small and frail, on a black ground.

"A comet," said Parload softly. "New. Nobody's named it yet, but it's coming, Leadford, straight in. And there's a green in its spectrum—a line I can't place. Cyanogen, some say, and that would be the end of us all. Others say not."

"Let it come," said Leadford.

"You say that now."

"I say it always. What's a comet to a man who's lost his girl?"

"A great deal, if you'd look at it," said Parload. "Men are fools. Every nation in Europe is sharpening something. I've read the papers. Germany this, England that, and the Powers sitting about like men at a bad card-table, each sure the other cheats. And over all, that." He tapped the tube. "It doesn't care for any of us."

Leadford looked a long time at the small cold light. It did not comfort him, but for the first time it did not seem to him that his trouble was the biggest thing in the world.

It was a short mercy.

They dismissed him on the Thursday. He had struck a foreman, a man twice his weight and half his grief, and the works were not in a mood to forgive. He took his wages in a little brown envelope and spent the afternoon in the public library, reading of Morocco and of ships and of an Ultimatum that was being drafted somewhere in a room with a carpet.

The newspapers that week were full of two things, the comet and the war, and they spoke of both in the same voice. The Menace in the Heavens. The Menace on the Rhine. Boys cried them in the street. Men on the corners argued, and the arguments ended always with a laugh that was not quite a laugh.

"Let 'em fight," said a collier outside the Crown. "We'll be the better for a clearing."

"Aye, and the comet'll clear what's left," said another.

Leadford went home through the dusk. In a pawnbroker's window in Hanley he saw what he had been walking toward all day without letting himself know it: a revolver, blue-black, cheap, with a card beside it—Five shillings and sixpence, with cartridges.

He went in. The pawnbroker, a small damp man, looked at him once and then looked carefully at the till.

"For what?" he asked.

"For rats."

"Ah," said the pawnbroker. "There's a great many rats about this year."

He wrapped it in brown paper. Leadford carried it home against his ribs, and it was warm by the time he reached the door; and that, too, he thought, was a thing a man might remember.

He went south by the night train, third class, among soldiers.

They were Reservists, mostly, called up in a hurry, with their boots creaking new and their tunics too tight, and they sang and slept and swore by turns. Somewhere beyond Rugby a corporal with a broken tooth leaned over and asked him if he, too, was going to the war.

"I'm going to a wedding," said Leadford.

"Whose?"

"Nobody's. Not yet."

The corporal laughed and passed him a flask. Out of the window the country slid by, black fields and white mist, and in the east the comet hung, large now, a green-white feather on the cheek of the night. He found he could not look away from it. It had grown since Parload's loft. It seemed to lean toward the earth like a man leaning over a table to say a thing he has been rehearsing for a thousand years.

At a junction in Kent, grey with morning, he learned the rest. Parload had written the name of a village; a cousin of the Verralls kept a cottage there; the cottage stood on a hill above a chalk-pit, he said, and the lovers were in it. How Parload knew, Leadford never asked.

Beyond the station the road went up. Guns were speaking somewhere very far to the east, like doors closing in a large empty house.

It was evening when he reached the chalk-pit. The sky above the Weald had gone a thin, strange green, as the sea goes before a gale, and every hedge and gate and tile stood out in that light with the clearness of things seen in a dream. The birds had stopped. A dog, somewhere, howled and then did not.

The cottage was small and white, with a pear tree against the wall. A lamp was lit in the lower window. Leadford stood in the lane under the elms and took the revolver from his coat. His hand was perfectly steady; that surprised him. He had expected it to shake.

Now, he thought. Now it is done with talking.

Through the glass he saw her. She sat by the table, her head bent, her brown hair loose; and Verrall stood behind her, one hand on the chair, saying something that made her look up and smile. It was the old smile, the sum-doing smile, and it was not for him.

He cocked the weapon. The click was very loud.

And then the light changed.

It was not a flash. It was a deepening, as though the air itself had been dipped in green water and the whole world were being poured into a vast glass. He felt it in his throat, sweet and cold, a taste like apples and iron. The lamp in the window paled. Nettie's face went soft and far away. He tried to lift the revolver, and his arm, which had been so steady, was heavy as a bar of lead, and then was not heavy at all.

"Parload was right," he said aloud, and was astonished to hear himself laugh.

The elms leaned over him. Somewhere the guns were still speaking, but gently now, like a man talking in his sleep. The revolver slid from his fingers into the grass, and he sank down after it, quite slowly, with the great green light pouring over him; and the last thing he knew was the pear tree shining against the wall, white and unafraid, as though the world had been waiting for this since the beginning.
