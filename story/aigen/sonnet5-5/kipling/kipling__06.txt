I.

In the Five Towns the smoke stands up from the bottle-ovens like the pillars of a heathen temple, and under it men are born, and bake, and are buried, and are none the wiser for the sun. William Leadford was born under it. He was a clerk at Swinnerton's, nineteen shillings a week, a narrow, black-browed man with a grievance for company and a book of figures for a conscience. He was twenty-two, and he loved Nettie Stuart.

He loved her as the poor do, who have nothing else to love with: wholly, sourly, and in dread. She was the head gardener's daughter, brown-haired and quick, with a laugh like a dropped spoon, and she had promised him nothing in particular and everything by implication.

"When I'm earning more," said Leadford, "we'll see."

"Ah, we'll see," said Nettie, looking down the road where the motor-cars went, throwing up their white dust.

That was in the spring. The newspapers had begun to print a small, cold thing on their back pages: that an astronomer at some far observatory had found a new comet, and that it was coming on. Leadford read it as he read the price of coal, and turned over. A comet was nothing to him. The rent was something. Nettie was everything.

He did not know that the comet had already begun to read him.

II.

It was Verrall who took her. Verrall of the Hall, the master's son, with his gloves and his easy voice and the money that fell from him as dust from a miller. He had spoken to her in the shrubbery twice, then six times, then not at all, for in June the two of them were gone. A note on the kitchen table said that Nettie was sorry; that it was no use; that she wanted things.

Old Stuart brought the news to Leadford at the works gate, holding his hat in both hands like a man at a graveside.

"They've gone to London, or beyond it," he said. "I'd kill him, Willie, if I'd the legs."

Leadford said nothing. He folded the paper that the old man had given him, folded it small, and put it in his waistcoat. Then he went in and added columns till the bell rang, and not one figure was wrong. That was the horror of it. Inside him a second man had stood up, and the first man went on working because there was nothing else for him to do.

That night he walked the Burslem road till dawn. The sky was clear above the furnaces, and low in the north-west, where the dark was thinnest, hung a smudge of pale light, like a thumbprint on a window. He looked at it without interest.

"Take her, then," he told it, and did not know whom he meant.

III.

There was one man in Burslem who cared for the comet, and that was Parload, who clerked at the same works and kept a brass telescope on a table in his mother's back bedroom. He was fair, stooping, and half blind with watching, and he spoke of the heavens as other men speak of a horse they have backed.

"It's the gas, Willie," he said, in the public bar of the Marquis, where the lamps were lit at four in the afternoon. "It's the spectrum. They've found a new line in it, and no one knows what it is. It'll cross us in the autumn."

"It'll cross the Kaiser too," said a potter with his pot at his lips. "And a good thing, say I."

That started them. For in that year, with the comet climbing, there was a second thing climbing with it: a noise from over the water, of fleets and ultimatums, of reserves called up and frontiers closed. The men in the Marquis argued it as they argued football. Some said Germany would never dare, others that she'd dare tomorrow, and all of them agreed that it would be over by Christmas and that someone else would do the dying.

Leadford drank his one pint slowly.

"There'll be war," said Parload. "And there's the comet. I don't know which is the bigger fool, the sky or us."

"Men are the bigger," said Leadford. "The sky doesn't pretend."

IV.

He had a revolver by September. He bought it from a pawnbroker in Hanley for eleven shillings and six cartridges thrown in, and he cleaned it on the kitchen table by the light of the lamp, with the dull care of a man doing a sum. He did not hurry. He found, by writing to a cousin who was in service, where Verrall had taken her: a rented villa on the Surrey side, hidden among pines and railway cuttings, and called, with the humour of the rich, "The Haven."

"You'll hang, Willie," said Parload, who had come round and stood in the door with a candle.

"Likely."

"For her?"

"For nothing," said Leadford. "That's the worst of it. For nothing at all."

He took the night train south. The third-class carriage was full of reservists, sleeping on one another's shoulders, and a boy opposite him was singing very low a song about a girl in Dundee. At every station there were soldiers and placards. At Rugby, through the dirty window, Leadford saw the comet plain for the first time, and he forgot, for the space of a breath, his own business.

It was no longer a smudge. It was a great pale-green eye, wide open, and from it a thin cold light lay over the sidings and the shunting engines and the sleeping men, as though the world were something already drowned and seen through water.

V.

He came to the pines at dusk on the third day, footsore, with the revolver heavy against his ribs. The villa stood quiet. A lamp was lit in an upper room; a gramophone somewhere was playing a waltz, tinny and pleased with itself. And over everything the sky had turned a colour that Leadford had no name for, a thin, clean, poisonous green, as if the heavens had been rinsed in absinthe.

He stood at the gate and heard, far off, a sound like a long tearing of cloth. It was guns. The war had begun that afternoon on the frontier, and the wind carried it, or he thought it did.

"Now," said the second man within him. "Now, Willie."

He drew the revolver. His hand was dry and steady. He had meant to say a speech, and he found he had none, only her name, which he said aloud once, to the pines. Then he saw that a mist had come up from the ground, a faint, glittering, greenish mist, no thicker than breath on a cold morning. It was sweet in his throat. It did not burn. It lay along the road and over the hedges and softly up about his knees.

The gramophone ran down in the middle of a bar. A dog barked once, and stopped. Leadford felt his knees give, quite gently, like a man's who lies down for a rest after long walking, and the last thing he saw was the green sky, bending closer, and very kind.

VI.

He woke in a clean white light, with wet grass against his cheek and a lark going up out of the field like a thread drawn through silk.

For a long while he lay and did not think at all. He was aware only that something had been lifted from him: a weight he had carried so long he had mistaken it for his own spine. The air was sweet as if it had never been breathed. The sky was blue and ordinary, and the green was gone, and the sun was coming up behind the pines as plainly and gladly as on the first morning of the world.

He sat up. The revolver lay by his hand in the dew. He looked at it as a man looks at a tool belonging to some other trade, and it seemed to him a small, mean, foolish thing, a child's toy made of iron. He could not now remember what he had wanted of it. He remembered Nettie, but the Nettie he remembered no longer tore at him; she stood in his mind as a girl stands in a photograph, frightened and young and wanting her bit of the world. And Verrall, he thought, was a boy also, who had wanted the same.

"Why, we were all mad," said Leadford aloud, and laughed, and the laugh startled him by being so clean.

There were men and women coming out of the cottages along the road, blinking, touching one another's hands. Nobody spoke at first. A soldier in a reservist's tunic sat on a milestone, weeping and smiling at once, and he had thrown his rifle into the ditch. Down the road, from the gate of The Haven, a man and a woman were walking, hand in hand, slowly, as people walk who have just been let out of prison.

Leadford stood, and brushed the grass from his knees, and went down to meet them with his empty hands held out before him.
