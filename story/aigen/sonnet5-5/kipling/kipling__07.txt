The Five Towns lie in their own smoke like cattle in a wet field, and the kilns stand round them with their bottle-necks to the sky. A man born there learns two things early: that clay is patient, and that money is not. William Leadford had learned both before he was twenty, and the third lesson, which is Woman, he was now taking at the hands of Nettie Stuart.

She was a draper's daughter, grey-eyed and quick, and she had been to a school where they taught the piano. Leadford had been to no such school. He was a clerk's drudge at a pot-bank, and his wage would have kept one man decently and two men badly. But he had a tongue in his head, a great anger against the order of things, and a way of looking at Nettie as if she were the only lit window in a dark street.

"Thee'lt wait for me, Nettie?" he said, one night by the canal, where the water lay black and greasy under the furnace-glare.

"I'll wait as long as it's sense to wait," said she, and laughed. A man in love should be wary of that kind of answer. It had a back door in it.

He did not see the door. He saw her hand on the lock-gate and the red light on her hair, and he went home through the slag-heaps singing, like a fool or a poet, which are brothers.

Verrall came in the spring, a tall young man in a tall hat, the son of the house that owned half the valley and had never soiled its hands with any part of it. He came to look at the works the way a man looks at a dog he has bought and does not mean to feed. He spoke a soft, expensive English. He did not look at Leadford at all, and that was an insult to match any blow.

He looked at Nettie, however, and Leadford saw it, and said nothing, because a poor man who speaks too soon loses even his silence.

Then came a Thursday in June. Nettie did not come to the canal. Her mother stood in the shop door with a face like a shut ledger and said, "She's gone, William. Gone up to London by the afternoon train. There's a letter." There was. It said that she was sorry, that she could not bear to be poor, that Mr. Verrall had been very kind, and that William would one day thank her for her honesty.

He read it twice standing in the street. A cart went by with crates of saucers, rattling like teeth. He did not feel anything yet. The feeling came afterward, as the noise comes after the flash, and when it came it was not grief at all.

All that summer there was a star in the wrong place.

The astronomers had found it first, and the newspapers made it a monster. It grew night by night, a smear of pale fire with a tail behind it, hanging low over the chimneys. Men stood in the streets at dusk and said that it would strike the Earth, or poison it, or do nothing, according to their temperaments and the beer in them.

"It's the end o' the world," said old Gammer Tonks at the Hope and Anchor.

"It's the end o' the month," said a collier, "and I want my wages."

But there was another talk under the comet-talk, and it was uglier. The papers were full of Germans and of Russians, of ships laid down and armies drilled, of ultimatums and notes. The great nations stood about Europe like drunken men in a narrow bar, each with a hand on his pocket, each swearing he wanted only peace. A child might have seen how it would end. Leadford, who was not a child, saw it and did not care. Let the whole world burn, he thought, since it had no room in it for him.

He lost his place at the works for brawling. He drank, and was sorry, and drank again. And one night he looked up at the swollen comet and spoke to it aloud, like a man to a creditor.

"Come on, then. Come an' have done."

He found out where they lived. A pot-bank clerk learns where letters go. They had taken a villa at Lonton, down in the soft south, among gardens, and Verrall's name was on the gate.

Leadford sold his watch, his books, and his father's Sunday coat. With the money he bought a revolver from a pawnbroker in Hanley, who asked no questions, as is the custom of that trade. He bought it, cartridges and all, as coolly as he would have bought a pound of tea. The coolness frightened him a little. It was not like him. It was like some other man, a stranger who had taken lodgings in his skull.

The train went south through a country he had never seen: wheat, elms, and slow white roads. The comet rode ahead of him in the dusk, and the sky about it had a curious greenish tinge, as if it had been breathed upon by something. The other passengers talked of it in low voices. A soldier going on leave said that the reservists were being called up in Germany. A woman said she would not like to be in London tonight.

Leadford sat with his hand in his pocket on the cold butt of the weapon, and he rehearsed his speech. It was a good speech. He had made it a hundred times in the potteries, to the kilns and the canal. He never got past the second sentence without seeing her face.

Lonton was very still when he came to it. The gardens lay warm and breathless, and the light was strange, neither day nor night but something poured between them, the colour of a bottle held to a lamp. Dogs were whining behind their gates. Not a leaf stirred.

He found the villa and the lighted window. Through the glass he saw them: Verrall lounging with a cigar, Nettie in a chair with her hands idle in her lap. She was thinner than he remembered, and she was not laughing. She was listening to something that was not in the room.

He raised the revolver. It was heavier than a man would credit.

Then the sky, which had been green, burned emerald from rim to rim, and a wind that was not a wind went over the garden without bending a blade of grass. It smelled of nothing. It was only a great clean coldness, such as a man might feel who dived into a mountain pool. Leadford saw Verrall's cigar fall. He saw Nettie lift her head. He heard, far off, a sound like a thousand voices sighing at once, and he thought, quite clearly, So it has come, and he did not mind.

His knees went from under him, and the lawn rose to take him gently, like a bed.

He woke with the dew on his face and the sun in his eyes.

He lay still a long while, wondering what had changed. Then he knew that it was himself. The anger was gone. It had not been driven out. It had been lifted off him like a wet coat, and under it his mind stood up straight, light and quiet, seeing everything as it was.

The revolver lay in the grass beside him. He looked at it with a kind of tender astonishment, as a grown man looks at the toy that once made him weep. What had he meant to do with it? Kill a man for being a fool, and a girl for being afraid? He thought of the potteries, the kilns, the little hard lives, the shopkeepers' prayers, the masters' talk of wages and war. It had all been a fever, a thing the whole world had done to itself in the dark, and the comet had come and broken it.

He rose. The garden was bright and washed, the air sweet to drink. At the window Verrall stood bareheaded, looking out like a man new-born, and Leadford saw that his face was not that of an enemy. It was only the face of another sleeper waking. Nettie came out upon the step, and for a moment they looked at each other across the dew without shame or hatred.

"William," she said, and said no more, for there was no more needed.

He laid the revolver on the sundial, as one lays down a tool whose work is over, and went to meet them.
