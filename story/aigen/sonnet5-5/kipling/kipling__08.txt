The Potteries do not weep for a man. They give him a bottle-oven to look at, a wage that is never quite the wage, and an evening of smoke the colour of old pewter, and they bid him make what he can of it. William Leadford, nineteen years in the world and eleven of them in the clay, made of it a great and sullen love for Nettie Stuart, who was slight and quick, and had grey eyes that looked at the far end of the street while her tongue answered you at the near.

"I'll have thee, Nettie," he had told her on the canal bridge at Etruria, "when I've bettered myself."

"Betterin' takes a deal o' time, Will," said she, and laughed.

He should have heard the whole of the answer in that laugh. A man in love hears only the half he likes.

There was a Verrall: a young gentleman with a motor-car, a good tailor, and a voice like warm cream, who came to look at the works for his father and stayed to look at the girls. He looked longest at Nettie. On a wet Thursday in March she did not come to her mother's door, and her box was gone from under the bed, and a ticket-collector at Stoke recalled "a young lady, very pale, along with a gentleman wot paid for two firsts."

Leadford heard it standing at the wheel with his hands in the slip. He finished the pot he was throwing. It was a good pot. It was the last good thing he did that year.

After that, a sourness got into him, as damp gets into a rick. He lost his place for insolence, and found another, and lost that for drink. He took lodgings in Hanley, over a chandler's, and there he read the newspapers, because a man must hate something in print when he cannot hate it in person.

The papers that spring had two matters to tell. The first was the Comet, discovered by an observer at the Cape: a smudge, a rumour, a thing no larger than a thumbprint on the glass, but growing nightly. The second was the Continent, which was arming itself, as a cat arms itself in a corner, with fluffed fur and a fixed eye.

"They say," observed the chandler's man, "it'll strike us."

"It'll strike nobody," said Leadford. "Comets is like gentlemen. They go where they please and leave the bill with the poor."

"There's to be war wi' Germany."

"Good," said Leadford. "Let 'em send a few of the Verralls."

He said it lightly, but he was not light. Behind his eyes a small red room had been furnished, and in it a revolver lay on a table, and two people stood against a wall. He bought the revolver on a Saturday, from a pawnbroker who asked no questions because the answers would have been dull.

They had gone, so he learned, to the south, and the south was a long road for a man with eleven shillings. He walked a good part of it, and rode the rest on the tail of carters' goodwill, and the nights grew strange as he went.

For the Comet had come up now into the evening sky, and it was no thumbprint. It hung above the elms like a pale green lamp turned low, with a tail that spread behind it as a woman's veil spreads in water. The cattle would not graze beneath it. Dogs lay with their noses to the ground and whined at nothing. At a public-house in Oxfordshire a drover said that his grandfather had seen a comet in the year of Waterloo, and that had meant blood, and this one, by the look of it, meant more.

"Meant a good deal of corn, too," said the landlord, who was a fair man, "that year."

"It's the Germans I mind," said the drover. "The papers is full of 'em. Ships, they say. Ships and more ships."

Leadford said nothing. He lay that night in a haystack, and the green light came sifting through the straw upon his face, and he thought, quite calmly, of the sound the revolver would make. A man can think so calmly of such things only when he has already died inside, and is merely waiting for the body to catch up.

He found them at a villa near Pangbourne, set among lawns, with a white gate and a cat asleep on the step: Verrall's villa, taken for the season, and Nettie within it.

He saw her first through the window. She was sitting alone with a book she was not reading, and she was thinner. Something had gone from her that the clay-country had liked in her, a kind of brightness. Then Verrall came in, bored and handsome, and said some trifle over his shoulder. She flinched; but she answered him as a woman answers who has long since stopped expecting to be answered back.

Leadford stood in the shrubbery with the revolver heavy in his coat. It was an odd thing. The red room was in his head still, but the people in it were smaller than he had planned, and one of them looked tired.

"Damn them," he whispered. "Damn the two of them."

A wind rose then, from nowhere, and it was warm and smelled faintly of a thing he knew only from the chemist's. The sky had turned from violet to a deep, unnatural green, and the Comet, enormous now, seemed not to be above the world but to be bending down toward it. Far off, over the Berkshire downs, there came a dull sound like a door slammed in another house; and then another, and another.

"Guns," said Leadford. "That's the guns. They've begun."

He drew the revolver. His hand shook, and he steadied it with the other.

He never fired it. Afterward he could not have said why, except that the air thickened in his throat like warm milk, and the green flooded the lawn until every blade stood out in it separately, as though cut from glass. He saw the cat rise, stretch, and fall over on its side. He saw Verrall come running out of the house with his mouth open, and stop, and sink down upon the gravel with a puzzled and gentle look, as a tired child sinks. He heard, miles away, the guns falter, and cease, and leave behind them a silence so large that the very leaves seemed to hold their breath.

"Nettie," he said, and could not say more.

The revolver slid out of his fingers. He thought: So this is how the world ends; not with a bang, but with a yawn. Then the green closed over him like a sea, and there was neither Staffordshire nor Berkshire nor any grievance in it.

He woke to birdsong, which he had not heard since boyhood. The sky was a clean, washed blue, and the air tasted as water tastes to a man who has crossed a desert. He lay on the grass with the dew on his coat and felt for his hatred, as one feels in the dark for a familiar tool, and could not find it.

The revolver lay a yard away. He looked at it a long time. It seemed a foolish, ugly little object, like a toy that a rough boy had broken and been ashamed of. He picked it up, and, with no particular ceremony, dropped it into the garden pond, where it went down among the lilies.

Verrall was sitting up on the gravel, rubbing his eyes. He looked at Leadford without fear and without insolence, as one man looks at another on the first morning after a long fever. "I have been," he said slowly, "a great fool and a great scoundrel. I think I knew it all the while, and slept on it."

"Aye," said Leadford. "So have I, I reckon."

There was nothing in either voice but wonder. Beyond the hedge a ploughman was whistling; beyond the ploughman, along the distant railway, a train was moving, and no one in it, Leadford somehow knew, was going to a war. Nettie had come to the door and stood there in the light with her hand upon the post, looking not at the far end of the street but straight at him.

"Will," she said. "Oh, Will. Is it true? Is it all over?"

"It's all begun," said William Leadford, and went up the path to meet her.
