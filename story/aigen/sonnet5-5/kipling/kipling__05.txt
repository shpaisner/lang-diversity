The Five Towns lay under their usual smoke when the comet first showed itself, and the smoke made nothing of it. It hung over the bottle-kilns of Staffordshire like a smear of verdigris on a dirty plate, and men looked up from the pavements, said "Ah," and went in to their tea. William Leadford looked up too. He was a thin, hard-worked clerk with a pottery-man's pallor, and he had a letter in his pocket that he had not yet the courage to burn.

It had come on a Tuesday, in Nettie's round schoolroom hand, and it said she was sorry. She was sorry, it said, but she could not go on living as she had lived, in a house that smelt of boiled cabbage and clay-dust, waiting on a man whose largest ambition was a rise of two shillings. She was gone with Mr. Verrall. She hoped Willie would think kindly of her, in time.

"In time," said Leadford to the kitchen wall. The wall, which had heard many such remarks, returned him nothing.

That night he climbed the slag-heap behind Clayton Lane, where the boys played at soldiers, and sat with his knees drawn up. The comet stood in the north-east, small and cold and green. He hated it without knowing why. It looked, he thought, like something that waited.

~

He had known Verrall by sight a year before he knew him by name. Verrall was the son of the great house on the hill, and he drove a motor-car through the mean streets with the careless courtesy of one who had never been asked the price of anything. He was not a bad man. Leadford, who would have given a year of his life to find him one, had been forced to admit it. He was merely a man who had always had what he reached for, and had reached, one afternoon in June, for Nettie.

She had been at the gate, in her blue print, when the car stopped to mend a tyre. That was all. Leadford had made much of it afterwards, lying awake; the tyre, the gate, the blue print, the lift of the wrist as she gave the stranger a cup of water. A man may be undone by very small hinges. He remembered that she had laughed at something Verrall said, and that the laugh was not the one she kept for him.

He had never been clever with words, and he had no words now. What he had was a slow Staffordshire anger that burned from the floor of him upward, like a kiln drawing, and by the end of the week it had reached his head. The bolts of the world, he decided, were all rusted fast, and a poor man might beat on them till his knuckles bled.

~

The Black Swan was loud that Saturday, and not about Nettie. The talk was of Germany.

"They'll have us by Michaelmas," said old Marlow, the saggar-maker's bottom-knocker, who knew everything. "Mark me. It's in the papers. Ships here, ships there, Kaisers saying this, Czars saying that. Somebody lights the match and the whole street goes up."

"And what's that to do with you, Tom?" said another. "You're sixty-two."

"It's to do with all of us. There's a rottenness about. Look at that thing in the sky."

They all looked, through the dim window, where the comet had grown in a fortnight from a green coin to a green hand. The newspapers called it a visitor. The astronomers, a little pale, called it a nucleus and a tail, and said that the Earth would pass through the tail, and that there was no cause for alarm. Leadford noticed that the men who said so did not sleep well either.

He drank his half-pint slowly. A small bitter voice within him was growing very reasonable. If the world is going to burn, who will mind one more small fire?

~

On the Monday he went to a pawnbroker's in Hanley and bought a revolver, which the man wrapped in brown paper as though it were a pair of boots. It cost him eleven shillings and seven weeks of saving for a ring that now would never be given. He carried it home against his ribs, and it was heavier than it had any right to be.

That evening a telegram lay in the passage. Mobilisation. The reserves were called, and the newspaper-boys ran shouting in Waterloo Road with their bills held up like banners. Men stood in knots at every corner. Some looked glad, which Leadford thought the worst thing of all. Some women cried, and some did not, and those who did not looked older.

He did not hear much of it. He had a map spread on the kitchen table, and a finger upon it, travelling from Stoke down to the green county where Verrall's friends kept a villa among the beech-woods. He had learned the place from a gossiping carter. It would be a day's journey, if the trains ran. He folded the map small. He laid out his Sunday suit, as a man might for a funeral, and he did not know whose.

~

The trains ran, but they ran like frightened cattle. Every platform was a flood of khaki and cheap bags, and the carriages stank of tobacco, wet wool, and fear. Leadford sat wedged among reservists who sang at first, and then slept, and then woke and did not sing. Out of the window the country slid by, hayfields and elms and little quiet stations, and over all of it burned the comet, now big as a dinner-plate, pouring a light that was not any colour a man ought to see on grass.

"It's the end o' the world, mate," said a corporal, kindly, seeing the look on his face.

"No," said Leadford. "Only of some of it."

He reached the green county at dusk, and walked the last six miles, because there was no cab and no carter. The lanes were silent. No dog barked. The birds, he noticed, had stopped altogether, and the hush lay on the hedges like a hand. His boots rang on the road. The revolver rode against his side, and he found that he was saying over and over, in no tune, the name of the girl he had meant to marry.

~

The villa stood at the end of a beech avenue, and a window in it was lit.

He reached the gate as the sky changed. First it was only a paleness, as of a shaded lamp turned up, and then the green grew in the air itself, a thin clean sweetness drifting between the trunks. It did not smell of gas. It smelt of rain on a hot day, of cut apples. He felt it enter his chest and was astonished that it did not choke him. Far off, toward London, a gun spoke, and then another; and then, across the whole hushed valley, the guns stopped, one after the other, like men lowering their voices in church.

He had the revolver in his hand. He could not remember drawing it. Through the lit window he saw them, Verrall and Nettie, standing very still, looking up, and Nettie's face had the strange wide stillness of a child's. She was not afraid. That was the unbearable part. She looked glad.

"Nettie," said Leadford, and the word came out small and ordinary. His anger, that had drawn so fiercely in him for a month, wavered like a fire in a changing draught. He raised the pistol, and his arm was heavy as clay, and the green light lay along the barrel and made it look like a toy.

His knees went first. The beeches wheeled over him, and the stars, and the great green hand of the comet opening above. So this is the finish, he thought, without any terror. He sank among the leaves, and the revolver slid from his fingers, and the last thing he knew was a vast kindly quiet, like the quiet at the end of a long day's work when the kilns are drawn and the men go home.
