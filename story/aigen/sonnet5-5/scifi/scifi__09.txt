The Potteries lay under their usual pall that autumn, and William Leadford walked through it as a man walks through a dream he cannot wake from. The bottle-kilns smoked against a sky the colour of dishwater; the canal showed a skin of iridescent filth; and above it all, on every clear night, hung the Comet, a smear of pale green fire that grew a little larger with each passing week.

He had no eyes for it. He was twenty-two, a clerk's son fallen to the work of a drudge, and for three years he had carried Nettie Stuart in his mind as a pilgrim carries a relic. She had been the one clean thing in Staffordshire. Now her father's door stood open, her bed was cold, and on the kitchen table lay a note in her round hand: Don't look for me, Willie. I am going away with Mr. Verrall. I am sorry.

Verrall: the squire's son, with his white hands, his motor-car, and his easy way of looking at a working man as one looks at a fence post.

Leadford read the note until the words lost all meaning. Then he went out into the street, where the evening paper boys were crying something about Germany and the Channel fleet, and he understood none of it. The universe had been reduced to a single point, and the point was a pain behind his breastbone.

At the works, the next morning, the men talked of nothing but the Comet and the war.

"Professors say it's a gas," said old Hickson, leaning on his barrow. "Cyanogen. Come to poison the lot of us."

"Then it'll save the Kaiser the trouble," said another, and they laughed in the flat, hopeless way of men who have long ceased to expect anything.

Leadford said nothing. He shaped clay with his hands and thought only of Verrall. The thought had hardened in him overnight, like glaze in a kiln, and had taken on a bright, brittle finish.

By noon the foreman had read the posters at the station. Reservists were to report. The Foreign Office had spoken; the German Chancellor had replied; and in Whitehall, men in black coats were drawing lines across maps as though the earth were a pastry to be divided.

"It'll be war by Friday," Hickson said.

"Let it come," said Leadford, and was surprised at the hollow ring of his own voice. "Let it all come."

That evening he went to a pawnbroker's in Hanley and bought a revolver for thirty shillings, with six cartridges in a twist of paper. The pawnbroker did not look at his face. Nobody in those days looked at anybody's face; every man was shut up in his own small anxiety, as in a cell.

He learned from a servant at the Verrall house, bribed with half a crown, that the pair had gone south, to a cottage near the Kentish downs where the young man's mother kept a summer retreat. Leadford took his savings from the tin box under his bed, bound them in a handkerchief, and caught the night train to London.

The carriage was crowded with young men in civilian clothes who already wore the expression of soldiers: that blank, patient, slightly foolish look of animals being driven toward a gate. Some sang. One wept quietly in a corner. Through the grimed window, as the train rattled southward, the Comet blazed above the fields, now so large that it threw a faint shadow from the telegraph poles, a wan green moon with a hundred feathered streamers.

"They say it'll touch us Thursday," said a man opposite him, a commercial traveller by the look of him. "Touch us, mind you. Brush us, like a woman's skirt."

Leadford fingered the cold shape in his pocket.

"I shouldn't think it matters," he said.

"No," said the traveller, after a pause. "No, I don't suppose it does."

London was a hive struck with a stick. Omnibuses carried placards; newsboys ran with sheets still wet; crowds shouted before the clubs in Pall Mall, and the night sky above the chimneys was streaked with the green ghost-light. He pushed through it, sleepless, unshaven, a small dark figure with one fixed idea, and took a slow train into Kent.

Beyond Sevenoaks the trains stopped altogether. Troops were moving on the line, and the guard, white and harassed, told the passengers to find their own way. So Leadford walked. He walked all that day along white roads thick with carts and bicycles and marching men, while the rumour ran ahead of him that the first shots had been fired, that a cruiser had gone down off the Dutch coast, that the Germans had landed, that nothing of the kind had happened.

The air was curiously still. The birds had gone silent in the hedges. The light took on a strange quality, as if the world were being seen through a bottle of absinthe, and the faces of the soldiers he passed looked sickly and unreal.

At dusk he sat by a gate to rest and looked up. The Comet had swollen to fill a third of heaven. It was no longer a star, but a vast green curtain, shot through with threads of white and violet, folding down upon the earth with an awful deliberation. A peculiar sweetness touched his nostrils.

On the hill ahead, a battery of guns stood unlimbered, and the gunners were staring upward, their mouths open. One of them laughed, a strange, high, delighted laugh.

Leadford rose to his feet. His hand closed on the revolver.

"Nettie," he whispered, as the green swallowed the stars.

Then the sky fell down in a soft silence, and the world went out.

He woke on his back in the grass, and for a long while did not know that he was awake.

The sky above him was blue, a clear, trembling, luminous blue he had not seen since childhood, if he had ever seen it. The sun was rising through a haze of gold. The air tasted like cold water after a fever. Every blade of grass beside his cheek stood separate and brilliant, each with its bead of dew, and he lay and looked at them with a kind of astonishment that was almost tears.

He sat up slowly. The revolver lay in the grass beside him. He looked at it as a man looks at some curious tool from a vanished age, a thing whose use he could remember but whose meaning had drained away. Why had he wanted it? The memory of his rage was there, intact, like a room in a house he had left; but he walked through it now as a stranger, and found it small, mean, and rather pitiful.

On the hill the gunners were stirring. They sat up one by one, rubbing their eyes, and looked at one another, and at the guns, and at the dawn. One of them stood and stretched out his arms to the light. Another began to weep, and a third put an arm round him, and no one found this strange. Not a word of command was given. The guns stood silent, absurd, like iron toys.

Leadford rose. His body felt light, his mind washed and clear, as if some grey film had been lifted from it. He understood, without any argument, that the gas of the Comet had changed the very air; that the old fear, the old jealousy, the old dull cruelty of men had been burnt out of the blood. He understood that Verrall, and Nettie, and he himself, had been creatures caught in a sour and foolish game, and that the game was over.

He took up the revolver, walked to a ditch, and let it fall into the black water. Then he turned toward the south, toward the cottage and the two people in it, and walked on through the shining morning, not to kill, but to speak.
