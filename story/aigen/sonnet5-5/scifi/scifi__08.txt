The Potteries lay under their usual canopy of smoke, and William Leadford walked home through it with his collar turned up and his hands numb from the cold. Along the canal the bottle-kilns stood like blackened chessmen, and the slag heaps glowed dull red where the evening furnaces breathed upon them. He was twenty-two, a clerk in a pottery office at a wage that would not have kept a respectable dog, and he had a single possession of any worth: the belief that Nettie Stanton would one day be his wife.

Over the roofs, in the strip of sky the smoke permitted, hung the new star. It had been a pinpoint in the autumn. Now it had a faint, smudged tail, and the newspapers spoke of it with the cheerful vagueness of men who understood nothing. Leadford disliked it. It seemed to him an idle thing, a spectator.

"It's coming for us," said the man at the corner, grinning.

"Let it," Leadford muttered. "It would find little changed."

He climbed the steep street to the Stantons' door. Nettie's face appeared at the window, pale and fine, and then withdrew. He stood in the cold, rehearsing what he would say, and found, as he always found, that the words had gone out of him like heat from a cooling kiln.

The letter came on a Thursday, left on the table in his lodging between a gas bill and a tract on temperance. He knew her handwriting. It was round and careful, the writing of a girl who had been taught that neatness was a kind of virtue.

Dear Willie, I am going away. Do not look for me. You are good and I am not good enough for you, and I cannot bear the grey of it any longer. Verrall has been kind to me. He has shown me there is a world that is not made of smoke. Forgive me if you can.

He read it twice, then laid it flat and smoothed it as though it were a bandage over some wound in the table.

Verrall. He knew the name too well: the idle son of the big house at Clayhanger, with his motor-car, his gloves, his easy and resplendent laugh. Leadford had once watched him lean from that car and speak to Nettie at the market, and had seen her face open like a flower under lamplight.

The room contracted. Through the cracked window the comet glimmered above the chimneys, larger now, a pale and feathered wound in the heavens. He thought, with an odd distinctness: Everything is ending, and no one but I has noticed.

Parload found him at the works gate the following Monday. He was a lanky, harassed fellow with ink on his cuffs and an amateur's passion for the sky, who kept a small telescope on the roof of his father's house.

"You look dreadful," Parload said. "Come and look at the thing. It'll do you good to see something larger than yourself."

On the roof the wind was sharp. Through the little brass tube the comet swam up: a ghostly head, a tail flung out across a quarter of the dark. Parload spoke of cyanogen in the spectrum, of a poison that the tail might carry, of the astronomers who shrugged and the journalists who did not.

"Doesn't matter much, I suppose," Parload said. "The papers are full of Germany now. And the Balkans. And the Admiralty. Everyone is polishing their guns. Odd, isn't it? The heavens send a warning, and we answer by arming."

Leadford said nothing. Below them the town glittered meanly, and beyond it, in the dark, lay all of Europe: crowded, nervous, bristling with frontiers and railway timetables of mobilization. He felt the continent as a held breath, a great stupid animal gathering itself to spring.

"She's gone with Verrall," he said at last.

Parload lowered the telescope. "Ah," he said, and for once said nothing more.

He bought the revolver in Hanley, from a pawnbroker who asked no questions and wrapped it in brown paper like a loaf. It was heavy and cold. That night Leadford sat with it on his knees and felt nothing at all: neither rage, nor even hatred, but a vast, level resolve, like a road ruled across a plain.

He took the morning train south. Third-class carriages thronged with reservists and soured clerks, and every conversation bent toward the same subject, so that the whole country seemed to mutter in one voice. They'll not stand for it. We'll show them. It'll be over by Christmas. At a junction a recruiting sergeant sang bawdy verses, and a boy of seventeen laughed too loud. Leadford watched them with the stony pity of one already condemned.

The comet rode high now, even by day a pale smear, and at dusk it blazed. The sky was growing strange: a greenish pallor along the horizon, a hush upon the fields. Cattle stood motionless in the meadows with their heads lifted. Dogs howled along the line.

By evening he had reached the neighbourhood of Verrall's father's house, and he went up through the black beechwoods with the weapon in his pocket and the whole of his small life narrowed to a single bright purpose. Somewhere beyond the trees there was a lamp burning. Somewhere there was Nettie.

He found them in a summerhouse at the edge of the park, two shapes against a lantern. He halted in the wet bracken, perhaps forty paces off, and drew the revolver. His hand did not shake. That surprised him.

Then the light changed.

It was not the lantern. It was the sky. A green radiance rose from beyond the trees and flooded the lawn, the beeches, the pale faces in the summerhouse, until the world seemed to lie at the bottom of a luminous sea. Verrall stood, and Nettie stood, and both stared upward with their mouths a little open. The air grew sweet, then sweeter, with a clean cold taste like water drawn from deep rock.

"Look," Nettie said, very softly.

Leadford looked. The heavens had become a vast curtain of emerald fire, rippling slowly, folding over the horizon like the wing of some immense creature. He raised the revolver again, but it was absurdly heavy, and the thought of using it drifted from him like smoke through an open window. He tried to curse. His tongue would not obey. His knees loosened. Somewhere an owl cried out once and fell silent.

The green deepened to the colour of the sea, then of sleep. He felt the earth coming up to meet him, soft and unresisting, and the last thing he saw was the great sweeping brightness, and in it, absurdly, the figure of Nettie slowly folding down upon the grass, as though bowing to some invisible and gracious power.

He woke to a sky of perfect blue.

For a long while he lay quite still, unable to remember why he was lying in bracken, why his clothes were soaked with dew, why he was so uncommonly happy. The air was keen and sweet. Birds were singing, a great many birds, and their voices seemed to him to have in them some new quality of meaning, as if he had never really listened before. He sat up. The revolver lay in the leaves beside him, a small ugly shape. He regarded it with wonder, as one regards a toy left behind from a childhood nightmare.

I was going to kill a man, he thought. And the thought, instead of burning, passed through him like cold clean water, and left only an immense astonishment at the narrowness of the person who had meant it.

He rose. The park gleamed. Away across the lawn, the summerhouse door stood open, and two figures were stirring on the grass: Verrall sitting up with a hand to his brow, Nettie kneeling beside him. They looked at one another. Then, slowly, they looked at the sky, and at the world, and each seemed to understand the other's wonder without a word.

Leadford walked toward them, empty-handed. Whatever had come down out of heaven in the night had taken the gas and the grudge out of the air and out of the heart. He saw Verrall's face with no envy in it, and Nettie's with no longing, only a vast and tender recognition, as of fellow-travellers waking in the same strange country.

"Good morning," he said, and found that his voice shook, not with rage but with joy. "I think we have all been asleep a very long time."

Far away, over the hills, no guns were speaking. The comet had gone, and the day was new.
