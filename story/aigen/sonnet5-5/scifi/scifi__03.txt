I.

In those days the Potteries lay under their own smoke like a sick man under a blanket, and I, William Leadford, lay under it with them. I was twenty-two, a clerk by courtesy and a hand at Rawdon's works by necessity, and I lodged with my mother in a row of brick cottages whose windows had been black so long that nobody remembered them clean. Above the bottle-kilns the sky was usually the colour of a dirty plate. But that autumn, on the rare nights when the furnace-glare lifted, a small smear of pale light hung among the stars, and the men at the Marquis of Granby's would point their pipe-stems at it and say, "That's your comet, Bill. Coming to see us." Then they would laugh.

I did not laugh. I had no laughter in me that autumn. I had Nettie, or I had had her.

She was a housemaid's daughter from up the Trentham road, quick and dark and discontented, and I had loved her with the sullen, hungry love of a man who owns nothing else. We had walked out for two years. I had promised her a future I could not see, and she, with her fine restless eyes, had never quite believed me. Then Verrall came.

II.

Verrall was the son of the great house at Lugton, a slim, easy, sunburnt young man who wore his clothes as though they cost him nothing, because they did. He had a motor-car, a rare and shocking thing then, and he had a way of looking at a girl as if the world were a shop and she were something he had not yet priced. I saw him look at Nettie once, at the gate of the Park, and I felt in my stomach the cold drop of a lift descending.

She left on a Thursday. There was no scene, only an envelope on my mother's table and a few lines in her round board-school hand. She said she was sorry. She said I should not understand, and that she could not be a potter's wife and watch herself grow grey. She said Verrall would take her abroad.

I read it standing, with the kettle singing behind me. Then I went out and walked until the lamps were lit, and I do not remember one street of that walk. I remember only the comet, larger now, hanging low over the kilns like a pale thumbprint on the dark, and I remember hating it, as one hates anything bright and indifferent when one is hurt.

III.

The newspapers that winter spoke of two things, and I read them both with the same bitter attention. One was the comet. Astronomers wrote grave, hedged letters. The thing was a vagrant from beyond Neptune, they said, and its spectrum showed a strange new line, a green band no earthly flame had ever drawn. Nothing, they added, was likely to come of it. Men who say that nothing is likely to come of a thing are usually uneasy.

The other was war. It was in the leaders and the music-halls and the very air. Germany was building ships; we were building more. A quarrel in the Balkans, a quarrel over a colony, a speech from a Minister: each was a spark falling on straw. In the works the men talked of it with a kind of grim relish, as if war were a holiday from the rolling-mill of their days. I heard clergymen preach peace and saw recruiting sergeants in the market square. All Europe seemed to me a great house in which every man had a hand upon his neighbour's throat and was waiting only for someone to cry the signal.

And I thought: let it come. Let the whole rotten structure burn, and Verrall in it. I was not myself. A man who has been robbed of the one thing he owned grows large with an ugly, clean-edged purpose.

IV.

I bought the revolver from a pawnbroker in Hanley for seven shillings and sixpence. It was foreign, rusted at the cylinder, and I cleaned it at night while my mother slept, bent over it by a candle with my heart going slow and heavy. I knew from a cousin in service where Verrall had brought her back to: Lugton, whose house stood empty while the old man was in town. They had returned, he said, and were to be there that week.

I set out on the night the comet came nearest. The whole country had gone strange. Trains ran late and half-empty, and men stood about the platforms with their faces tilted up. The thing filled a quarter of the heavens now, a great pale smoky eye with a dim fringe about it, and the light it threw was a thin, unwholesome silver in which the hedgerows had no colour at all. In the carriage a drunken man was singing a hymn, and a woman wept quietly, without knowing why.

I walked the last miles from the station. The air was very still. The birds had fallen silent, and I remember that even the dogs were not barking. Behind the black cutout of Lugton wood the sky was turning, slowly, to a sickly and luminous green.

V.

I had reached the lodge gates when the first wind came. It was not a wind that moved the trees. It lay along the ground like a mist, faintly sweet, with a cool, thin taste like the taste of metal on the tongue. I saw it coiling pale emerald about my boots, and above me the stars swam as though seen through water.

I knew, with a clearness that astonished me, that this was the thing the astronomers had not named. The comet's tail had touched the world. I thought of my mother. I thought, absurdly, of the kettle. I put my hand into my pocket and closed it on the revolver, and said aloud, "Not yet, not yet," as if a man could bargain with a sky.

Then my knees went from under me. I did not feel myself fall. I felt the gravel against my cheek and the breath going out of me, not painfully, but with a long, sinking comfort, like the end of a day's labour. Somewhere far off I heard a clock striking and could not count the strokes. The last thing I saw was the green light spreading over the whole sky, soft as a hand drawn across a forehead, and I remember thinking that it was not terrible at all; that it was, if anything, kind.

VI.

I woke to a morning I had no words for.

The sun was well up. I lay on my back in the lodge drive with dew in my hair, and above me was a sky so clear and deep a blue that for a long time I could only lie and look at it. The green was gone. The air tasted new, like water drunk after a fever, and every leaf of the beeches stood out edged and separate and brilliant, as if the world had been washed and set out to dry. The smoke of the Potteries, which had hung over every dawn of my life, was simply not there.

I sat up. My head was quite clear. That was the marvel of it. The thick black knot I had carried in my chest for weeks had gone, and I looked at it, as a man looks at a stone he has set down, with wonder that I could ever have borne its weight. The revolver lay in the grass beside me. I picked it up and it felt ugly and absurd, a small dead tool of a dead way of thinking. I broke it open and shook the cartridges into my palm, and let them fall.

Then I saw them coming up the drive, Verrall and Nettie, walking slowly, hand in hand, bareheaded in the sun. Her face was not the face I had known, nor his; both had been rinsed of their hurry and their cunning. They looked at me, and I at them, and there was no anger anywhere.

"Leadford," said Verrall, gently, as though he had known me all my life. "You've come a long way in the night."

"Yes," I said, and found that I was smiling. "I think we all have."
