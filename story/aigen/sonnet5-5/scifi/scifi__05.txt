The Potteries lay under their usual pall that October, a long smear of bottle-kilns and slag heaps and sooty brick, and William Leadford walked through it as a man walks through a house after a death. The letter was in his pocket, creased soft from handling. It was short. I cannot go on as we were. Do not look for me. Nettie. She had not needed to write the other name. All Burslem knew that Verrall's motor-car had been seen idling at the corner of Hope Street on Tuesday night, and that a girl in a grey coat had run to it with her hat in her hand.

He had been a clerk in a pot-works until the firm shrank and let him go. Now he carried crates for eleven shillings a week and lodged with a mother who coughed through the wall. He had nothing to offer Nettie but a promise: Wait, and I'll get on. He understood now how small a thing that was beside a young man with a car, a father with land, and a laugh that cost him nothing.

At night he sat on his bed and said the name over, Verrall, Verrall, until it stopped being a name and became a taste in his mouth, bitter as pot-glaze.

Outside, over the chimney-pots, something pale had begun to hang in the east.

"You've not looked up at all, have you?" said Parload.

They stood on the cinder-covered hill above Clayton, where Parload, who clerked by day and watched the heavens by night, kept a battered brass telescope lashed to a tripod. Leadford had come to him because he could think of no one else who would not offer sympathy.

"Look," said Parload.

Leadford bent to the eyepiece. A smudge swam there, a ghost-feather of light with a bright knot at its heart, and beyond it the hard cold points of ordinary stars.

"It was a speck in June," said Parload. "Now it is growing a tail. They say it will pass very near us. And its spectrum—" He became technical, glad of it. "There is cyanogen in it. And a green line I can't place. Something new."

"Does it matter?" said Leadford.

"The newspapers think it matters less than the Balkans."

That was true. The papers Leadford had read at the carriers' yard were full of ultimatums and frontier incidents, of Germany's grievances and France's patience, of fleets steaming to exercises that were not quite exercises. Men at the works spoke of war with a sort of hungry cheerfulness. It would be a clean thing, they said. It would settle matters.

Leadford looked once more at the pale smear. It seemed to him that it was looking back, with the slow indifference of something that would arrive in its own time.

He found Verrall's address by theft and patience: a letter in the porter's rack at the Swan, a forwarding mark, a village in the south Midlands named Hinton Quarry. It was a poor, ugly little discovery. He had expected rage to make him cunning, but cunning came like a clerk's habit, and he did it all neatly, without feeling.

The revolver he bought in Hanley from a man who had been a soldier and now sold odds and ends from a barrow. It was a heavy old Webley with a worn butt, and six cartridges in a screw of brown paper.

"Rats?" said the man.

"Something like it."

He told his mother he had work in Crewe. She looked at him with her tired pale eyes and said nothing. On the platform the evening train smelt of coal and wet wool, and a newsboy was shouting that the Austrian ambassador had been recalled. The people in the carriage argued about it with the satisfaction of those who expect to watch from a distance. A stout man said Britain would keep out, and a thin one said that she never did.

The comet hung over the signal-gantry, larger now and tinged with a faint sea-green. Leadford sat with the revolver against his thigh, hard and cold through the cloth, and thought of Nettie's mouth.

By the second day of his journey Europe had begun to come apart. At Rugby the station was jammed with reservists, men in bowlers and cloth caps with brown-paper parcels under their arms, being cheered by women who were not quite cheering. A train of horse-boxes stood on a siding, and the horses stamped and rolled their eyes at the sky.

Everybody looked at the sky. It was no longer possible not to. The comet had swollen to a great luminous fan that spread over a third of the heavens, and its light had stained the night a faint unnatural green. Faces looked corpse-like and the grass looked sick. Dogs wouldn't settle. Somewhere beyond the platform a band was playing and a man was singing flat.

"They say we'll go through the tail on Thursday," said a porter. "Gas, they say. Poison."

"Do they?" said Leadford.

"Does it matter, sir? There'll be war by Friday."

Leadford carried on walking. He was aware, distantly, that the whole huge fabric of his world, the factories and treaties and loyalties and bankers' fears, had been wound to the point of snapping, and that he, with his one small private hatred, was a minor wheel in the same machinery. The thought brought no comfort. It only made the revolver heavier. He turned his collar up against a wind that had begun to smell, very faintly, of something like crushed almonds.

Hinton Quarry was a straggle of grey cottages around a disused pit, and the house stood a mile beyond it on the edge of a heath, a villa too new and white for the landscape. Leadford reached it at dusk. The whole sky had turned the colour of a bruise going green, and across it the comet's tail spread like a vast frozen fire, so bright that the heather threw shadows.

There was a lamp in the lower window. He crept to the hedge and looked.

Nettie sat by the hearth with a book open on her knee and did not read it. She was thinner. Her hair was pinned up in a way he did not know, and she wore a dress he had not paid for. Verrall came and stood behind her chair, handsome, restless, and said something. She answered without turning. Leadford could not hear the words, but he knew the sound of them. It was the sound of two people already tired of each other and neither willing to say so.

He had thought it would feel like triumph. It felt only like cold. His fingers, closing on the butt of the Webley, were numb. Overhead the sky had begun to shimmer, in long slow ripples as if a curtain were being drawn across the stars, and the air tasted sweet and strange. A bird fell silent in the hedge. A second one, then every bird. The lamplight in the window took on an emerald edge.

He drew the revolver and walked to the door.

The latch gave. He stood in the passage with the pistol raised and the smell of that sweetish air coming in behind him, and Verrall turned in the doorway of the room and saw him.

"Good God," Verrall said. "It's the Staffordshire man."

Nettie stood. She did not scream. She looked at the revolver, and then at Leadford's face, and what he saw in hers was not guilt, nor even fear, but a kind of tired pity that was worse than either. "Willie," she said. "Oh, Willie."

"Stand away from her," he said. His voice shook.

"Don't be a fool," said Verrall, but he said it faintly, and his eyes had drifted to the window. The glass glowed green. The lamp-flame had gone pale and tall and strange, and a thin vapour was creeping across the floor from the open door, curling about their ankles like a quiet animal.

Leadford breathed it. He had meant to hold his breath and could not. It went down into him cool and sweet, and the thudding of his anger slowed, slowed, as a clock slows when no one winds it. The pistol was very heavy. He wondered, with mild surprise, why he had brought it. Verrall had sat down on the arm of a chair and was staring at his own hands. Nettie was swaying.

"It's the comet," she said, almost laughing. "Willie, look at the light."

He looked. The walls had dissolved into radiance. The revolver slid out of his fingers and struck the boards, and the sound came from a long way off. He felt himself sinking, gently, not falling but being lowered, like a child into a bed. The last thing he knew was the green glow on Nettie's face and the thought, absurd and clear, that nobody in the world need ever be so angry again.

Then the dark closed over him, warm and kind, and the old world ended.
