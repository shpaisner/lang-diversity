I.

I was twenty-two that autumn, and I lived in a world no wider than the smoke of the Potteries. I was a clerk in a works at Burslem, at eighteen shillings a week, and I lodged in a back room in Hanbridge Row where the window gave upon a wall, a privy, and a sliver of sky. That sky had a new thing in it. Night after night, above the bottle-kilns and the furnaces, a smear of pale light had been growing, and men who had never looked up in their lives would stop at the corner and point.

I did not care for it. I had Nettie to think about. Nettie Stuart, with her grey eyes and her quick, scornful mouth, had promised, or I had thought she promised, to wait until I was in a position to marry. I was to be a great man, as I saw it. I read cheap books about science and justice, and I hated the people above me with a hot and futile hatred that I mistook for a philosophy.

Then, on a wet Thursday, her letter came. It was short, and it was kind, and that was the worst of it. She had gone away with young Verrall, whose father's great house stood among the beeches beyond Trentham, and whose motor-car had been seen too often on the lanes. She said I should forget her. She said I was better than I knew.

I read it standing under the gas-jet, and the whole low ceiling seemed to lean upon me.

II.

I will not tell you what I did that night, for it was nothing worth telling: I walked, and I cursed, and I sat on a slag-heap with my hands in my hair. But in the morning something had set in me, hard and cold, like glaze in a firing.

I went to the Stuarts' cottage. Her mother stood weeping in the doorway, and her father, who had been Verrall's gardener for thirty years, would not look at me. "They've gone to London," he muttered, "and then abroad, they say. Abroad!" He said it as though it were another planet.

On the way back I bought a newspaper, and for the first time in my life I read it with attention. The comet filled a column; the Balkans, the Rhine frontier, and the quarrel over the Channel ports filled five. There was talk of ultimatums and reservists. There was a speech by a lord, full of large, soft words about honour. I remember thinking that the whole of Europe was a great overheated machine, with no one at the valve, and that it was only the size of the explosion that was in doubt.

I felt, I confess, a sort of kinship with it. Let it burst, I thought. Let everything burst. I had nothing to lose but eighteen shillings and a back room, and I was beginning to find that I could spare both.

III.

I had a friend named Parload, a thin, freckled, stooping fellow who did odd work at a little observatory on the edge of Bursley, trimming lamps and copying figures for a professor who seldom came. He was a dreamer of the gentlest sort, and since the comet he had been almost unbearable with excitement.

I went to him on the Saturday. I think I wanted somebody to hear that I was damned. He heard me out, blinking, and then he said, "Come and look, Willie. You'll feel better."

"I'll not feel better."

"Come and look."

So I climbed with him into a cold dome that smelt of oil and brass, and he swung the long tube round and bade me put my eye to it. I saw a pale, blurred head with a tail flung out behind it, enormous even in that narrow circle, and in the heart of it a thin, strange glitter.

"That's the spectrum," Parload said, and showed me a smeared strip of paper, crossed with bright lines. "There's a new line in it, Willie. Green. Nobody has seen it before. It's something we haven't a name for, and in a few weeks the earth will be passing through the tail."

"And what will happen?"

"Nobody knows." His voice dropped. "Perhaps nothing. Perhaps it is only a rarefied vapour. But I tell you, I sometimes think—" He broke off, looking at me with a kind of shy awe. "I think it will be the end or the beginning of something."

I thought him a fool. I thought of Nettie, and of a revolver I had seen in a pawnbroker's window in Longton for seven and sixpence.

IV.

I bought it on the Monday. The pawnbroker, a gnome with a grey face, wrapped it in brown paper with ten cartridges and did not ask a question, and that seemed to me a fitting comment on the world.

War came upon us that week like a slow, great stroke of a bell. Reservists went through Stoke in their civilian clothes, with bundles and bottles and a ragged, shouting gaiety. Placards were pasted over placards. A crowd roared in the Market Square for a man who spoke of the Fatherland and the Flag, and my own throat roared with it, not because I cared a rap for either, but because I wished to hear something louder than my own thoughts.

Meanwhile the comet swelled night by night, until it hung above the chimney-stacks like a pale, immense feather, and the very smoke below it took on a queer, sickly sheen. Dogs howled. Women stood in doorways with aprons over their heads. Old men said the world would end on Thursday, and some of them were drunk by Wednesday.

I lost my work; the firm, in a panic, reduced its clerks. I counted my small money, folded the remaining notes, and took the train to London, to the address Nettie's father had grudgingly given me. In my pocket the revolver lay, a small, heavy, comfortable weight, like a decision already made.

V.

I did not find them in London. I found, instead, a landlady with a face like a shut door, who told me they had gone down to Verrall's country house, to wait out the troubles, and so I turned back into the vast anxious roar of the evening and went north again.

It was a night I shall remember until I die. The trains were thronged with soldiers, and the stations were lit with unnatural lamps, and over everything, through every window, stared the comet, now a great seething arch from horizon to zenith. Somewhere in the Channel, a man at the next compartment told us, the first shots had been exchanged. Nobody cheered. We sat in the swaying carriage, a dozen strangers, and listened to the wheels and the whispering of our own fear.

I left the train at a dark little junction and walked the last miles across the fields, with the revolver in my hand and the light of the sky on my knuckles. The air had altered. It was heavy and it was still, and a faint green glimmer lay on the grass, on the hedges, on the roofs of far-off farms. The birds had fallen silent. I could hear my blood.

Ahead of me, among the black beeches, the windows of Verrall's house burned yellow. I remember how my heart beat with a joyless, bitter exultation. I remember that I was quite sure of what I would do.

VI.

I was thirty yards from the terrace when the sky changed. It did not blaze, as the preachers had foretold, nor crash. It simply turned green, a deep, luminous, living green, as though the whole vault of heaven were a vast pane of glass held to the sea. The beeches stood out against it in sharp and delicate filigree, and a strange sweetness filled my nostrils, a taste like cold water after fever.

I tried to lift the revolver. My arm was heavy. I thought, absurdly, that it was the sort of sleep that comes upon a man in a warm room, and I fought it, and the fight seemed to me the last honest thing I would ever do. I saw a figure come out on the terrace, a white, wavering shape, and then my knees folded, and the cold grass rose to meet my face, and I knew no more.

I do not know how long I lay there. It might have been hours or an age. But I woke, and the first thing I knew was that I was lying under an immense and tender dawn, and that I was breathing as I had never breathed before, deep and clear to the bottom of my lungs, as though some foul, old burden had been lifted out of the air itself.

The revolver lay a yard from my hand. I looked at it for a long while, and I did not understand how I could ever have wanted it. My rage was gone, not dulled but gone, as a fog is gone, and in its place was a quiet, wondering clearness, in which I saw Nettie, and Verrall, and the poor distorted shape of my own mind, with a pity that was almost like laughter.

I rose, and the world stood round me, washed and astonishing, and the whole sky was silver and rose.
