The smoke of the Five Towns had hung over my childhood like a lid, and on the evening Nettie left me it hung there still, flattened by a low sky the color of old pewter. I stood in the doorway of the Stewarts' cottage with her note crushed in my hand. I cannot go on as we were, Willie. I am going away with Mr. Verrall. Do not follow me. You would only make it worse.

I was twenty-two and earned eighteen shillings a week at the potbank. I had a head full of grievances I could not name and a girl I could not keep. Verrall had a motorcar, a father with an estate, and a way of leaning on a gatepost as though the world had been built for him to lean on. I had only the knowledge that I loved her, and that knowledge, I found, was worth nothing in any market.

Her mother sat inside, rocking and sobbing into her apron. I did not go in. I walked out onto the cinder road, where the kilns were breathing their red breath against the dark. High above the chimneys, in the south-west, a faint smudge lay among the stars. I had seen it for several nights without thinking about it, as one does not think about a stain on a ceiling.

I was to think about it a good deal more before long.

It was Parload who first made me look at it properly. He was a thin, stooped clerk with a cough and a second-hand telescope, and he lived in the attic of a house in Trafalgar Road, where he kept his instruments and his indignation.

"There," he said, and pushed me toward the eyepiece. "Tell me what you see."

I saw a pale disc with a feathery tail, shining coldly in a field of black. "A comet."

"A new one. Come closer every night." His voice trembled with a joy I found obscene. "I've had the spectroscope on it, Leadford. Cyanogen. Carbon. And a line, a green line, that nobody has ever classed. Something is coming out of space, something strange, and we are going straight through its tail."

"What does it matter?"

"What does it matter?" He stared at me, as at a man who has asked what the sea is for. "It may matter more than anything has since the world began."

I laughed, and I think the laugh frightened him. I had spent the afternoon walking past the Verralls' lodge gates, and I wanted to hear of nothing that was not Nettie, or the man who had taken her, or the pain that sat under my ribs like a swallowed coal.

The newspapers, in those weeks, were full of two things, and both seemed to me equally remote. One was the comet, which the popular prints were beginning to call a portent. The other was the war.

No one yet said the word plainly, but it was in every column. There were notes between the Chancelleries, mobilizations on frontiers, a naval programme answered by a larger one. Men in the tap-rooms spoke of Germany as they might speak of a neighbor's dog that had begun to snarl. The whole continent seemed to me a great dim machine of iron and sullen men, wound tighter every day, its springs beginning to hum. And I, who had been told all my life that the world was ordered and that a man who worked would be rewarded, saw that it was ordered only toward ruin.

I lost my place at the works that month, for fighting a foreman who had made a joke about Nettie. I took a few shillings and my tools and went home to a room that smelled of damp plaster. There I wrote her four letters and burned them all. Under my mattress lay a revolver I had bought from a pawnbroker, with money I told myself was for something else.

I did not decide to use it. I merely held it often, and grew used to its weight.

At the end of October the comet was a great sword across half the heavens, and by night the fields were lit as by a dim and ghostly dawn. People stood in the streets with their faces turned up, whispering. A preacher at the corner of Hope Street promised the end of all things on Thursday. Parload came to my lodging, wild-eyed, and told me that the tail was now touching the upper air.

"You must come and watch," he said. "Something will happen. I don't know what, but it's the one great thing we shall ever see."

"I am going to Lord Verrall's," I said.

He looked at my coat pocket, then at my face, and his own face changed. "Leadford," he said. "Don't."

"Verrall brought her to his father's house. I mean to speak to them."

"Speak, then. Not with that."

I pushed past him. Even then, I think, some small cold clerk in my brain was observing me, noting that I walked like a man in a play. I do not excuse myself. I was bitter all through, and the bitterness had made me stupid and very certain.

I went out onto the road. The air was strangely still, with a taste on it like metal. Above me the comet burned, and the stars about it seemed to shrink away.

It was past midnight when I came to the park wall of Verrall's house, and over it I saw the lighted windows. I climbed the wall and crossed a wet lawn, and my boots were soaked. A summer-house stood by the garden path, and from it came a murmur of voices, one deep and easy, one that I knew as I knew my own pulse.

I took out the revolver and stood in the dark, shaking. I had meant to say something grand, I believe. I had rehearsed it on the road. I could not now recall a word.

Then I saw the sky go green.

It did not happen as lightning happens. It spread, like a drop of dye in water, from the zenith downward, a clear, luminous, unearthly green that turned the lawn to emerald and the trees to cut glass. The air grew sweet. A faint sound came, a hiss as of a far-off wind in tall grass, and I felt something enter my lungs, light and cool and strange.

I heard Nettie cry out, and Verrall's voice rise in sudden alarm. I raised the pistol. My arm would not hold it steady. My thoughts, my whole poor tangle of hatreds, seemed to come loose from their moorings and drift apart.

"Parload," I whispered, absurdly, "you were right."

My knees gave. The green light rose over me like deep water, and the last thing I knew was the wet grass against my cheek and the sound of the world drawing one long breath.

I woke, and the first thing I knew was that I was happy.

It was not the happiness of a man who has had good news. It was quieter and more terrible than that, as if a fever I had carried all my life had broken in the night and left me clean. I lay on the lawn with the dew on my face. The sky above was a pale and flawless blue, and the trees stood in a morning light so tender that I could have wept.

The revolver lay in the grass a yard from my hand. I looked at it for a long while. It seemed to me the most pitiful object I had ever seen, a small black clot of stupidity, and I could not think how I had come to carry it.

Voices came from the summer-house. I rose, stiffly, and went toward them. Verrall was standing at the door, bareheaded, his fine clothes rumpled. He looked at me, and I saw that he knew me, and knew what I had come for, and was not afraid. Behind him sat Nettie, her hair loose, her eyes wide and clear as a child's.

For a moment none of us spoke. I thought of all the things I had been going to say. They were gone, not forgotten, but simply emptied of their poison, like old bottles rinsed out in a stream.

"Leadford," said Verrall softly. "I'm sorry. I see now how I wronged you."

"And I you," I said, and was astonished to find it true. "I came here to kill you."

"Yes."

"I would have been wrong."

Nettie rose and came to me and put her hands in mine. She was crying, but not with grief. Beyond the park wall, the chimneys of the Potteries stood quiet, and above them a thin green veil still lingered, fading into a sky that seemed, for the first time in all the history of men, to have nothing left to threaten us. Far off, over the fields, I heard the first birds sing, and I understood that something had ended and something had begun, and that none of us would ever again be quite the creatures we had been.
