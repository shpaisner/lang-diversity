The kilns of Clayton had been burning since before Leadford was born, and the smoke they made lay along the valley like a low, sullen ceiling. Under it he came home that Thursday evening to find Nettie's father standing at his door, hat in hand, looking at him as a man looks at a dog he has to tell is going to be drowned.

"She's gone, William," said old Willersley. "Went by the afternoon train. There was a note on the mantel."

Leadford took the paper and read it twice. It was written in her round schoolroom hand, and it said that she was sorry, that she could not bear to be poor any longer, and that Mr. Verrall had promised to make her happy. Verrall. He saw the man as he had seen him last, on the Newcastle road, in a motor-car that cost more than all the houses in Leadford's street, laughing with his head thrown back.

He went upstairs without a word. From his window the sky was clearing in the east, and there, above the black teeth of the bottle ovens, hung the comet. It had grown since the previous week. It was no longer a smudge but a pale and definite thing, with a tail like a feather of cold light. He stared at it with a hatred he could not have explained. It seemed to him then that the heavens were as indifferent to his misery as the Verralls were.

At the pot-bank next morning the men spoke of nothing else.

"Cyanogen," said Parload, the draughtsman, who read the papers and had once looked through a telescope. "The spectroscope shows it. Poison gas, the tail of it. And we're to go right through."

"Then the Kaiser'll not need his guns," said a dipper, and there was laughter that did not go far.

For the other news was worse. The papers lay on the bench, greasy with clay, and every headline spoke of notes, of ultimatums, of reservists called up in Germany and mobilisation along the Rhine. Europe, which had never in Leadford's life seemed a real place, had become a great dark engine somewhere beyond the sea, and its wheels were turning toward them all. Nobody could say who had started it. The men talked of the comet and the war as though they were two weathers of the same season, equally beyond bargaining.

Leadford said nothing. He turned his saggar-clay with hands that did the work without him. He thought of Nettie in a room with silk on the walls. He thought, with a slow certainty that frightened him, that a world about to end in fire or in poison had no right to leave Verrall untouched, laughing, in a motor-car.

On Saturday he drew his wages and walked to the pawnbroker's in Hanbridge, where the window held concertinas, wedding rings, and one heavy revolver with a cracked grip. The man behind the counter did not ask questions. Times were strange, and half the town was buying whatever it could lay hands on.

"Cartridges are dear," the man said. "Everything's dear this week."

Leadford paid. The weight of the weapon in his coat dragged one shoulder down, and he walked home beneath a sky that would not darken properly. The comet had put a green stain on the dusk. The street lamps looked feeble, and the faces of the people on the pavement had the pallor of things seen under water.

He knew, from a letter Nettie's father had hidden and he had found, that Verrall's people kept a house on the ridge above Knype, empty in the summer, and that the lovers had gone there. He packed nothing. He sat on the edge of his bed with the revolver on his knee until the church clock struck two, and he told himself that it was justice, a plain and Staffordshire word, and that no man could be blamed for a thing so simple.

He walked the last ten miles, because the trains had been commandeered for troops.

All the way the country seemed to hold its breath. Soldiers in new khaki lounged at stations, bewildered and cheerful. Placards at crossroads announced that the ultimatum had expired at midnight. Once a motor-lorry went by at a furious pace, loaded with crates and shouting men, and he stood aside in the hedge and watched it vanish in its own dust. Overhead the comet burned in full daylight, a ghostly bar across the blue, and the light that fell from the sky had an odd quality, as if the sun were shining through a glass of thin green wine.

By evening he was in the larch wood above the Verrall house. It stood below him, white and prosperous, with a lamp already lit in an upper window. A figure crossed it, slim and bare-headed. He knew the shape of her shoulders at once.

He drew the revolver. His hand shook, and he steadied it against a tree trunk and waited, feeling the whole of his short life narrow to the length of that barrel. Somewhere to the south, very faint, there was a sound like furniture being moved in a distant room. He did not recognise it for guns.

Then the air changed.

It came first as a smell, faintly sweet, like crushed almond, and then as a pallor that crept over the sky from the zenith downward, until the blue was gone and the whole heaven was the colour of a bruised leaf. The larches turned a strange gold-green. The birds, which had been screaming at the dusk, fell silent all together. Leadford saw a bank of vapour pouring over the ridge, soft and unhurried, and he thought quite clearly: So it is the end, and I have not done it.

He lifted the revolver again, and found that his arm had grown tremendously heavy. The window below was blurring. He had time to cough once, to feel no pain, only a great loosening, as if every knot in him were being gently untied. The weapon slipped from his fingers. He sank down among the roots with the green light pouring over him and thought, absurdly, of his mother's kitchen, and of the smell of bread. Then there was nothing at all.

He woke to birdsong.

For a long while he lay without moving, and the first thing he knew was that he was not cold, though he had slept all night on the ground, and the second was that the sky above the larches was clean. It was a blue he had never seen, deep and rinsed, with no stain in it. The air tasted like water from a spring. He sat up slowly. His head was clear, clearer than it had ever been, and the knot of hatred that had sat behind his ribs for three days was simply gone, as a fever is gone after the breaking of the sweat.

The revolver lay among the pine needles. He picked it up and looked at it with astonishment, as he might have looked at a child's toy wrongly believed to be a dangerous thing. It seemed incredible, and not merely wrong, that he had carried it. He wondered how a man could ever have wanted to hurt another, and the wondering was not remorse but a kind of wide, bright surprise at his own former blindness.

Below him the door of the white house opened. Nettie came out into the sunlight, and behind her Verrall, dishevelled, his face naked of its old assurance. They stood a moment on the step, looking at the morning like people newly born. Then Verrall turned and said something to her, and she answered, and both of them looked up toward the wood as if they had known he was there.

Leadford stood. He laid the revolver gently in the hollow of a root and went down the hill with his hands open. Far to the south there was no sound of guns; there was only a lark, and over all the valley, where the smoke of the kilns had always hung, the thin clear light of a world that had been given a second beginning.
