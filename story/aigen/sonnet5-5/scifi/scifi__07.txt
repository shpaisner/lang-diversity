The night Nettie went away, the comet hung over the chimneys of Clayton like a pale green thumbprint on the glass of the sky. I remember it because I stood in the yard behind my mother's house and hated it, as I hated everything that was large and indifferent. Nettie's note was in my pocket, folded small. I cannot go on as we are, Willie. I am going where there is a little brightness. Do not follow me. I read it by the glow of the bottle ovens until the words came apart in my fingers, and the smoke of the Five Towns drifted across the stars as it had always done.

I was twenty-two, a clerk at Rawdon's pot-bank at eighteen shillings a week, with a head full of Socialism and a heart full of Nettie Stewart. Verrall had a motor-car and a father who owned half the hill country. He had a way of leaning on a gatepost, smiling at nothing, that made a poor man feel like a stain on the pavement. I had seen him lean on Stewart's gatepost often enough that spring.

The comet had been a rumour in the winter. By midsummer it was a presence. The newspapers carried diagrams: a thin ellipse, a dotted path, and the small blue disc of Earth waiting at the intersection like a mark on a target. The Astronomer Royal said there was nothing to fear. A professor in Berlin said the spectrum showed cyanogen, and a professor in Paris said it showed something else altogether, a bright unfamiliar line at the far green edge. Nobody agreed. Meanwhile the thing swelled nightly, a cold lamp of no known purpose.

I had no patience for it. Cosmic matters were for men with leisure. I went to the pot-bank, and on the third day Rawdon's foreman told me my services were no longer required, as I had been, he said, "a bit distracted." I walked home through streets where men stood in knots reading the evening papers. It was not the comet that held them. It was a column on the Morocco business, on German cruisers, on the mobilisation of reserves in a dozen countries. Europe, which had been quarrelling in its sleep for forty years, seemed to be stirring toward a waking temper.

"They'll have us all in uniform by Michaelmas," said a man at the corner.

"Better a uniform than a dole," said another, and laughed. It was a hard laugh, the kind that precedes a fight.

I went to see Nettie's father that evening. Old Stewart sat in his kitchen with his boots off, a small gray man who had been crushed so gradually that he no longer noticed the weight. He would not look at me.

"She's at Lord Redcar's place," he said at last. "Verrall's got a cottage on the estate, past Checkley. There's talk of a wedding. There's always talk."

"Is there to be one?"

He shrugged, and in that shrug I read the whole bitter arithmetic of class: a clerk's daughter, a rich man's whim, and no register to sign.

I left him there. On the way home I stopped at a pawnbroker's in Hanley and bought, for two pounds ten, a revolver of foreign make, with six cartridges in a twist of brown paper. The pawnbroker did not ask my business. Those were days when no one asked.

I went by train, third class, on a carriage packed with reservists. They sang, and some of them wept, and the compartment smelled of beer and wet wool. At every station the green light followed us. By night it was bright enough to throw a faint shadow, the comet's tail now a luminous veil spread over a quarter of the heavens. The soldiers fell silent when they looked at it, and then began singing again, louder.

I walked the last nine miles. The country there was soft and dark, all hedgerows and sleeping farms, and the fields shone with a faint, sickly phosphorescence that seemed to rise out of the ground itself. Twice I heard guns, or thought I did, a dull concussion beyond the horizon. Later I learned that on that night a German squadron had fired upon a British cruiser in the Channel, and that the telegraph wires were singing with ultimatums. At the time I knew only that the world was breaking, and that I was glad of it. Let it all break, I thought. Let there be nothing left of Verrall's motor-car or Redcar's park walls or my own eighteen shillings.

The cottage stood in a hollow beyond a plantation of larches, white-walled, with a lamp in the window. I came down through the trees with the revolver in my fist and my shoes full of dew. Through the glass I saw them. Nettie sat by the fire with her hair loose, and Verrall was reading aloud from a book. She was not smiling. That was what undid me. She looked tired, and a little frightened, and very young. But in my fever I took her weariness for his doing, and the hatred rose in me like a tide through a sluice.

I do not know how long I stood there. The light in the sky grew stronger, until the lamp in the window looked yellow and mean beside it. A wind rose from nowhere, a warm wind, smelling of nothing I knew. The larches turned silver and then a delicate green. I lifted my face, and the whole heaven was one pale, flawless emerald, shot with slow ribbons of brighter fire, and I understood that the comet had arrived.

I kicked in the garden gate. I remember the splintering, and Verrall's face turning to the door, startled and white. I raised the revolver. My arm was steady, and I thought: this is the one clean thing I will ever do.

Then the air changed. It thickened and sweetened, as though the night had been poured through honey. My finger tightened, and it was as if I pulled against a river. Verrall had risen, but he did not run; he stood with his mouth open, looking not at me but at the window, where the green light poured in like water into a sinking ship. Nettie's hand went to her throat. Somewhere in the plantation a bird began to sing, and stopped, and I heard the soft thump of its body on the grass.

A great weight of peace came down upon my eyelids. I tried to hold on to my hatred as a drowning man holds a spar, but it was slippery, and my hands were too heavy. The revolver slid from my fingers. I saw the stone threshold rising slowly toward my face. I thought of the Five Towns, of the bottle ovens burning down, of the guns beyond the hills falling silent one by one. I thought, so this is the end of it, and was not sorry.

Then the green swallowed everything, and I slept.

I woke to a morning such as I had never seen. The sky was a deep, unclouded blue, and the air was like cold water on the tongue. I lay on the path with dew on my coat and a daisy against my cheek, and for a long moment I did not remember who I was or why I had come. When memory returned it came strangely, as a story told of somebody else: a poor young man in a rage, with a gun. I sat up and saw the revolver lying in the grass, small and foolish and ugly. I picked it up and could not think what it was for.

Verrall lay on the doorstep, his head on his arm, breathing gently like a boy. Beyond him, through the open door, Nettie slept in her chair with her face turned to the dawn. I found that I loved her, and that my love had changed, as the air had changed. It was no longer a fist closed upon a possession but an open hand. I wished her well. I wished Verrall well. I wished, with a startling and painless generosity, the whole stupid, suffering world well.

The comet was gone from the sky. In its place the sun was rising over Staffordshire, and every hedge and chimney stood washed in new gold. Far away a church bell began to ring. It was not a call to arms, and I knew, as certainly as I knew my name, that it never would be again.
