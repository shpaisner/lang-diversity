I.

I remember that the comet was first to me no more than a smudge in the corner of a newspaper, a thing for the clever people who wrote of such matters. The smoke of the Five Towns hung over Clayton, and the nights were seldom clear enough to show any star at all. I was a clerk, then, at Rawdon's pot-bank, earning a pittance, and I lodged with my mother in two rooms that smelt of damp and boiled cabbage. My whole world was Nettie.

I had known her since we were children together in the lanes beyond the furnaces. She had grey eyes and a way of lifting her chin when she was displeased, and I had given her all of myself without ever having the courage to say so. I thought there was time. I thought poverty was a thing that would one day be solved by patience and a few shillings saved. I was a fool, as the poor are often fools, because they dream in the language of those who rule them.

It was on a Thursday in October that Nettie's mother met me at the gate with a face like curdled milk, and told me the girl was gone.

"Gone where?" I asked.

"With him," she said. "With that Verrall. In a motor-car, they say, to London."

I stood there with my hat in my hand. Above the chimneys the sky was the colour of dishwater, and behind it, unseen, something vast was falling toward us out of the dark.

II.

Edward Verrall was the son of a man who owned land and mines and, it was whispered, a title waiting in the wings. I had seen him twice: a slender young man with careless hands and an easy, indolent laugh. He had come to Clayton to look over his father's property. He had looked over Nettie as well.

I did not sleep that night. I walked until my boots were grey with cinder-dust, and I composed in my head speeches that no one would ever hear. By dawn the speeches had gone, and what remained was a small cold knot of purpose. I would find them. I would ask her to her face whether she had chosen this. And if she had not chosen it, if she had been tricked—

I did not finish the thought. At the pawnbroker's in Hanley there was a revolver in the window, an old Webley with a cracked grip, and I had eleven pounds in the world, saved shilling by shilling for a future that had just been cancelled. I bought the revolver and a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker looked at me over his spectacles and said nothing, which I thought then was indifference, and now believe was pity.

On the train south I read a newspaper left on the seat. The comet filled one column; the other columns were full of Germany, of ultimatums and mobilisations, and of a Balkan quarrel that no sane man could have explained. It seemed to me that the whole world was an engine running faster than its governors, and that I was merely one more small and bitter part flung off from it.

III.

London received me like a dull blow. I had never seen so many people nor so little concern in their faces. I took a bed in a lodging-house near Euston, and from there I began my hunt, pursuing rumours of the Verralls through clubs and servants and the tolerant condescension of cab-drivers.

It was in those days that I first saw the comet plainly. It hung above the housetops in the evening, a thing like a pale thumbprint, with a tail that grew longer each night. People gathered in the squares to look at it, and some of them laughed, and some prayed, and a few sat on the kerbs with their hands over their faces.

I fell in with a man named Parload, an astronomer's assistant of a sort, hollow-cheeked and shabby, who took me up to a leaded roof in Highgate and let me look through his glass. The comet swam into the lens with a pale green core, and about it a dim, vaporous glow.

"Cyanogen," he said, "and other things. We're going to pass through the tail of it, Leadford, and nobody can tell what will happen."

"Will it matter?" I said.

He looked at me curiously. "Everything matters," he said, "or nothing does. But it won't matter to the Germans. They'll go on with their war."

He was right about the war. That week the newspapers printed their first black headlines, and men in the streets began to speak of it as one speaks of a death expected in the family. I felt myself caught in the same current. My private grief was too small for the times, and yet it was all I had.

IV.

I found them at last in a small hotel in the Surrey hills, where the pines grew thick and the air smelt of resin and rain. A groom in a livery coat told me for half a crown that Mr. Verrall had taken the end cottage, and that "the lady" walked in the garden at dusk.

I watched her from the edge of the wood, with the revolver heavy in my pocket. She was thinner than I remembered and she wore a dress I had never seen, a grey silk that cost more than my mother's year. She stood a long while looking up at the sky. And I understood, with a shock that struck me harder than any anger, that she was not happy. Neither was she unhappy. She was merely waiting, as all of us were waiting, for something to be done to her.

I did not go to her that night. I stood in the trees until the cold was in my bones, and in the pale air above the garden the comet burned larger than I had ever seen it, its tail fanned out across a third of the heavens. Dogs howled somewhere in the valley. A bell began to ring and was silenced.

At the hotel I read a telegram pinned in the hall. It said that the fleets were at sea.

I thought then of the old, plain, savage justice that had been in the world since the first man stole the first woman. I thought it was mine to enforce. I sat on my bed with the Webley across my knees and I cleaned it with a handkerchief until the oil shone.

V.

The morning came strange. There was no wind, and the birds were silent, and the sky had taken on a tint I could not name, something between amber and the faintest green. The air had a taste, sweetish and metallic, like a coin held on the tongue.

I walked up the lane toward the cottage, and I was calm. Calmness is the worst of all conditions in a man bent on violence, for it removes the last excuse of passion. I knew precisely what I was doing, and I did it.

Verrall stood on the little lawn in his shirt-sleeves, smoking and looking at the sky, as if the day had been arranged for his entertainment. He saw me and his expression passed from surprise to a lazy, almost kindly recognition.

"Ah," he said. "The young man from Clayton."

I drew the revolver. My hand was steady. Behind him the cottage door opened and Nettie came out and stopped upon the step, her hand at her throat.

"Willie," she said. "Don't."

"Did you choose this?" I said, and my voice sounded to me like a stranger's. "Tell me you chose it."

She opened her mouth to answer, and at that moment the light changed. A green radiance fell over the garden, over the pines and the roofs and the three of us, brightening as though some great lamp were being turned up beyond the sky. The smoke of the cottage chimney bent and shivered. Verrall's cigarette dropped from his fingers. I felt the air thicken in my lungs, and a curious lightness take hold of my limbs, and a sudden unbearable clarity, as though a veil that had hung before my eyes since birth were slowly parting.

The revolver slid from my hand. I saw Nettie sink onto the step, and Verrall reach out as if to steady the world, and far off, thin and fading, the sound of guns. Then the green closed over everything, and the last thing I knew was a vast and gentle stillness, and a thought, strange and unbidden, that I had never in my life been so near to waking.
