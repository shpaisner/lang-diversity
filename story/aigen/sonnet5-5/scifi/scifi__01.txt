I was twenty-three that autumn, and the whole world, so far as I knew it, was a smear of smoke between two ridges of Staffordshire clay. I lived in a back room in Swadlincote Row with a cracked basin and a window that looked on the bottle-kilns. Every morning I went down to Rawdon's pot-works and carried saggars until my shoulders ached. Every evening I came back with the grit of the place in my teeth. I was poor, and I was angry, and I had no idea that this was anything but the natural state of a man.

I had one possession that made me different from the other lads: Nettie Stanton.

I had loved her since the Board School. She had grey eyes, a quick, discontented mouth and a way of looking past me, as if the real business of her life were going on somewhere beyond my shoulder. I told myself it was shyness. I walked with her on Sundays by the canal, and I talked of the day I should be foreman, and then manager, and then God knows what. She listened, and watched the barges.

Above the kilns, that October, a new star hung low in the evening sky. The papers called it the Comet, and said that an astronomer had measured it and found it growing. I looked at it as I looked at everything then, without interest. It was a pale blot in the smoke, and it had nothing to do with me.

Verrall came into our lives in November. He was the son of a man who owned half the country beyond Clayton, a slender, well-groomed, careless young man who spoke as though words cost him nothing. He motored through the Potteries on some errand of his father's, and he stopped at the Stantons' gate to ask the way.

I did not see it happen. I saw only what followed: Nettie with a new hat, Nettie unaccountably gay, Nettie walking beside the canal and no longer hearing a word I said.

"You don't understand, Willie," she told me once. "There's a bigger world than this."

"There's a bigger world for those that can afford it," I said, and she looked at me as one looks at a dog that has barked at the wrong moment.

On the last Thursday of the month I came home and found her mother weeping in the kitchen. A note lay on the table, in a hand I knew from the copybooks of our childhood. It was very short. She was going away with Mr. Verrall. She was sorry. She hoped I would not take it hard.

I read it twice. Then I went out into the street, and the street was full of a fine grey rain and the green glimmer of the Comet behind it.

I do not know how I got through the next two days. I remember the works, and the foreman's voice telling me I was no use to him, and the wages counted into my palm with the last of my place in the world. I remember sitting in the Potters' Arms among men who talked of nothing but the Continent.

"It'll come to war before the spring," said a bald man in a corduroy jacket. "Germany, France, Russia, the lot of them. They've been arming for twenty years. Somebody's got to use the guns."

"Let them," said another. "Maybe it'll clear some room."

I listened without surprise. All Europe seemed to me then a vast machine that nobody guided, grinding forward with its levers locked and its gauges rising, while the men at the controls looked at one another and talked about honour. It suited my mood. I wanted the machine to burst. I wanted a great noise and a great ruin, and Verrall somewhere beneath it.

On the Saturday I went to a pawnbroker in Hanley and bought a revolver, a small black ugly thing, with six cartridges and a greasy cloth to wrap it in. The man did not ask my business. He looked at my face and counted my money, and I think he understood perfectly well.

They had gone to London, so Mrs. Stanton had learned; and from London, perhaps, abroad. I did not know how I should find them. I knew only that I must move. I took the train as far as my money went, and walked the rest, southeast along roads grey with sleet and loud with the carts of a country that did not notice me.

The nights were strange that week. The Comet had grown to a vast hazy lantern, and a faint green tinge lay over the fields and hedges, as though the air had been tinted by some celestial dye. Men stood at crossroads and stared. At an inn where I begged a corner of the barn, a drover told me that the astronomers said we should pass through the tail on Wednesday.

"Pass through it?" said the innkeeper.

"Pass through it. Gas, they say. Might poison us all."

"Then it'll poison the lot of us together," the innkeeper said, with the comfort of a man who has found something fair at last.

I lay in the straw with the revolver against my ribs and watched the green light creep across the rafters. I told myself over and over what I would say to Verrall. I rehearsed it. I gave it dignity, which it did not deserve. Beneath it ran another voice, thin and mean, that said: You are nothing, and she has seen it. I had no answer to that voice, and so I clung to the weapon.

On Wednesday the sky was green from edge to edge.

I was on a hill above some nameless village in the Midlands, and I remember that the people had come out into the lanes and were standing very quietly. There was no wind. The light had no source. It lay upon the land like clear water, and every hedge and gable and upturned face took on a glass-like brightness. A woman near me was praying in a low monotone. A boy laughed, and the laugh stopped as though cut with a knife.

Then I saw the air itself change. I cannot give it better words. There was a shimmer, a shudder along the horizon, and a sense of vast and gentle pressure, as if the planet had put its head beneath some enormous hand. My heart beat in my throat. I fumbled for the revolver, not knowing why, and I think I meant to shoot the sky.

I breathed. The air tasted of nothing I had known, thin and sweet and cold, and a drowsiness went through me from scalp to heel. I saw the praying woman sink down upon the road like a folded cloth. I saw the boy lie among the stones. The hedges went away in a swimming haze of emerald.

My last thought was of Nettie, and it was a strange one. It was not hatred. It was a kind of bewildered tenderness, as though I had caught sight of her across some great distance and understood, for the first time, that she was afraid.

Then the green closed over me and I knew nothing.

I woke to a morning that I could not at first believe.

I lay on my back upon the hillside, and above me was a sky of such clear and tender blue that I wept at the sight of it, without understanding my own tears. The air was wine. The grass about me had a distinctness I had never seen in grass, each blade sharp and bright and separate, and the smoke of the Potteries, which I had thought was simply the colour of the world, was gone. Somewhere near, a bird was singing, and the song went through me like the opening of a door.

I sat up slowly. The revolver lay in the grass beside me. I took it into my hand and looked at it, and it seemed the most absurd object that had ever been made, a little clot of black iron with no meaning, a thing that a sick child might have fashioned in a nightmare. I could not remember the man I had meant to shoot with it. I could remember his name, and his face, but they had lost their power over me, like words in a language I had outgrown.

All about me the people of the village were rising from the road. They moved like men who had slept a long, long time and dreamed well. They looked at one another, and at the sky, and at their own hands, and no one spoke. Then the bald man from the Potters' Arms, who I do not know how had come so far, turned his face to me and smiled with a wonder so naked that I smiled in return.

"It's gone," he said. "Whatever it was. It's all gone out of us."

I knew what he meant. The fear was gone, and the grudge, and the long habit of suspicion. In their place stood a clear, wide quiet, and in that quiet I saw my whole life as one sees a landscape from a height: the kilns, the quarrels, the war that Europe had been rehearsing, and Nettie, poor frightened Nettie, running toward a bigger world because she could not breathe in ours.

I flung the revolver into the gorse as far as my arm would carry it. Then I stood up, a poor man with an empty pocket and no work, and for the first time in my life I was not afraid of the day.
