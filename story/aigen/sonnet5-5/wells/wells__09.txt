I remember the evening Nettie left as the last evening of my old life, and I remember it with a curious distinctness, as one remembers a room in which one has been ill. It was a wet, smoky Staffordshire night. I came home from the pot-bank with my wages in my pocket and my boots full of the clay of the yard, and found my mother's neighbour waiting at the door with a face of dreadful, hungry sympathy. Nettie's father had come asking for her; her bed had not been slept in. On the kitchen table, under the tea-caddy, lay a note she had written me, in her round clerkly hand, and I read it standing, with the rain dripping off my cap.

She said she was sorry. She said I was good and she was not good enough, and that Verrall had a way of speaking of the world as if it were a place one might walk about in. That was the phrase that stuck in me: walk about in. I had never walked about in anything but Burslem and Hanley, and the paths between them.

I sat down and stared at the wall. I was a lean, sallow, sullen young man, with large hands and small prospects, and I understood in that hour, with a clarity that was almost a pleasure, that the world had been arranged for people like Verrall and against people like me.

I did not sleep. Toward morning I went out and stood in the road, and the rain had ceased and the sky had cleared to a hard, cold grey. Low in the east, above the chimneys, hung a faint smear of light, like a thumbprint on a window-pane.

"That's the comet," said a milkman, going by. "Cost a sight o' money, they say, to look at it through a glass."

I hated the comet at once, as one hates everything on that sort of morning.

For some weeks I went on at the works. I turned and glazed and carried, and said nothing, and men looked at me and spoke low among themselves. The foreman, a decent man, told me I was "going to the dogs," and when I answered him sharply, I was paid off. It seemed to me quite natural. The universe was in a conspiracy, and it was only keeping its appointments.

I took to walking at night upon the hills above the town. The comet grew; each evening it was larger and more definitely green, a baleful, glaring, ill-conditioned eye with a tail behind it like a drift of smoke. The papers were full of it and fuller of Germany. A man in a public-house would put down his pot and say that the Kaiser was making ready, and another that the Russians would not be bought, and a third, with the owlish gravity of drink, that the comet was a sign. Nobody believed in anything, and everybody believed in this. The newspapers printed maps of Europe with arrows on them, and astronomers' letters about cyanogen, and no one troubled to read the two together.

Only one man I knew spoke of the comet with real knowledge. That was Parload, once a fellow-clerk of mine, who had given up wages for astronomy and a loft. He took me up one night to his observatory, a shed with a hole in the roof, and put my eye to a brass tube.

"Look," he said. "It is not like anything we have understood. It is something coming into the world, Leadford. A new element, I believe, in the tail."

I looked, and saw a ragged greenish glare that meant nothing to me.

"Will it hurt us?" I asked.

"I think," said Parload slowly, "that it will change us."

"It cannot change her," I said, and he looked at me with a kind of troubled pity, and said no more.

Through that winter I learned where they were. Verrall's people had a house in the south, near Lord Redcar's park, and Nettie had gone with him to a cottage on its edge, in the guise, I heard, of a convenient friend. I did not know how I learned it; such things come to a bitter man as water comes to a hollow place.

I bought a revolver in Hanley from a pawnbroker with a squint. It cost me seventeen shillings, and it seemed to me an astonishing thing, so small and so heavy, so neatly shaped for the end of a quarrel. I took it back to my lodging and sat with it upon my knee and thought, in great sentences, of justice. I did not think of Nettie much. I thought of Verrall's face, his easy, confident, finished face, and of how it would look when it had met the thing I carried.

I sold my watch and my mother's brooch and went south by third-class, with my coat buttoned over the weapon. All the way, at every station, the placards cried of ultimatums, and soldiers' wives wept on platforms, and in the carriage a fat man read aloud that the fleets were at sea. I watched the fields go by, grey and sodden and quite indifferent. I thought: it will all be over presently, for them and for me. I did not feel afraid. I felt only a tightening, like a screw being turned very slowly in the back of my head.

I reached the park at dusk, and the sky was an extraordinary colour. The comet had swollen until it covered a quarter of the heavens; the clouds had parted from it as from a presence, and the whole landscape lay in a livid, translucent green, as if the earth had been sunk to the bottom of a pool. The trees were green, my hands were green. Far off to the east there came a dull noise, a throbbing, that I took for thunder and afterward knew to be guns. The war had begun, and I did not care.

I went up the lane with the revolver in my fist. Then the air changed. I cannot tell it otherwise. There was a faint sweetness in my nostrils, like the smell of some great unknown flower, and a strange lightness of the chest, and I coughed, and coughed again, and the gun seemed to grow remote at the end of my arm. I saw a pale mist streaming over the hedges in long, slow, shining skeins, and I thought, quite quietly: It is the tail. It has come. Birds fell from the hedge as I watched, softly, like dropped gloves. I tried to lift the weapon; my knees folded; the lane tilted gently and laid me down in the wet grass, and the green went white, and the whole sky seemed to open like a hand.

I do not know how long I lay. It may have been a night and a day, or it may have been a thousand years; it was a stillness beyond the measure of clocks. Then, very gradually, I was aware of light upon my eyelids, a light unlike any I had known, clear and golden and clean, and of a sound of birds, and of my own breathing, deep and effortless.

I opened my eyes. The sky above me was blue, a pure, tender, washed blue, and there was no green in it anywhere. The grass about my face was starred with dew, and every blade had a bead on it that held a small bright sun. I lay and watched one drop tremble and fall, and I wondered why I had never before thought a dewdrop so wonderful a thing.

Then I sat up. The revolver lay in the grass beside me, black and absurd, like some dead beetle. I looked at it for a long while without anger, with only a puzzled wonder that it should ever have seemed to me important. The hatred that had been so solid in me, the screw, the grey hard certainty of the world's malice, was gone; I searched for it as a man searches for a tooth that has been drawn, and found only a tender place, and a great quiet.

Across the park men were moving in the morning light: soldiers, I saw, in the dust-coloured uniforms of the army, but they had laid down their rifles and were sitting in the grass, talking, and one was laughing. And the air was so sweet, so sweet, that it hurt me to breathe it. I stood up trembling, a new man in an old coat, and knew, with a wonder past all words, that the world I had known was ended and another had begun.
