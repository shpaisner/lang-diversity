I.

I was twenty-two that autumn, and I lived in a world that seemed to me as finished and as irrevocable as the brick walls of the pot-banks between which I walked to work. I do not think young men now will understand this. They will not know what it was to be shut in by a sky of smoke and a ground of cinder, with every road leading, after a mile or so of dingy terraces, back to some other part of the same blackened district. We called it the Five Towns, though a map would have shown them as one vast, sprawling, ill-considered smear upon Staffordshire. I was a clerk, and then something less than a clerk, for the firm that had employed me failed in October, and I became a man who watched for the postman and the advertisements, and who grew daily more ashamed of the face he saw in the shaving-glass.

I had, however, Nettie. That was the whole of my philosophy. I held to her as a drowning man to a spar, and I made out of her a sort of religion, which is a thing that poor men are apt to do with a woman, to their cost and hers. I thought that if I could but earn thirty shillings a week she would marry me, and that after that the world would arrange itself.

II.

I will not tell here all that Nettie was. She was slight and quick, with brown hair that never would lie flat, and eyes that looked at one very directly and then, quite suddenly, away. Her father kept a small garden-ground, and the family was, I suppose, a little above my own in the fine and foolish gradations we observed. She had read books. She had ideas of a larger life, and I, with my cheap enthusiasms and my tattered hope, had fed these ideas more than I knew.

It was Verrall who took advantage of them. I had seen him twice: a tall, easy young man in excellent clothes, with the leisurely certainty of one for whom doors have always opened. He came of the people who owned things. He talked to her in the lane behind her father's house, and gave her books I could not afford, and an image of the world I could not offer.

On a wet Thursday in November I called, and her mother met me at the door with a face of stone. Nettie had gone. A note on the mantelpiece spoke of "a chance of a bigger life," and was quite kind. Kindness was the intolerable thing. I walked out into the rain and found that I was holding my hat in my hand and had forgotten to put it on.

III.

It was in those days, when the world had gone grey and I wished to hear no more of anything, that the newspapers began to speak of the comet.

I recall that it was first a small paragraph, tucked among the racing and the police-court news: an astronomer at some observatory had found a faint, smudged star where no star should be, and it was moving. Then it was a larger paragraph, and then an article. A spectroscope had been brought to bear upon it, and it appeared that the thing was made largely of some gas, not of any known kind, and that its path would bring it very near to our earth.

Nobody cared. That is the truth I wish to put down, since it is so strange in the light of what followed. The men in the tap-room of the Marquis of Granby read the articles aloud and laughed. "End o' the world, Bill," they said to me, "ye'll not be needin' that thirty shillin' after all." I laughed with them, sourly. The comet was a thing for the leader-writers; it was no more to us than the Chinese Empire. We went on with our small spites and our smaller ambitions, as ants will, I suppose, go on about their hillock while the boot is lifted.

IV.

But there was another cloud above us, nearer than the comet and less innocent. The newspapers that told of the stranger in the sky told also, upon the next column, of the stranger on the frontier. There was trouble in the Balkans; a certain Power had sent a note and another Power had replied to it; fleets were being built, and the great nations stood about one another like angry men in a crowded bar, each with a hand upon his pocket.

I read these things with a hot and stupid satisfaction. I wanted something to be broken. I do not defend the feeling, but I record it, because I believe it was widespread. The whole of Europe, it seemed to me, was sick with the same fretful bitterness that was in me: a vast, unlovely envy, a resentment of the neighbour, a hunger for any change so long as it were violent. Men who could not tell you why they hated the Germans, or the French, or the Russians, hated them heartily on the strength of a headline. Our rulers, who sat in clubs and cabinets, talked of honour and of interests; our masters in the potbanks cut wages and talked of foreign competition; and we who were cut stood at the corners with our hands in our pockets and thought, in our dull way, that a war would at least be a thing to do.

V.

In December I had a letter from Nettie.

It was not long. She was, she said, quite happy; she hoped I should not think too hardly of her; she was with Mr. Verrall, and they were in London, and then they were going abroad. She wished me well. There was a postscript saying that I should find someone better suited to me.

I read it standing in the doorway of my lodgings, with the fog pressing against the street lamps, and something went out of me that I have never exactly recovered, and something else came into me in its place. I had thought myself broken. I discovered, instead, that I was extremely hard. I saw, as it were with a sudden hateful clarity, the whole shape of the world: Verrall, with his money and his ease, taking what he wanted from the shop-window of life; and I, who had worked and hoped, left with the rain on my face.

I went that night into a pawnbroker's in Hanbridge, and there, among watches and flutes and wedding-rings, I bought a revolver. The man asked no questions. I remember that the weapon was wrapped in a piece of the Staffordshire Sentinel, and that a paragraph about the comet showed through the paper, damp from the fog.

VI.

I do not know how long I walked that night. I know that I took the road out of the town, through the grey slag-heaps and the sullen little villages, with the revolver heavy in my pocket and my mind going over and over its single thought. I should go to London. I should find them. I had, I think, no clear picture of what I should then do; there was only the hot, pulsing image of Verrall's face, surprised.

The fog had gone by the time I reached the top of the long hill beyond Trentham, and the night, for once, was clear. I stopped to take breath, and looked up.

It was there. I had read of it for weeks, and it had meant nothing to me; and now it hung in the north-eastern sky, a great pale smear of light with a faint, ominous tinge in its heart, like a thumb-print upon the dark. It did not twinkle as the stars twinkled. It seemed to me to regard the earth. Below me lay the whole huddled district, its furnace-glows like sores upon the night, its chimneys standing in rows like the pipes of some dead organ; and beyond, the dim shapes of the hills; and all of it, I thought, so small, so mean, so quarrelsome beneath that quiet and enormous visitor.

I took the revolver out of my pocket and held it in my hand, and for a long minute I could not tell whether I was going to throw it away or walk on. Then I walked on.
