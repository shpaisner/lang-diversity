I.

I find it hard, now that so much has changed, to set down plainly how small and mean a life I led in those days, and how entirely I took it for the whole of life. I was William Leadford, a draughtsman in a pot-bank in the Five Towns, with eighteen shillings a week and a mind full of grievances. I lodged in a street of blackened brick, where the chimneys of the potteries and the iron-works stood against the sky like the pillars of some vast, dirty temple. The smoke hung over everything. The very sparrows were sooty, and the children I passed in the gutters wore the same dull complexion as the walls they played against.

I had one thing in the world that I thought was bright, and that was Nettie. She was slender and quick, with dark hair and a trick of looking sidelong at me that I took for tenderness. We had walked together on Sundays in the thin country beyond the town, where the hedges were grey with dust, and I had talked to her of socialism and of the injustice of things, and she had listened, or seemed to listen. I thought she was mine. I had never asked what she thought of the matter.

I mention the comet only because it comes into the tale so late. At first it was a thing for the astronomers and the evening papers, a faint smear in the telescopes. I took no more notice of it than of the Balkans.

II.

She went in the spring. There was no quarrel, and that was the worst of it. There was only a note left upon her father's mantelpiece, and a dreadful ordinary emptiness in the kitchen when I called on the Thursday evening, with her mother crying into her apron and her father sitting rigid and ashamed, looking at the fire.

"She's gone off with Verrall," he said at last. "With young Verrall of the Hall."

I had seen the man twice: a tall, easy, fair young fellow, in clothes that seemed made of some softer stuff than mine, who drove through the town in a motor-car and looked at us as people look at the animals in a show. I could see him as I stood there, and I could see her face turned up to his, with that sidelong look.

I do not remember leaving the house. I remember walking, and a hot, choking pressure in my head, and presently finding myself on the canal bridge staring at the slow black water. It would have been a very simple thing to climb over. But even then something obstinate in me refused it. It was not I who ought to be in the water.

"I will have it out with them," I said aloud, to the chimneys.

III.

I went back to my drawing-board like a man walking in his sleep. At the works they knew, for such things are known in the Potteries before they are done, and the men looked at me and then away. Only Parload, who worked beside me, said anything. He was a lean, stooping, eager fellow with an enthusiasm for the stars, and he had no gift for delicacy.

"She's not worth it, Leadford," he said. "Look up, man. Look at the comet. There's a real thing for you."

I told him, with some profanity, what I thought of his comet.

"But it's coming," he said. "It's going to hit us, or very near it. Nobody can say what will happen. And meanwhile—" He waved a paper at me. "Meanwhile they're going to fight. Germany, France, ourselves, the lot of us. They'll make a pretty mess of the world before the comet does."

I took the paper. It was full of ultimatums and mobilisations and the large, empty phrases of statesmen, and between the columns, in small type, was news of a strike at the collieries and of soldiers in the streets of a town I had never seen. The world seemed to me a vast, stupid machine grinding to some end that nobody wished for. I read it all with a bitter pleasure. Let the great clash come, I thought; let it shatter the lot of them, the Verralls and the Lord Redcastles, along with the clerks and the hands. I had nothing to lose. That was my creed in those days.

IV.

I lost my place within the month. I had been sullen and careless, and my master owed me no kindness. I drifted for a time. I walked the roads, did odd jobs for farmers, slept under hedges, and everywhere I went the comet grew. It was no longer a smear. It had become a pale and ominous eye in the evening sky, and presently a great trailing brush of light that lay across the stars. People stood in the lanes and stared at it, and some spoke of prophecy, and some of the end of the world. A man at a public-house told me, with perfect gravity, that it was a judgment on the Germans.

I heard news of Nettie once, from a groom at a stable-yard. They were living at a house near Clayton, he said, and she wore silk. I thanked him, and I remember how politely I did it.

In a pawnbroker's window in Hanley, a week later, I saw a revolver. It was a cheap, ugly, nickel-plated thing, with a chipped butt, and the price was seven and six. I stood for some minutes looking at it. Then I went in and bought it, with money I had meant for bread.

V.

I went on a night of that last autumn, when the comet had become so bright that the roads cast faint shadows and the fields lay under a cold, greenish twilight. Everything was strangely still. Even the dogs in the farmyards were silent, and the wind had fallen. I walked all through the dusk and the dark, with the revolver bumping against my hip, and as I walked I rehearsed the thing I would say. I would not say it to her. I would say it to him: a few cold, terrible words, and then the shot. I composed these words a hundred times. I have forgotten them all, and I am glad.

The sky, as I climbed the long hill above Clayton, was a spectacle that I think no man before our time had seen. The comet filled a third of the heavens. It was not white but a faint, strange green, with a ragged fringe, and the stars showed through it as through a veil. I stopped once and looked at it, and for a moment my own small fury seemed to me ridiculous, a gnat's anger beneath that enormous silence. Then the picture of Verrall's easy smile rose before me, and I went on, and my heart was as hard as it had been before.

VI.

The house stood in its own grounds, behind a belt of firs, and one light burned in a lower window. I came to it by the garden wall, climbed it clumsily, and dropped upon a lawn that was wet and cold under my hands. A great quiet lay over the place. The air had a curious taste in it, faint and sweetish, and the green light lay on the gravel and on the black windows like a thin wash of paint.

I crept along the wall of the house until I came to the lighted window. The curtains were not drawn. I raised myself and looked in.

It was a pleasant room, a room of the kind I had never entered: warm, with a fire, and a great many books and flowers, and a litter of silk and silver upon the table. And there they were. Verrall lounged in a deep chair with his legs stretched out, and Nettie sat upon a stool near him with her face lifted to his. He was speaking, and she was laughing, and I saw that she had grown more beautiful, and that she was happy. That was the thing that struck me like a blow. She was happy, and had not once thought of me.

My hand went into my pocket and came out with the revolver. It was heavy and cold. I steadied it against the sill, and the front sight wavered, and Verrall's white shirt-front swam upon the end of it. I remember that I breathed slowly. I remember that my finger tightened.

And then I saw that they had stopped laughing. They had both turned toward the window, but not toward me; they were looking past me, upward, with parted lips and a strange intentness. The firelight went suddenly pale. The green radiance outside brightened in a moment to a pulsing, living emerald that poured across the lawn and over my hands and over the barrel of the gun. A strange sweetness filled my throat. The sill seemed to slip away beneath my arms, and the last thing I knew was the weapon, sliding out of my fingers, and the great green dawn that broke over the world.
