I

I was twenty-two that year, and I believe I was as sour a young man as any in the Five Towns. I lived in a row of grimy cottages under the shadow of the bottle-kilns, where the smoke came down upon the washing and the clay got into the very bread, and I worked for nine shillings less than I needed, as a clerk in the counting-house of Rawdon and Pole, earthenware manufacturers. Every morning I went down the cobbled hill among the crowd of potters, and every evening I came up again. The world, as I understood it, was a great grey machine that existed to make other people comfortable.

I had one friend, Parload, who worked in the same place, and who had, in an attic above a chandler's shop, a small and shabby telescope. It was Parload who first showed me the comet. It was no more than a faint smudge among the stars, a thumbprint of pale light on the black glass of the night.

"It is coming this way," he said, with his eye still to the instrument. "Every night it is a little brighter."

"Let it come," I said. "It could hardly find us worse."

He laughed, but I do not think he understood me. I was not thinking of the sky at all. I was thinking of Nettie Stuart, and of the nine shillings, and of the plain dreadful fact that the one was not to be had without a great deal more of the other.

II

I had known Nettie since we were children together in the lane behind Pytner's Yard, and I cannot remember a time when I did not mean to marry her. She was a slight, quick girl, with dark hair and grey eyes, and a way of lifting her chin when she was moved; and she had a restlessness in her that I mistook, in my stupidity, for a kind of spirit that would march beside mine.

We walked out on Sundays, along the canal and up over the slag-heaps to where the country began, thin and torn and sooty, but still faintly green. There I talked to her of what I would do. I talked of the Co-operative, of books, of a little house with a bit of garden. I talked, God help me, of Socialism, with all the dogmatic confidence of the half-educated. She listened with her eyes upon the distance.

"It is all so far off, Willie," she said once.

"It is only a matter of time," I said.

"But the time is our lives," she answered, and said no more.

I was too much occupied with my own grievance to hear the thing she was telling me. I did not see that she was hungry, as I was hungry, but not for ideas; she was hungry for colour, for ease, for the bright and gracious things that never came up our hill. I noticed only that her letters grew shorter, and that she had begun to speak, with a careless air, of the Verralls up at the Clayton end of the town.

III

The Verralls had the big house, and the son, Edward Verrall, had a motor-car. In those days a motor-car was still a thing that people turned to look at, and I remember well the afternoon I first saw Nettie sitting in it, her hat tied down with a veil, her face turned toward him and alight. I stood on the pavement with my hands in my pockets. She did not see me.

I told myself that it was nothing. I told myself so for three weeks.

Then, on a wet Thursday in October, I called at her father's house and found him sitting alone by the fire with a letter open on his knee. He was an old, dull, decent man, and he looked at me as if I had come to bring him news of a death.

"She has gone," he said. "She has gone with that young man. To London, she says, and then abroad."

I read the letter. It was a short letter, and the writing was her own, round and hasty. She said that she was sorry. She said that I should understand some day. She said that I was good, and that she could not bear to be merely good for ever, in a little house, with the smoke.

I put it back upon his knee. I remember that I said something civil. Then I went out into the rain and walked, for hours, up on the slag-heaps among the cinders, until the lamps of the Five Towns lay below me like the embers of a fire gone sour.

IV

It is a curious thing, but in the weeks that followed, the whole world seemed to come into tune with my misery. The newspapers, which I had never greatly regarded, filled up with the talk of war. There was some quarrel over a frontier, and another over a harbour; there were ultimatums and denials, and great battleships steaming about in grey northern waters. Men in the pottery yards began to speak of Germany as they spoke of a neighbour with whom they had a feud. The bands played more often, and the leader-writers grew shrill and self-righteous. Europe, it seemed to me, was a vast, overcrowded, ill-tempered house, in which everyone had armed himself and no one could find the door.

And above it all, night by night, grew the comet. It was visible now to the naked eye, a tilted feather of whiteness above the western chimneys, and the papers gave it columns. It was to be the end of the world, said some; it was to be a mere display, said others. The astronomers disputed about a strange green band that the spectroscope had found in its tail.

"It is a gas," said Parload, excited. "Something new, that has never been in any laboratory."

"I hope it chokes the lot of us," I said.

I meant it. I had begun to nurse a thing in my mind that was not grief at all, but something harder and hotter, and it had a name, and the name was Verrall.

V

I do not wish to excuse myself. I was, in those days, a man in whom every generous impulse had been turned sour by poverty and humiliation, and I made of my wrong a kind of religion. I took my savings, such as they were, and I bought, from a pawnbroker in Hanley who asked no questions, a heavy old revolver and a box of cartridges. I carried it home wrapped in a copy of the Staffordshire Sentinel, and I sat with it that night on my narrow bed, turning it over under the gas.

I knew now where they were. Her father had had a second letter, a stiff and guilty one, from a house the Verralls rented near Dorking, and from that day I could think of nothing but the road to it. I gave notice at Rawdon and Pole's. The old clerk, who disliked me, accepted it without a word, and I think he was glad.

On the last evening Parload came to my lodging, with his hat in his hand, and looked at me for a long while.

"You are going after her," he said.

"I am going to London."

"Leadford, there is something in the air. I cannot say what. The comet is very near now, and the instruments are doing strange things. Do not do anything violent just yet. Wait a few days."

"I have waited all my life," I said, and I shut the door upon him.

VI

I came into the south that night by the last train, through a country that seemed already half asleep and half afraid. At every station there were knots of people standing on the platforms with their faces turned up. And when I got out upon the lonely road beyond Dorking, with the revolver heavy against my ribs, I looked up and I saw it fully for the first time.

It filled a quarter of the heavens. It was no longer a feather, but a vast, curved, silent flame, white at the heart and pale about its edges, and across it there lay a faint, strange tinge of green, like the first light on the sea before dawn. Under it the hedges, the road, and my own hands were drained of colour. A great stillness had come over everything. No dog barked, no wheel turned; even the wind had died.

I walked on. I remember that I was quite calm. I rehearsed what I should say at the gate, and what I should do when he came out, and what Nettie's face would be like. I remember that I noticed, with a faint surprise, that I was no longer very angry, but only tired, and that the tiredness was as vast as the sky.

Then, at a turn of the road, I saw the house, with a single window lit among the dark trees. And as I stood looking at it, there came a thin, sweet, sharp smell upon the air, and the green in the heavens deepened, and began, very slowly, to descend.
