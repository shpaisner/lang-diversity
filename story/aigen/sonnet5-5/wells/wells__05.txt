I.

I was twenty-two that autumn, and I was a clerk at Rumbold's pot-bank in Clayton, which is to say that I was nothing at all. I lived with my mother in a little house in Swan Row, where the smoke from the bottle-kilns came down so thick on wet days that the very bread tasted of it. I had read a little and thought a great deal, and the result was a sullen and tormenting discontent. The world, I felt, was a vast machine for crushing such as I, and I hated it with an energy that I now see was largely wasted.

And I loved Nettie Stuart. I loved her as a man loves who has nothing else to love, with a hungry, jealous, ill-managed passion. She was the daughter of a gardener at the big house on the hill, and she had a trick of looking at one sidelong, with her lips pressed together, that I would have walked to London to see. I had thought she would be mine. I had thought it, God help me, because I wanted it so much.

Then Verrall came home from Cambridge, tall and easy and well-clothed, and in six weeks she was gone. She left a note for her father. She did not leave one for me.

I remember that I went out that night into the yard behind our house and stood looking up. There, low in the east above the kilns, hung the new comet, a small, bright, greenish smear. I hardly noticed it then. I was thinking only of Verrall's neck and how it would feel under my hands.

II.

In those weeks the newspapers were full of two things, the comet and the Germans, and I cared nothing for either.

Yet one could not escape them. The men at the works talked of war across the benches. The Balkans were in a flame, the Powers were mobilising, and the great nations stood about Europe with their hands upon their weapons, like drunken men at a street corner, each waiting for the other to strike. Meanwhile, on every clear night, the comet grew.

My one friend was a man named Parload, a lean, absent-minded fellow who worked in the same office and had a telescope on the roof of his father's cottage. On Sunday he took me up the hill beyond Bursley and showed me the thing. It was no longer a smear but a great luminous head with a faint feathery tail, and through the glass it showed a strange green tint at its heart.

"They've found something new in it," said Parload, his eye at the instrument. "Some gas the spectroscope can't name. Nitrogen of a sort. It's coming nearer, Leadford."

"Will it hit us?"

"Oh, no. But we shall go through the tail of it."

"And what will happen?"

"Nobody knows." He laughed excitedly. "Nobody has the faintest idea."

I looked out across the black, smoking valley, with its lights and its furnaces, and its thousand small cruelties. I thought that nothing which could happen to such a world would be anything but an improvement. Then I remembered Nettie, and my hatred came back like a fit of sickness.

III.

I learnt where they had gone from Nettie's sister, who told me for a shilling and a lie. They were at a cottage near the river, somewhere beyond Wolverhampton, which Verrall's family used in the shooting season. I borrowed a revolver from a man at the Crown who asked no questions. I had never fired one in my life.

I will not pretend that I was in any proper sense a sane man during those days. I walked and begged lifts on carts. I slept in a barn. The air had taken on an odd, strained quality, a hush that lay on the fields like a hand. The sunsets were a livid, copperish colour, and at night the comet stood in the sky so large and brilliant that it cast a faint shadow from the hedges. The cattle stood huddled and silent. Once a dog came out of a farmyard and howled at me for a quarter of an hour, and then crept away.

On the road I met soldiers. A train of horse-boxes stood in a siding with the horses stamping inside, and at the next station they were chalking the news on a board: Ultimatum expired. The porters spoke in low voices. Men stood about in knots with their papers, not reading them.

It all seemed so remote. I saw it as through a window. The only real things in the world were the weight of the revolver against my ribs and the picture in my mind of a lit window, and two figures behind it, laughing.

IV.

I came to the cottage on the fourth night, at an hour when the whole sky was a pale, ghastly green.

It was a low place, white among dark trees, with a lamp in the window as I had pictured it. The garden gate was open. I remember crossing the lawn with my feet making no sound, and the green light lying upon the grass so that every blade stood out clear and separate. I could hear, far away to the south, a dull, continuous muttering. I did not know then that it was guns.

Then I noticed the birds. They had been silent for days; now they began to fall. A thrush dropped from the thorn tree and lay upon the path, quite unhurt, with its eyes closed. A sweetish, tingling vapour was creeping up the garden from the river, a thin mist with a faint emerald glow in it. When I breathed it my head swam, and a curious lightness, almost a gaiety, came upon me, which I fought as one fights a drug.

"Not yet," I said aloud. "Not yet."

I went to the window. They were there. Verrall stood by the table in his shirt-sleeves, and Nettie sat looking up at him, her hand on the cloth, her face turned towards him with that sidelong look I knew. She was lovely, and she was not mine, and I saw it all, with a bitter, final clearness, in the moment before I kicked open the door.

V.

I do not know what I said. I know that Nettie sprang up with a cry, and that Verrall, very white, stepped in front of her, and that for an instant it all hung like a picture, with the lamp burning green in the gas and the revolver steady in my hand.

"You swine," I said. "You pitiful, rich swine."

"Leadford," he said, "don't be a fool."

I fired. The room leapt with the noise. I saw a splinter fly from the mantel; I had missed him by a yard. I fired again, and this time there was no crash but only a small, flat click, and I felt a queer, swimming weakness come over me. The vapour was in the room. It lay in the lamplight like a green breath. Verrall swayed, put out a hand to the wall, and slid slowly down, with a puzzled look, as a child goes to sleep in its chair. Nettie sank beside him with her arm across his breast.

"Get up," I said thickly. "Get up and fight."

My own knees were bending. The revolver was too heavy to hold; it dropped from my fingers and I watched it fall with an extraordinary slowness. The comet's light poured through the window. I thought, with a last absurd flicker of rage, that even now they had cheated me, that they had slipped away into peace and left me with my hatred. Then the floor tilted, and the green light closed over me like water, and I knew nothing more.

VI.

I woke to the sound of a bird singing.

For a long time I lay still, with my cheek upon the boards, and I could not remember who I was. I felt rested as I had never been rested in all my life. My body seemed to have been washed. The air in the room was cool and sweet, and a clear, golden light lay along the floor. I raised myself on one arm and saw the revolver lying a yard away, small and ugly and strangely foolish, like a toy a child has broken and forgotten.

Then I remembered, and waited for the black wave of anger to rise in me. It did not come. I searched for it, as one feels in a pocket for something lost, and found only a vast, quiet wonder. My hatred had gone. My thoughts, which had been tangled and tormented for years, had become as clear as water in a glass. I knew, quite simply and without argument, that I had been mad, that the whole world had been mad, and that the madness had somehow been lifted in the night.

Verrall was stirring. He sat up, rubbed his eyes, and looked at me, and there was no fear in his face, nor any anger. We stared at each other with a sort of grave astonishment, like men who have been dreaming the same evil dream. Beside him Nettie opened her eyes.

I got up and went to the window. The sky was a deep, tender blue, and the green was gone from it. Over the fields the dew was shining. Far away a few soldiers were rising from the roadside, unsteadily, looking at their rifles as if they could not think what they were for. A thin smoke went up from a farm, and in the east the sun was rising, as it had risen on all the days before, yet it seemed to me that I had never truly seen it.

"It is over," I said, and did not know what I meant. I only knew that I wept, and that it was good.
