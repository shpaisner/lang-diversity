I have set myself to tell how the world changed, and I find I must begin with how small I was before it changed. I was William Leadford, born in the Five Towns of Staffordshire, where the pot-banks stand shoulder to shoulder and the smoke of their bottle-ovens hangs between the hills and the sky like a dirty ceiling. I was a clerk of sorts, and then a clerk out of work, and then something less than that. My father had been a potter's thrower. My mother had died of the lungs the potters die of. Between the black walls of our street there was nothing to look at but the backs of other lives, and I had learned to look at nothing, and to hate it.

Then there was Nettie.

I do not know how to make you see her. She was Nettie Stanton, a pale, bright-eyed girl with a quick way of lifting her chin, and she lived in a house where the lace curtains were a little cleaner than their neighbours'. I had loved her since we were children together at the board school. I had, in my slow and sullen fashion, supposed that she would marry me when I had a little money, and I supposed it so thoroughly that I never troubled to ask her. That is how poor men love: they are too poor to say it, and too proud to be refused.

I was not clever. I had only a great stubborn capacity for wanting, and for being hurt.

She went in October, on a wet Tuesday, and she left a note upon the kitchen table for her mother to find. I have the note still, or the substance of it; it is among the few things I carried out of the old life. I cannot stay and be nothing. Do not follow me. Do not be angry with Will. That last clause was the cruelest; it was so kindly meant.

Verrall had taken her. Edward Verrall, with his soft collars and his good boots, who had come to the Potteries on some business of his father's and stayed to read poetry on Sunday afternoons to a girl who had never been read to. He was a young man of a thoughtful, restless, unfinished sort, with ideas about the future and a little money of his own, and he talked to her as if she were a person and not a pair of hands. I saw them once upon the canal bank, and I saw her face, and I knew.

I walked all that night on the slag heaps above Hanbridge, in the rain, with my hands in my pockets and my teeth set. I did not weep. I thought, with a clearness that frightens me now, of Verrall's throat, and of how it would feel beneath my thumbs. The lamps of the town glimmered below like a spilt tray of sparks, and the whole sooty valley seemed a vast machine for producing exactly such a night as this.

It was in that autumn that people began to speak of the comet.

At first it was only a line in the newspapers, a smudge upon some astronomer's photographic plate, a faint thing in the constellation of the Lion. Then it was a paragraph, and then a column. It was a new comet, they said, and it was coming straight toward us. Some said it would pass harmlessly, a splendid ornament for the winter sky. Others, more learned and more doleful, spoke of a collision, or of a poisoning of the air by strange gases from its tail.

I confess I cared nothing. A comet seemed a very distant and a very foolish matter beside my private wound. Yet I remember that in the evenings I would stand at the end of our street and see it, a little blur of pale fire above the chimneys, growing a very little each night, and I felt toward it a kind of surly kinship. Let it come, I thought. Let it smash the lot of us, and the pot-banks, and Verrall's soft hands, and Nettie's lifted chin. I should not be sorry.

The men in the public-house talked of it for a week and then forgot it, for there was a larger matter on their tongues.

That larger matter was war. It was always at the edge of things then, like the smell of gas in a closed room. The newspapers, which were owned by men who sold more copies when we were frightened, spoke of Germany and of the fleets, of ultimatums and of "the inevitable day." The foreign ministers made speeches that said nothing, and the armies, I understand, made plans that said a great deal. Every nation lay armed in the dark, watching its neighbours' windows.

I sat one evening in the Cock and Anchor with a man named Swint, who was out of work like myself and a good deal better informed. Swint had opinions. He had a red, argumentative face and a habit of tapping the table with one finger as if driving nails into the argument.

"They'll have us in it by Christmas," he said. "And who'll fight? You. Me. Not the gentlemen who write the leaders. They'll find a use for a man without a job, Leadford; they always do."

"Let them," I said.

"That's no way to talk."

"It's the only way I've left," I said, and I stared into my beer and thought, not of the Kaiser or of Europe, but of a cottage somewhere in the south where a woman was laughing at something a man had read to her. That was the whole of my politics. I understand now that it was the whole of most men's, and that this was why the world was so near to ruin.

I got the revolver from a pawnbroker in Bursley, for fifteen shillings. The man looked at me over his spectacles as he wrapped it in brown paper, and I think he guessed; but he was a tradesman, and a tradesman's business is to sell and not to know.

I learned where they were by an ugly chain of inquiries I will not detail: a letter from Nettie to her mother, a postmark, a name let slip. They had taken a house on the edge of the Kentish downs, within sight of the sea. That fixed the thing. I took the night train from Stoke, third class, with the parcel in my breast pocket pressing against my ribs like a second heart.

I remember the carriage, with its smell of damp wool and tobacco, and the sleeping, jolting bodies of my fellow passengers. I remember the stations that came and went in a lamplit blur: Rugby, Willesden, the vast cinder-yards of London. Toward dawn I wiped the steamed window, and there, low over the roofs, hung the comet, and it was no longer a smudge. It was a great ghostly thing, with a head of greenish light, and a tail flung half across the heavens. The other passengers slept on. I alone looked at it, and I had a curious sense that it was looking at me.

I came to the house toward evening of the following day, after a long tramp from the railway station across the chalk. It was a still, strange, windless evening. The sea lay below me like a sheet of dull metal, and the sky had a quality I cannot describe: a hard, expectant clearness, as though the air itself were holding its breath. The comet filled a quarter of the heavens now, and the dusk beneath it was tinged with a pale and unearthly green. Birds had ceased to sing. A dog somewhere howled once and was silent.

The cottage stood before me, small and white, with a lamp lit in the window. I could see a figure move across the light. It was Nettie. She had her hair down, and was laughing, and the sight of her laughing, so near, so utterly beyond me, struck through all my plans like a knife through paper. I took the revolver out and stood in the lane with it hanging in my hand. My heart was beating heavily; my mouth was dry.

"Now," I said aloud. "Now."

But even as I said it I became aware that the light about me was changing. The green had deepened, and a faint, sweet, unfamiliar odour, like nothing I had ever smelt in the reek of the Potteries, drifted over the down. The grass seemed to shiver. The hedge, the lamp, the white wall of the cottage, took on a strange, brilliant, translucent look, as if the world were being seen through a pane of emerald glass. I felt a lightness in my head, and a curious reluctance in my hand.

I raised the pistol. I saw Verrall come to the window and put his arm about her shoulders, and they both turned and looked up at the sky, not at me, and their faces were full of awe.

Then the air itself seemed to sigh, and the green came down upon the world like a falling veil.
