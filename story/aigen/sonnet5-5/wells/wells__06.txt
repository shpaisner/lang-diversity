I

I have to tell you first what manner of man I was, for without that the rest will not be understood. I was William Leadford, twenty-three years old, the son of a potter's packer who had died of lead and drink in the same winter, and I was a clerk at Rawdon's Pot-bank at eighteen shillings a week. I lived in a back room in Clayton, in the heart of the Staffordshire smoke, where the kilns stood about the town like fat, sullen idols and the very hedges wore a grey fur of soot. I had read a little, enough to know that I was cheated and not enough to know by whom. I was sore and angry and ambitious, a small, tight, hot-tempered creature in a celluloid collar.

And I loved Nettie Stewart. I loved her as such creatures do, jealously and with a sort of grudge, as though she had been a share in the world that had been withheld from me. She was a slender girl with brown hair and a trick of lifting her chin, and she was the one thing in Clayton that did not seem to have been made out of clay and smoke.

I had thought she was mine. We had walked together on Sunday evenings along the canal path and talked of the future, which I had planned for both of us like a clerk drawing up an invoice. I had never once supposed that she might have a plan of her own.

II

It was in the February of that year that the comet first came into our talk. It had been a speck in the telescopes since the previous autumn, and then a paragraph, and now it was a thing in the evening papers with a diagram. At Rawdon's the men argued over it in the dinner-hour, sitting on upturned crates. Some said it would strike us, and some that it would miss us, and old Hickson the saggar-maker said that it was a judgment on the masters, and was laughed at, and went on saying it.

I cared nothing for it. I remember reading the astronomers' figures, the tail of so many million miles, the strange new lines in the spectrum, and thinking, with a bitter satisfaction, that if it were really coming to smash the world it would at least smash Rawdon's and the Rawdons with it. That was the whole of my cosmic feeling. The sky was a long way off, and Nettie was near, and not as near as she had been.

For she had grown quiet. She had a way of looking past me when I spoke. She had begun to speak of "Mr. Verrall," who had a motor-car and a house at Lord Redwood's end of the county, and who had been kind to her father about a lease.

III

I saw Verrall once before the end, and it was enough. He came down the High Street on a wet afternoon in a fur-collared coat, tall and easy and fair, with that look of having never in his life been refused. He lifted his hat to Nettie, who was with me, and she coloured, and I hated her for colouring.

"That is the man," I said, when he had passed.

"What man?"

"The man who is going to take you away from me."

She laughed at me, and the laugh was too quick, too bright; it was the laugh of a person who has been found out and means to brazen it. "You talk like a book, Willie," she said. "Mr. Verrall has been very good to Father."

"I have no doubt of it," I said.

We quarrelled there in the wet street, with the tramcars grinding by, and I said things that I would give a great deal now to have left unsaid, and she said things that were truer than I could bear. I went home with my heart like a stone in a boot, and lay awake listening to the trains shunting in the yard, and thinking how the world was divided into those who took and those who were taken from.

IV

She went on the first Thursday in March. I had it from her father, a grey, bewildered little man who stood on my landing with his hat in both hands as though he had come to confess to a murder.

"She's left a letter," he said. "She's gone with him, Willie. They've gone to London, and abroad after. She says she is sorry. She says she could not bear it, the smoke and the pinching, and that you would understand in time."

I took the letter and I did not read it. I put it in the fire, and watched the blue edge run along the paper, and I said nothing at all. Then I sat down on my bed, and I think I sat there until the light went.

That night, going out for air, I looked up and saw the comet for the first time with my own eyes. It was low in the north-west, over the furnace-glare of Hanley, a pale smear with a fan of light behind it. It was no bigger than a thumb-smudge on a window, but it seemed to me that it looked at me. I stood in the road with my fists clenched, and I swore a foolish, melodramatic oath, which I will not set down. It had Verrall's name in it, and a revolver.

V

The weeks that followed were a sort of fever. The newspapers were full of Europe now, as well as the comet: Germany and France sparring over some African coast, the Russians mobilising on a frontier I could not have found on a map, the Powers exchanging notes in that polished and menacing language that precedes the shedding of rivers of blood. In the Potteries they were laying men off. The pot-banks went on half time. Rawdon's put me on three days, and I took the news as a man takes a blow who has already been beaten.

I pawned my father's watch and bought a revolver from a pawnbroker in Burslem, a short, ugly, five-chambered thing, with a handful of cartridges in a screw of paper. I practised nothing. I merely carried it, and its weight against my hip was a comfort, as a grievance is a comfort.

I learnt by a roundabout road that Verrall and Nettie had come back from abroad, hurried home by the war scare, and were staying at his father's house near Lord Redwood's park. I thought of nothing else. The comet grew each night, till it filled a quarter of the sky, and a strange tinge of green came into the dusk, and the dogs howled in the yards. And the people went about their business, stupid, quarrelsome and afraid, as if the end of the world were a matter of the rates.

VI

I walked there. It was thirteen miles, and I walked them through a night such as I had never seen, in a light that had no business in a Staffordshire sky. The comet stood overhead now, an immense green-white wonder, its tail flung across half the heavens, and the fields lay under it as under a sea. The hedges were silver-green and the road a pale river. Not a bird sang. Far away, towards Newcastle, a distant gun-like thudding of some furnace-hammer went on like a pulse.

I came to the lodge in the grey before the dawn, and found the gate open, and went up through the park among the dark beeches. The house was dim before me. And then, in the garden on the terrace, I saw them: Verrall in his shirt-sleeves, with a cigarette's small red eye, and Nettie in a white wrap beside him, her face turned up to the sky. They were not talking. They stood together as people stand at a death-bed, watching.

I took out the revolver. My hand shook so that the barrel wavered across them both, and I remember thinking, quite clearly, "I shall hit her." I came out from the trees and walked on to the gravel, and Verrall turned and saw me.

"Why, it's Leadford," he said, and his voice, I swear, was not frightened but strangely gentle, as though he had been expecting me. Nettie said nothing, but her eyes were very wide.

I raised the weapon. And as I did so the whole sky changed. A tremor went through the green light, as of a veil drawn aside. A sharp, sweet, unfamiliar smell came upon the air, like no smell that I had ever known, and a great stillness fell over the garden. My finger would not close. I tried to speak, and my tongue was heavy. The park wavered, and the terrace tilted gently, and I saw Nettie's white figure sink slowly down upon the stones, as one sinks into sleep.

"Look," said Verrall, from very far away. "Look at the sky."

And the revolver slid out of my fingers.
