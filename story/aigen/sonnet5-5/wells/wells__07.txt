I.

I was twenty-two when Nettie went from me, and I remember that the day itself was fine and indifferent, as though nothing whatever had happened. There was a letter on the mantel-shelf of my mother's kitchen, propped against the tea-caddy, and the clock ticked beside it with that same dull, regular, domestic insolence. I read it standing, with my cap still on. It said that she was sorry, that she could not bear the life before her, the dirt and the narrow rooms and the waiting, and that Mr. Verrall had been very kind. That was the word she used: kind.

I need hardly say what manner of life it was from which she fled. We lived in the Five Towns, under a sky the colour of an old saucepan, among the pot-banks and slag-heaps and the little blackened streets that run uphill and downhill like a sickness. I was a clerk of sorts at a pottery, and my wages were eighteen shillings. Nettie's father kept a failing garden. We had talked of a house with a strip of yard behind it, and of Sunday afternoons, and for two years I had saved toward that house, a shilling at a time.

Verrall had a motor-car, a wide, careless laugh, and a father who owned half a hill of ironstone. He had come to our district to "see something of the working side of the business," and he saw Nettie.

I crushed the letter in my hand. I do not think I felt grief then. I felt a kind of blind, hot swelling, like a boiler without a valve, and I stood in that kitchen and hated the whole creaking order of things that had made him rich and me a clerk.

II.

It was in these days that the Comet began to creep upon the common mind.

Few of us had given it a thought through the spring. It was a faint smudge, discovered by some professional star-gazer, and the newspapers handled it as a thing to fill a corner. But the smudge grew. By late summer, when I walked home from the works in the dusk, there was a small pale disc in the east above the chimneys, with a tail of ghostly feathers behind it, and men stood about at street corners looking up with their pipes gone cold.

My friend Parload had a post at an observatory, and he knew more than he cared to say. He was a lean, stooping man with ink on his cuffs, and he came to me one evening, in my misery, and talked of the Comet as though it were the one clean subject left in the world.

"It's the spectrum," he said. "There's a new line in it. Something we haven't a name for. It's coming close, Leadford, closer than anything on record."

"Will it strike us?"

"No. It will pass. But we shall go through the tail of it."

"And then?"

He rubbed his nose. "I don't know. Nobody knows. Perhaps nothing."

I laughed, I remember, because the Comet seemed so small a matter beside Nettie. I thought of the heavens as a vast and idle show put on for the amusement of better people.

III.

But there was another menace besides the Comet, and it was nearer, and it had no beauty in it.

All that autumn the newspapers talked of war. A quarrel over some frontier, a harbour, a squabble of diplomatists with unpronounceable names, had set the great Powers glaring at one another across Europe, each with its millions of men and its armoured ships. The papers I read at the works were full of ultimatums and mobilisations, and of the base, insistent drumming of the patriots. At the Duke's Head, where I spent more evenings than were good for me, men who had never been farther than Stoke argued about the German Emperor with the zeal of cabinet ministers.

I found, to my shame, that I welcomed it. That is the truth, and the truth is what I set out to tell. In my wretchedness I wished for the crash. I wanted the whole smug machine to go down, Verrall and his father and his motor-car with it. I would stand in the bar with my mug before me and think: Let them come. Let the guns begin. I was not a good man, nor even a very sane one. I was one of a great multitude of small, aching, ill-used people, and we were all, I think, a little like the fuel of the conflagration, only waiting for the spark.

Parload said once that the whole of Europe was like a sick man, feverish, and that the Comet was but a coincidence. "The fever is our own," he said.

IV.

I bought the revolver in Hanley, from a pawnbroker with a squint, for seven and sixpence. It was an ugly, old, American thing, and he threw in six cartridges.

I had learned where they were. Nettie had written once more, a timid, frightened note, and had let fall an address: a cottage near Lymington, hired by Verrall for the season, "where we are very quiet." I read it over many times. I had no plan, only an image, which I carried about in my head like a coal: a door, a lamp, Verrall's face turning, and myself in the doorway.

I told my mother I was going to look for work in the south. She looked at me, a little grey woman, and said nothing, and presently she put some bread and cheese in a paper for me, and her hand was shaking.

I travelled third-class through the long September night. At every junction there were soldiers, reservists in civilian clothes with bundles, and women walking beside them. Carriages were shunted to let troop trains pass. Posters shouted from every hoarding. In the dark windows of the train I saw the Comet, enormous now, a greenish, sickly lamp that threw faint shadows on the fields, and its tail stretched halfway up the sky like a pale arm. I remember that the people about me no longer looked at it. They were too busy being afraid of something else.

V.

I came to Lymington in the dusk of the next day, and walked out along a lane between hedges that smelt of dust and late blackberries.

The light was strange. The sun had gone down in a stain of sulphur, yet the sky in the east did not darken as skies should, but grew paler, more luminous, a sea-green, as though the Comet had thinned the very air. The birds were silent. A dog howled somewhere and ceased. I felt a tightness in my chest, and a curious lightness in my head, and I told myself it was hunger and hate.

I found the cottage at last: a low white house behind a hedge of privet, with a lamp already lit in the window. I stood in the road for a long time with my hand upon the revolver in my pocket. I could see a figure move across the curtain. It was Nettie, I knew it by the carriage of her head, and my heart gave a great ugly leap.

A little way off I heard the tramp and murmur of a column of men, marching somewhere to some station or camp; and a bugle. And I thought, with a clearness that astonishes me now: This is the end of everything. There will be no tomorrow for any of us, so what does it matter?

I pushed open the gate. The gravel crunched. The green light lay upon the roses like a veil, and the lamp in the window seemed to swim and grow faint.

VI.

I had my hand on the latch when the air changed.

I cannot describe it as I would wish, for I had no words for it then and have few now. It was as if a great breath had been drawn in the sky and let go. A vapour, thin and green and luminous, was drifting over the hedge and along the road, rolling low upon the ground in little curling wreaths, and where it touched the hedge-leaves they seemed to shine. There was a faint sweet smell, like nothing I had known, clean as rain on stone. The marching feet had stopped. Down the lane someone cried out, a short, startled cry, and then there was no sound at all.

I tried to lift the revolver. It had grown unaccountably heavy, as heavy as a bar of lead, and my fingers would not close. A warmth ran up my arms. The lamp-lit window seemed to open into a vast, quiet distance. I saw Verrall come to the door, I think, and look out at me with a face blank with wonder, and then everything about him seemed to fall softly away. I remember that I was not angry. That is what I cannot get over, even now. The anger was going out of me like water out of a cracked jug, and a great stillness was taking its place.

I sank down upon the step with the green light all about me, and I thought, quite simply, of my mother's hands. Then the world went out like a candle in a clean wind.
