I.

I was twenty-two that year, and I remember the world as a grey and sooty place, bounded on the north by the chimneys of Clayton and on the south by the slag heaps of the Potteries. I lived with my mother in a narrow house of blackened brick, one of a hundred in a row, and I went each morning to a clerk's stool in the office of a firm of earthenware manufacturers, where I copied invoices for eighteen shillings a week and was made to feel that even that was a kindness. I was a sullen, discontented young man. I read what I could find, and what I read made the days seem meaner than before.

Nettie Stuart was the one bright thing in it. She was a slight girl with brown hair and quick, grey, mocking eyes, a draper's daughter by origin, now fallen to service in the big house on the hill. We had walked together on Sunday afternoons by the canal. I had talked to her of a future that I had no earthly means of reaching, and she had listened, I think, as one listens to a boy describing a country he has never seen. I believed that she would marry me. I believed it with the dull, obstinate faith of a man who has nothing else to believe in.

Of the comet I thought nothing. The newspapers spoke of it in a corner, as a small, curious smudge among the stars.

II.

It was Verrall who took her from me. He was the son of the great house, a young man with an easy voice and an easy way with money, who had been to Oxford and to places beyond it, and looked on the neighbourhood as a stage on which he could play at leisure. I had seen him once, in a motor-car, laughing at something beside him, and I had hated him then without knowing why.

Nettie went in October. There was no scene. There was a note, left with her landlady, in her small, hurried hand. She said she was sorry. She said I should never understand, and that it was better so. She said there were things she wanted that I could not give. It was all true, and it was the truth that I could not forgive.

I read it standing in the passage of my mother's house, with the gas hissing above me. Then I went out into the street and walked until I was beyond the lamps, among the black hedges and the pit mounds, and there I cursed her, and Verrall, and God, and the whole order of things that gave one man a motor-car and another eighteen shillings. When I came home my mother looked at my face and said nothing. She was a wise woman in her narrow way.

III.

All that autumn the comet grew. It hung in the east before dawn, a pale, ragged feather, and then in the evening too, with a strange green tinge in its tail that the astronomers argued over. The papers gave it more space each week. But they gave more to Europe. There was a quarrel in the Balkans, and a quarrel in Morocco, and the Germans were building ships. Men in the pothouses spoke of war as men speak of a coming holiday, with a kind of sour relish. There were strikes in the pits that winter, and soldiers in the streets of Hanley. Everybody seemed to be waiting for something, and every year, every month, the waiting grew more bitter.

I cared for none of it. I had a smaller, narrower war of my own. I learnt, from a gossiping fellow-clerk, that Verrall had taken Nettie to a cottage on the edge of the moor, beyond the Wolds, and that he meant to keep her there until he tired of her. I carried that piece of knowledge about with me for weeks as one carries a stone in the pocket.

Then I bought a revolver. I bought it from a pawnbroker in Burslem, and I paid seven and sixpence for it, and the man did not ask my name.

IV.

I started on the evening of the last day, though of course I did not know that it was the last. The sky was extraordinarily clear. The comet filled a quarter of the heavens, a vast, luminous, green-white sweep of light that threw a second twilight upon the fields, and the stars about it seemed small and withdrawn. People stood at their doors to watch. I passed a group of colliers on the road, silent, with their pipes out, looking up. A woman was crying quietly at a gate, and I do not think she knew why.

I walked eleven miles. The road ran upward through the bare hills, and the night grew colder and stranger with every mile. There was a curious stillness in it. The wind dropped. The dogs were quiet. Once I thought that I smelt something faint and sweetish on the air, like the breath of a hothouse. But I did not heed it. My mind was fixed on one picture: the door of the cottage, Verrall's face, Nettie's face, and my own hand coming up steadily with the weapon in it. I had gone over the scene so often that it had become a sort of joyless prayer.

I remember saying aloud on the road, "I will do it." I remember the sound of my own voice, thin and absurd under that enormous sky.

V.

The cottage stood in a hollow of the moor, a low whitewashed place with a light in one window. I came upon it a little before midnight. I saw the light, I saw a motor-car under a lean-to shed, and I felt my heart beating in my throat like a hammer.

I had meant to knock. Instead I stood in the garden, among the dead stalks of someone's cabbages, and looked up. The comet had come low. It seemed no longer a thing among the stars, but a thing among us, a green, shining vault hung just above the roofs. The whole moor was lit with an unearthly pallor. And then I saw, drifting down from it, a thin pale mist that rolled along the ground like smoke, and the grass took a faint sparkle where it passed.

I did not understand. I raised the revolver, foolishly, as though to point it at the mist. A giddiness came over me, a sweet, languid faintness, and the weapon felt suddenly very heavy. I thought of my mother, and of the canal on a Sunday afternoon, and of the grey eyes that had mocked me. I thought: this is what dying is. And I sank down upon the path, and the green light closed over me like water.

VI.

I woke to a morning such as I had never seen.

The air was the first thing. It was cool, and sweet, and absolutely clean, so that each breath was a kind of drinking. I lay upon my back and looked up into a sky of tender, luminous blue, in which no trace of the comet remained. A lark was singing somewhere out of sight. The moor about me was green, vividly green, as though washed in the night, and the dew on the stalks beside my face held the sun like jewels.

Then I knew that I was changed. I do not mean that I thought it; I felt it, as one feels warmth. The old, hot, knotted bitterness that had lain in my chest for months was gone. I looked for it, I tried to find it, and in its place I found only a kind of clear, wondering sorrow, and beyond that a great quietness. I sat up. The revolver lay in the grass by my hand, small and ugly, a cheap thing of metal. I stared at it. I could not think how I had ever imagined that it would answer any question.

A door opened. Verrall came out of the cottage, bareheaded, with his coat open and his face drawn and strange, and stood blinking at the light. He saw me. For a long moment we looked at each other across the little garden, and I knew that the same thing had come to him.

"Leadford," he said, in a low voice. "Good God. You came to kill me."

"I did," I said. "I think I did. It seems a very long while ago."

He came slowly down the path and, to my wonder, held out his hand.
