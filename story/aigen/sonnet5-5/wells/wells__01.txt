I.

I was twenty-two that year, and I had by then discovered, in the dull, resentful fashion of my kind, that the world was an arrangement made by other people. I lived in Clayton, in the Potteries, in a row of small brick houses so grimed with the smoke of the bottle-kilns that they seemed to have been cut out of the same black cloud. I was a clerk in the office of Rawdon's, which made sanitary ware, and I was paid eighteen shillings a week to copy figures that grew no fewer for my copying. My mother washed and sewed. My father was dead, and had left nothing but a temper, which I had inherited.

Above all this, night after night, hung the Comet. It had begun as a mere speck in the glass of astronomers, and the newspapers had given it a paragraph. Now it was a small bluish smear in the evening sky, and the paragraph had become a column. A professor said it would strike us; another said it would not; a third said that in either case we were of no importance. I remember that I agreed with the third. I looked up at it from the yard behind our house, over the wash-line and the rotting fence, and I felt only that it was one more large thing that had nothing to do with me.

What had to do with me was Nettie.

II.

I had known Nettie Stuart since we were children together. She was the daughter of a gardener on the Verrall estate, a quick, bright, brown-haired girl with a trick of looking at one sidelong, and I loved her with all the narrow intensity of which a starved young man is capable. I do not think I loved her wisely. I loved her as a man holds a coin in a crowd, tightly, suspiciously, and with an eye on everybody else. I talked to her of my grievances, of the injustice of Rawdon's, and of the day when I should do something, I did not say what. She listened, and sometimes she laughed, and once she kissed me.

Then came Verrall.

He was the son of the great house, a tall, easy, well-tended young man who had been to Oxford and had brought back from it nothing in particular but a manner. He saw Nettie in her father's garden, and was pleased to be gracious. I saw it all happen, as one sees a distant cart coming down a hill toward a child in the road, and could do nothing. I made scenes. I said bitter, stupid, boyish things. And one evening in October, when the Comet had grown to the size of a thumbnail and the air had begun to feel curiously thin, I went to the Stuarts' cottage and found Nettie's mother crying in the kitchen, and a note upon the table.

Nettie had gone, and Verrall with her.

III.

I will not pretend that my grief was noble. It was a black, hot, choking thing, mostly made up of injured pride. I walked back through the streets of Clayton that night, past the public-houses and the chapels and the lamps that burned with a dirty yellow flame, and every face I met seemed to me to have known and to be grinning.

The talk in the street was all of war. For months Europe had been bristling like a street of quarrelsome dogs. There were German ships and English ships, and French frontiers and Russian armies, and dreadnoughts in the papers and reservists in the railway stations. Men at the corner argued it with a strange relish, as though a war were a kind of weather that would at least be different from the weather they had. I heard a man say, "It'll clear the air," and another, "It'll be the Comet that does it, you mark me." And I, who might have thought that all this was a vast and criminal stupidity, thought only: Let it come. Let it break everything. Let the great houses fall, and the Verralls with them.

That was the state of my mind. I record it because I have since learned how little of the wickedness in the world is wickedness at all, and how much is mere unhappiness, left to turn sour in the dark.

IV.

I borrowed a revolver. It was an old, ill-kept thing, got from a pawnbroker's for seven shillings by a man I knew in the works, and I cannot say that I intended to use it. I intended to have it. There is a difference, though it vanishes in the moment of action.

I learned from Nettie's mother that they had gone to London, and I followed by the night train, in a third-class carriage that smelled of wet wool and oranges. Opposite me sat a commercial traveller who read an evening paper by the dim lamp and told me, without being asked, that the Germans meant business. Beyond the window the black fields ran by under a sky in which the Comet stood, now unmistakable, a pale and spreading stain, with a faint streamer behind it like a smear of breath upon a glass.

I got out at Euston in a grey dawn, and wandered about London for two days. I asked at hotels and lodgings and was laughed at, or not noticed. I had no money, and little sleep. By the second night I was walking on the Downs outside the town, where I had heard they might be, with the revolver heavy in my pocket and my brain going round and round like a wheel with no cart attached.

And all the time the sky grew strange.

V.

It began as a light. The stars dimmed, and then the whole heaven took a faint tinge of green, as if the night were being slowly drained of its blackness. The air became very still. No wind moved, no dog barked, no distant train cried out. It seemed to me that the world had stopped to listen.

I stood upon the turf and looked up. The Comet was no longer a point or a smear. It filled a quarter of the heavens with an unearthly beauty, and out of its pale heart there fell, or seemed to fall, a delicate shining dust. I thought of the professors who had said it would strike us, and I felt no fear at all. I felt only a vast and curious detachment, as though my hatred, my small scheme, and the whole bitter structure of Clayton were things I had been reading about in a book.

Then, among the furze at the edge of the hollow below me, I saw two figures walking. A woman, a man; the woman's head turned a little, sidelong, as Nettie's head always turned. My hand went to my pocket. I remember the cold of the metal, the pounding of my heart, and the green light lying upon the grass like water.

VI.

I had taken three steps forward when the air changed.

I cannot describe it better than to say that it grew sweet. A breath came up the slope, no wind, but a great soft respiration of the earth, and with it a faint odour that I have never smelled before or since, and which I can only compare to the smell of rain on dry ground, and of bread, and of the very beginning of spring. My throat tightened. I drew a deep breath, and then another, and with each there went out of me something I had carried so long that I had mistaken it for part of my body.

My legs failed me. I sank down upon the turf, and the revolver slid from my fingers into the grass. I did not reach for it. I watched, with a wondering indifference, the green light thicken about me, and the stars go out one by one behind a luminous veil, and the two distant figures stop, and stand, and turn their faces upward, as if some voice had called them by name.

I thought: They are only people. The thought came quite simply, without anger, as a child might say it. Verrall is only a man, and Nettie is only a woman, and I am only another; and we are all of us lost together in the dark, and have been so very long.

Then a great quiet closed over my mind, and I lay back upon the earth. The last thing I knew was the sound of my own breathing, slow and even, and a tear that I had not noticed running warm into my ear; and beyond it all, high up, the soft green burning of the sky.
