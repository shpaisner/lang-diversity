William Leadford read the letter standing, in the grey kitchen of the lodging in Burslem, with the effect of a man who, having braced himself against one blow, is struck from the other side. It was a small letter, written on a small sheet of a violet tint that he had himself, as a matter of simple and shaming fact, bought for her at Christmas. It said, in effect, that she was sorry, that she was not unkind, and that she had gone. The name of Verrall was not in it; it did not need to be. The name had been put there, he now felt, by the whole intolerable weight of what she had left unsaid.

He laid the sheet on the oilcloth and looked about him as though the room might volunteer some explanation. It volunteered only its habitual poverty: the clock, the cracked jug, the window that gave upon a wall, and above the wall a smoke so continuous that it had long ceased to be weather and had become a kind of condition. He had been, until this moment, a young man of some small fierce hope. He had read, he had attended a class, he had supposed that the world, if sufficiently importuned, would at last remark him. It had remarked Verrall instead, who had not importuned it at all, and who had come down from his motor-car among the kilns with that look of being quite naturally expected.

He did not weep. He felt, rather, an odd enlargement of perception, as if grief were a lens through which every familiar object grew harder and more distinct.

Nettie's father received him in the front room, where he sat before a fire he had not the spirit to stir. He was an elderly person of a mild, bewildered integrity, and he had the air of being in the presence of a disaster too large for his furniture.

"She said nothing to me, Mr. Leadford," he declared. "Nothing. I had thought her settled. I had thought you—" He stopped, and the unfinished clause hung between them with a cruelty that no completion could have equalled.

Leadford said that it was of no consequence, which was a lie of such magnitude that he was himself a little awed by it. He asked only whether the old man knew where they had gone. The old man did not; but he knew, he said, what was in the evening paper, and he put it forward with the pathetic eagerness of one who, unable to mend a private sorrow, would at least share a public one. It was all, it appeared, the Comet, and the Germans. There was a new telegram from Berlin, and a new alarm in the Balkans, and the two matters were set side by side in the columns with an equanimity that struck Leadford as almost indecent.

"They say it's to be a great sight," said Nettie's father vaguely. "And a great war, besides."

Leadford looked at the fire. It seemed to him, in that dull hour, that a world which could entertain both prospects with such composure could hardly be expected to notice what had befallen a potter's clerk.

He found Parload that night on the hill above the town, where the young man, whose spectacles and whose passion were equally out of scale with his employment, had set up his shabby telescope amid the cinders. Parload was a person for whom the heavens were an intimacy; he greeted the visitor without turning from the eyepiece, as one might greet a neighbour across a garden wall.

"You'll want to see it," he said. "It's nearer. There's the tail beginning, you'll observe—a mere breath as yet."

Leadford looked, and saw a pale smudge, a thing so faint and so remote that it seemed an impertinence for it to have occupied the newspapers. Yet the little disc held him; he was aware, as he stood there, of a monstrous irrelevance in his own anguish, and of a perverse comfort in the irrelevance.

"It's the spectrum that's queer," Parload went on. "A new line, a green one, that nobody can place. Something in it that we haven't got."

"And the war?" said Leadford, from nowhere.

"Oh, the war!" Parload gave a short laugh. "The war is the one thing in the sky we've understood perfectly for years."

They stood together in the cindery dark, the astronomer and the abandoned lover, and below them the furnaces of the Five Towns threw their red unrest upon the clouds.

It was from Parload, though not by Parload's intention, that he learned the rest. A cousin of the astronomer's was in service near Lady Verrall's, and had it from the cook that the young master had taken a house, for the summer, on the Surrey side of London, and had taken it, moreover, for two. The information arrived in Leadford's mind not as a fact but as a destination. It stood up before him, definite and illuminated, like a station on a line he had been travelling blind.

He did not, even then, say to himself what he proposed. The mind has its decencies, and it practises them even at the brink; what he said to himself was that he should like to see them. But he had in his possession, in the bottom of a box, an old revolver that had been bought in a season of imagined danger and never fired, and he found, when the night was far advanced, that his hand had gone to the box as to a matter long arranged. The weapon lay in his palm with a small cold candour. It made no pretence of being anything but what it was.

He regarded it with a kind of impersonal curiosity. How strange, he thought, that a thing so modest should so abruptly simplify a life! It abolished the clerk, the class, the long unrewarded patience; it substituted a single, sufficient, terrible sentence, and he felt, putting it into his pocket, almost relieved.

The journey southward he afterwards remembered less as a sequence than as an atmosphere. The trains were crowded with an anxious, loud, holiday-making population who talked of the Comet and the Continent in the same breath; at a junction a number of young men in the new khaki sang something with a flourish, and a woman near him wept without apparent cause. Beneath it all he fancied a vast, suppressed tension, as of a house in which everyone has agreed not to mention the one thing that is in every mind.

The sky, as the day declined, took on an altered quality. It was not that the Comet was visible—it was too early, the light too strong—but that the air itself appeared to have thinned and sharpened, to wait. Once, from the carriage window, he saw over a field of stubble a strange pallor along the horizon, faintly, delicately green, and the sight touched him with an obscure misgiving which he put down to fatigue.

He was conscious, too, of a certain growing shame, though shame was not the word for it. It was rather the sense of being the only person in the whole hurrying country who had a private errand, and of that errand's growing, as the miles consumed themselves, so gross a thing beside the general suspense. Yet he held to it. It was all that he had been given to hold.

The house, when at the close of the evening he found it, was a villa of the pretentious modern sort, white and gabled, with a lawn that sloped to a row of poplars. Lights burned in the lower windows. He stood at the gate in the thickening dusk and perceived, with an acuteness that was almost physical, the immense and shining otherness of the life that was going on within—its lamps, its linen, its confident repose.

Then he saw her. She had come out upon the verandah, in a pale dress, and she stood with her hand upon a pillar, looking up. Verrall appeared behind her, careless, handsome, and said something at which she turned, and laughed; and the laugh, carried down the lawn, was the laugh of a girl who had never in her life been afraid. It was the very laugh he had so laboured to deserve.

He drew the revolver. His hand, he noticed, was quite steady, and this steadiness seemed to him the single dignified thing in the whole situation. He advanced two paces upon the gravel. And it was precisely then, as he lifted his arm, that the sky above the poplars, which had been darkening in the ordinary way, began to alter—to take on, from the zenith downward, a tint he had no name for, a luminous, tender, appalling green.

The green deepened as he stood there, until the poplars, the white gables, the figures on the verandah, and his own raised hand were all bathed in it as in the light of some immense and gentle sea. He heard Nettie cry out; he saw Verrall look up, with his careless face for once undone; and then a sweetness, a coolness, entered his lungs, and the revolver, with an odd slowness, left his fingers and went to the gravel.

He did not fall so much as lie down, with the profound acquiescence of a tired child. The last thing he knew was the sound of the evening—the far reverberation of some unimaginable gun, the cry of a bird—receding into a stillness of such perfect amplitude that it seemed less an ending than a hush before speech.

When he woke, it was to a morning of such clearness that he lay for some while merely looking at it. The air had a wonderful lightness; the grass beside his cheek was bright with every separate blade. He sat up, and his first sensation was that of an enormous, impersonal clemency, as though some old and obstinate dispute had been quietly withdrawn from the world's docket. He looked at the revolver on the gravel and saw it as a foolish, rusted object, the toy of some distant and rather sorry person. Then he looked toward the verandah, where Nettie and Verrall were rising, bewildered, from the boards, and he felt for them neither hatred nor surrender, but a wide, grave, and wondering pity that included himself.
