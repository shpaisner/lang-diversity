The news reached William Leadford on a Thursday, in the kitchen of his mother's house at Clayhanger, and it reached him, as such news commonly does, by the hand of someone who took no pleasure in the carrying of it and a good deal in the watching. His sister-in-law set the letter beside his plate and withdrew to the scullery with an air of having discharged a public office. He read it standing. It was not long; Nettie had never been given to length, and it was part of the odd, cold sharpness of the hour that he should find himself admiring, even then, the economy with which she dismissed him. She was gone, it said, with Mr. Verrall; she was sorry, in the way one is sorry for rain on another person's holiday; and he was not to follow, since following could only be, as she put it, "so very ugly for everyone."

He folded the sheet along its creases. Beyond the window the chimneys of the Potteries stood against a sky the colour of old pewter, and he had the strange impression, which he could not at once account for, that he was looking at them from a considerable distance, as at objects in a picture already sold. He had loved her, he supposed, with the whole of an undeveloped and obstinate nature; and it now appeared that this had been, to her, a circumstance as negligible as the weather in the next county.

He put on his cap and went out into the street without having touched his tea.

II

Mr. Stewart, Nettie's father, received him in the front parlour with the cautious sorrow of a man who has been robbed and who suspects the person he is telling of being, in some indirect way, the thief. It was a room of antimacassars and a clock that had never been trusted; and in it Leadford was conscious, as he had always been, of being larger than its furniture and smaller than its pretensions.

"She was a girl," said Mr. Stewart, "who looked at things. I never knew what she looked at. I'd say it was your way, Will, to look at nothing but her, and that, if you'll forgive me, is no preparation for a girl who looks at everything."

It was, Leadford reflected, as shrewd a remark as the old man had ever made, and the shrewdness of it hurt more than any rebuke would have done. He asked where they had gone. Mr. Stewart did not know; London, perhaps; perhaps farther. The young man was of the Verralls, people with money, and with that large unconsidered ease of manner which money gives to those who have never had to describe its absence. There had been motor-cars, gloves, a certain tone of voice.

"And there's the comet, besides," said Mr. Stewart, with a sort of helpless irrelevance, "all the papers full of it. Who's to say what's coming, or what anybody's doing?"

Leadford, going out again, glanced up and saw nothing; but the old man's phrase went with him like a stone in the pocket.

III

It was in those weeks that the heavens, which for the working population of the Five Towns had hitherto been merely a thing to which the smoke went up, took on the character of a public topic. The comet, discovered by some patient person in an observatory, had grown from a rumour to a smear, from a smear to a faint but definite and rather sinister stranger, with a tail that seemed to the popular eye a sort of indecent flourish. The papers discussed it between the columns that discussed the Balkans, and the Balkans, in turn, between those that discussed the Germans. It was impossible to tell which of the three portents — the star, the ultimatum, or the private misery — most nearly concerned one.

Leadford, who worked at a draughtsman's desk for a master potter and who had been, in his dim way, a reader, perceived with a bitter amusement that the world, in conspiring to be large, had chosen precisely the moment when he was most disposed to be small. Men in the tram spoke of fleets and frontiers. Women spoke of the end of things. He alone, it seemed, had an end of his own, definite and domestic, and he turned it over by night in his narrow bed with a patience that frightened him. The great news of Europe came to him as through a wall; the small news, the news of Nettie's gloves and Verrall's laugh, came as through an open door.

IV

He found out, at last, where they were; it was in the nature of such discoveries that they should be effected by accident and a postmark. A cousin of Nettie's, softened by his visible wretchedness, gave him an address in the south, a villa near the river, rented for the season; and the information lay in him for two days like a coal.

On the third he went into the town and, with a face he believed to be impassive, purchased a revolver from a pawnbroker whose own countenance suggested that he had sold such articles to impassive faces before. The transaction was brief and appallingly easy. The weapon, wrapped in brown paper, was heavier than he had supposed; it had the dull, committed weight of a thing that has already decided.

He walked home by the canal. The evening was close, the light queerly tinted; and when he looked up, there it was, above the roofs, the thing itself, no longer a smear but a presence, pale green and thin and horribly composed. It had a kind of attention in it. He stood a moment, the parcel under his arm, and it seemed to him absurd, and also quite natural, that something so remote should be so interested in a man on a towpath with a pistol. Then, because the absurdity was unbearable, he went on, and the parcel knocked softly against his ribs like a second and more resolute heart.

V

Of the journey he afterward retained only the impression of a prolonged and rattling suspension, in which the carriages were full of newspapers and the newspapers full of mobilisation. At one station soldiers sang; at another a woman wept with her face to the wall. Europe, it was clear, was leaning toward a precipice with the stupid grace of a sleepwalker, and he leaned with it, privately, toward a smaller precipice of his own.

He reached the river towards dusk. The villa, when he found it, was exactly what he had feared: white, tasteful, secure, the abode of persons who were perfectly happy and had no thought of being otherwise. There were lights in the lower windows; there was the sound of a piano, played idly, as by someone who did not care whether she was heard. He knew the touch. It was Nettie's, and the knowledge that she could play so, at such an hour, with the comet in the sky and his ruin in the hedge, was nearly the last element needed.

Yet he did not go in. He stood in the lane, under a hedge that smelt of hawthorn and dust, and a singular stillness gathered. The birds had stopped. Even the piano seemed to falter. Overhead the green light thickened, and a faint odd sweetness, like the breath of some unknown orchard, began to move in the air.

VI

He did not afterward remember drawing the weapon, though he knew it was in his hand; nor could he say at what moment the sweetness became a pressure, or the pressure a kind of music, or why the hedge, the lane, the lit windows should all have taken, at once, a glaze of the most delicate green. It seemed to him that the world was being tenderly and irresistibly rinsed. He thought of Nettie, and of Verrall, and of the small cold sentence, "so very ugly for everyone," and found with astonishment that the sentence had begun to lose its edges.

He sat down in the road. It was not faintness; it was rather as if a long-clenched thing in him, a fist he had never known he carried, had been gently opened by a hand that asked nothing. The revolver slid from his fingers into the dust. Far off, along the river, he heard a sound of men, many men, who had been marching and who now, apparently, had stopped; and it came to him dimly that they had stopped for the very reason that he had, since all through the country the same sweet air must be falling upon the same hard purposes, and undoing them.

"How odd," he thought, "how very odd," and the oddness had the quality of a prayer. Then the green closed over him like water over a stone, and he knew nothing more, neither grief nor intention nor his own name.

VII

He woke to a morning the like of which he had not conceived. The sky was of a blue so clear that it seemed less a colour than a form of attention; the hawthorn stood in the lane with every petal distinct and as if newly invented; and he lay for some time in the dust, quite without alarm, regarding these things with the grave surprise of a child admitted into a house he had always been told was shut. His head was perfectly lucid. It was this above all that struck him: the lucidity, as of a window from which a lifelong film had been wiped.

He sat up, and saw the revolver. He looked at it for a long moment, and what he felt was not remorse, which would have been a grosser thing, but a kind of wondering pity, as for a tool left out by someone who had meant very badly and understood very little. He had been, then, that person. He saw it without self-contempt, in the large, equable light, as he might have seen a mistake in a drawing, the line gone wrong at the angle, requiring only to be rubbed out and begun again.

And Nettie — he could think of Nettie now, and the thought, instead of burning, opened. She had been a girl who looked at everything; he had wished to be the whole of her looking, and no creature could have borne it. Verrall, too, was merely a young man, bewildered by his own good fortune, who had snatched at beauty as at a ball thrown to him in a field. Neither was to be hated; neither, he perceived, had ever been wholly free.

He rose. From the villa came no piano, but a door opened, and a figure stood in the garden, hatless, looking up at the sky with the same dazed, shining face he must himself be wearing. It was Verrall. Their eyes met across the hedge; and in that clear air there was no need of explanation, no accusation, no defence. Verrall lifted a hand, uncertainly, as one greets a neighbour on the first morning of a new country; and William Leadford, to his own deep and tranquil amazement, lifted his in return.
