The thing was known to everyone in Clayton before it was known to William Leadford, and this, as he afterwards reflected, was the particular cruelty of small places: that a man's disaster should circulate among his neighbours like a coin, passing through many hands, each of which had weighed it, before it was laid in his own. He had come up the cinder path from the works that evening under a sky of that dull, bruised copper which the potbanks of Staffordshire make of every sunset, and had noticed, because for some days everyone had been noticing, the small pale blot that hung above the chimneys, a thing like a thumbprint on the glass of heaven. The newspapers called it the Comet. They called it, too, with a strange simultaneity of tone, the Balkan Question and the German Note, as if the three were somehow cousins.

He thought of none of them. He thought of Nettie, as he had thought of her every evening for two years, with that mingling of hunger and abasement which was, for a young man of his means, the whole luxury of the affections. He was a clerk at nineteen shillings; she was a girl with a way of lifting her chin. He had not, he felt, ever quite deserved her. It was this humility that his mother's silence, as he entered, found so ready, and so cruelly unguarded.

"She is gone, William," said Mrs. Leadford at last, and it seemed to him that she spoke not so much to him as to the kettle. "Since the noon train. Mrs. Pell saw her at the station, with a box."

He did not at once understand. It is the privilege of the very great blow that it permits, for an interval, the mind's most trivial operations, and he found himself observing that the teapot had been placed, by some oversight, upon the wrong side of the loaf. "With a box," he repeated.

"And not alone." His mother's hands, which had never in his remembrance been idle, lay quite still upon the cloth. "You will make me say it. It was Mr. Verrall. The young man from the Rawdon side, who has been so much about the Lodge."

Verrall: the name arrived like the stroke of a gong in a quiet house, and the whole vibrating consequence of it—the white cuffs, the idle voice, the casual excellent boots—rose up before William with an insolent distinctness. He had seen the man once, on the steps of the Institute, and had been conscious, with the whole of his poor person, of being looked at as one looks at a gatepost. And Nettie, it now appeared, had been conscious of something quite else. He sat down very slowly. Outside, the green-tinged evening went on being evening, and he understood, with a kind of horror, that it would not consent to take notice.

Nettie's father received him next morning in the front room, among the antimacassars, with the air of a man who has been robbed and is disposed, on the whole, to resent the visit of the only other person who knew it. Mr. Stewart was a spare and anxious engineer, a reader of tracts and of the Staffordshire Sentinel, and he spoke of his daughter as of a failed experiment that had, at the critical moment, exploded.

"I do not defend her," he said. "But I say to you, Leadford, what I say to myself: that the times are loose. There is a looseness in the air. You see it in the papers—the Germans, the Russians, the Emperor with his hand on his sword. Everything is shaking. It is as though the very ground had become uncertain."

"And the Comet," said William, with an irony he had not intended, and which Mr. Stewart took gravely.

"And the Comet. Mr. Verrall's mother has a house at Lowestoft, I believe. They may have gone there. Or to London." He raised his tired eyes. "What will you do?"

It was the question, and William, who had not the least idea, discovered that he had answered it. "I shall find her," he said. The words fell between them with a curious finality, like a thing signed. And it struck him, in that stuffy room, that he had never before in his life announced a purpose, and that the sound of it, though it frightened him, was not wholly disagreeable.

The revolver he bought in a back street of Hanley, from a person who sold also canaries, and who asked no more questions than the canaries themselves. It was a small, ugly, serviceable object, and it lay in his pocket through the whole of the afternoon like a second and more candid conscience. He did not, he told himself, intend anything. He intended merely to possess the possibility, as certain natures can only bear a grief when it has been furnished with a grandeur; and the weapon gave him, as he sat in the third-class compartment bound southward, a sense of consequence that was perhaps the most piteous element in the whole affair.

The train crawled through a country that seemed to him unfamiliarly attentive. At every station there were men reading the evening papers with the heads bent together; at one, a woman wept without concealment, for a son, he gathered, with the reserves. The word mobilisation went along the platforms like a draught. And above the roofs, as the dusk gathered, the Comet had grown: no longer a thumbprint but a faint and lustrous stain, edged with a pallor that was not quite white, that was—he sought the word and was irritated to find it—green.

He had telegraphed nothing. He had, indeed, the dim conviction that to arrive unannounced was the only dignity left him.

Lowestoft lay under a high wind and a fine, strange light. He found the house, after some hours of bleak inquiry, at the end of a row of villas that faced the sea with a kind of blank politeness; and he stood for a long time on the opposite pavement, in the unnatural clarity of that evening, with his hand closed upon the metal in his pocket and his gaze fixed upon a lighted window.

It would be pleasant to report that he reasoned. He did nothing of the kind. He observed, merely, with the minute, helpless fidelity of the very wretched, the details of the scene: the lamp with its rose-coloured shade; the curve of a shoulder that he knew; the moving of a hand across a page. Nettie sat there, reading aloud, he supposed, for her lips moved, and beyond her, outstretched in an armchair with his boots toward the fire, Verrall listened with the faintly bored beatitude of a man to whom everything has always been brought. It was all so extraordinarily domestic. That was the thing William had not prepared for: that the outrage should wear the aspect of a quiet evening, and that the faithlessness he had come to avenge should have, for its entire furniture, a footstool and a lamp.

Something went out of him then—some rigid and unprofitable thing. He felt the revolver as one feels a stone that one has carried a long way for no reason.

It was then that the light changed.

He was not at first aware of it as a change; it came upon him as a deepening of the sense of attention, a hush in the very wind, as though the whole shore had drawn breath. The street lamps paled. The sea, which had been grey, took on a tint he had no name for, a luminous, tender, unearthly green, and the white fronts of the villas, one after another, became faintly and wonderfully stained with it, as if dipped in some cool tincture of dawn. He looked up. The sky was no longer a sky; it was a vast, silent, glowing vault, and across the zenith the Comet—but it was no longer a thing among other things—seemed to lean down toward the earth with an infinite and indifferent gentleness.

He drew his hand from his pocket. It was empty; he did not remember letting go, and he found that he did not care. A strange lightness had entered his breast, a sense not of pardon, for that implied a quarrel still in existence, but of the quarrel's having simply, mysteriously, grown small, like a figure seen from a great height. Across the road the window glowed; and in it, as in a picture, Nettie had lifted her head and was looking out toward the green. And William Leadford, standing bareheaded in the street, understood that he was looking at her for the first time without wanting anything whatever.
