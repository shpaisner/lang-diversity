It was characteristic of Leadford, as he afterwards came to think, that the largest event of his life should have announced itself to him first as a mere aggravation of the weather. He had come up out of the pot-bank at Clayhanger on an evening of late autumn, with the smoke of the Five Towns lying along the valley like a sullen tenant who declined to be put out, and had found, above the chimneys, a small, pale, obstinate thing that did not belong to the order of the lamps. It was the comet. He had heard of it for weeks. Parload, his friend, who kept ledgers by day and the heavens by night, had talked of little else. But it had remained for him an item of the newspapers, a curiosity of the same family as the Budget and the Balkans, and quite as remote from the question that occupied him. The question was Nettie.

He stood a moment at the corner by the chapel, a lean young man in a coat too thin for the season, and looked at the thing without in the least seeing it. What he saw was the small front parlour in Bursley Street, the geranium dying in its pot, and Nettie's father turning over, with a thick and helpless thumb, the one sheet of paper she had left behind.

The sheet had said very little, and it was precisely that little which he could not forgive. She had written that she was sorry, that she had not meant it to be like this, and that Mr. Verrall was very good. Leadford, going over the phrases in the fortnight since, had found in each of them a separate and exquisite cruelty. It was the goodness that wounded him. He could have borne wickedness; he would almost have welcomed it, for wickedness at least would have admitted that there had been something to be wicked against. But to be left on the ground of another man's goodness, as on the ground of a superior income, was to be told that his own love, so laboriously nursed through a hundred Sunday walks, had been a thing of no more consequence than a pattern in a catalogue.

Verrall was the son of the great house beyond the Beeches, a creature of light flannel and easy sentences, who had come down for the shooting, and had stopped, as it were, for Nettie. That was the whole of it. There had been no struggle, because there had been no rival. There had only been, on one side, the whole weight of the world, and on the other, William Leadford, who had a pound a week and a grievance.

The newspapers, in those days, were full of a different grievance, though it took him some time to perceive that the two were related. All Europe appeared to be quarrelling in the same low and fretful key. There were notes, and replies to notes; there were ministers who assured the Commons that the situation, though grave, was not without hope, and generals in distant capitals who assured their own sovereigns of the reverse. At the works the men read these things aloud at the dinner-hour, with a kind of dark relish, as people read of a fire that will not touch their own street.

Leadford read them too, and was surprised to find that he read them with sympathy. The great nations, it seemed to him, were behaving exactly as he felt. Each was convinced of its injury, each was nursing a slight too fine to be stated in any treaty, and each was preparing, with a sort of sombre pleasure, to make some other party pay. He found a bitter comfort in the thought that his private rage was but a small copy of the large one; that the whole spacious continent was, like himself, merely waiting for a pretext. The only difference was that he, unlike the Powers, had somewhere to go.

"You'll not do anything foolish," said Parload.

They were on the flat roof of Parload's mother's outhouse, where the little telescope, lashed to a clothes-prop, had been turned upon the quarter of the sky that mattered. Leadford, applying an eye to it, saw a faint smear of green, a sort of pale and ghostly flame, drawn out into a tail that seemed to him, in his state of mind, almost indecently vivid.

"It's coming nearer," Parload said. "There's a thing in the spectrum, a band, that nobody has made out. They say it's a gas. They say—" He broke off with the apologetic cough of a man who has been too long alone with a fact. "They say a good many things. But I'd as soon you didn't go about by yourself."

"I'm not going about," said Leadford. "I'm going to Lonsdale."

The word lay between them. It was the village, forty miles off, where, according to a cousin's cousin, Verrall had taken a cottage; and in Leadford's pocket, beneath the sullen weight of his hand, lay the revolver he had bought on Saturday from a man in Hanley, who had asked no question and had been paid in full.

He travelled by the slow trains of the country, through a landscape that seemed to have been emptied for the occasion. The stations were lit with an uncertain light; the porters, at each stop, looked up at the sky before they looked at the passengers. And the sky itself had begun to assume a quality he could not name. It was not darker; it was, if anything, brighter; but it was brighter with something remote from the sun, a pale, greenish, over-clear luminosity, as though the whole upper air had been rinsed.

He walked the last miles. Along the lane, between the hedges, the very leaves appeared to hold their breath, and when at length he reached the gate of the cottage, and saw a window lit and a shadow moving there, he was aware, with a clearness that astonished him, that he was not at all in a passion. He was only extremely attentive, as if the thing he had come to do had been done long ago and he were now merely arriving to witness it. The comet stood immense overhead. He put his hand upon the latch.

They were at table. That was the detail that undid him, though he was not yet aware of being undone: the cloth, the lamp, the two heads bent, and Nettie, in a gown he had never seen, lifting her face with an expression of pure and frightened welcome. Verrall rose. He was white, but he was not mean; he came a step forward, saying something, which Leadford did not hear, and Leadford, who had planned the moment so often, found that he had planned it without a voice. The revolver was in his hand. It looked, in the lamplight, extraordinarily small.

"Will," said Nettie.

It was then that the air changed. A faint sweetness, not unlike the smell of a field after rain, stole through the crack of the door; the lamp-flame leaned, turned a delicate green, and seemed to lose its warmth. He saw Verrall's hand go to his throat, saw Nettie's lips part upon a word that did not arrive, and felt in his own limbs a great and unalarming slackness, as of a man put down gently by an unseen nurse. The weapon grew heavy; the floor came up to meet him with a kind of courtesy. His last clear thought was of Parload, absurdly, and of the band in the spectrum that nobody had made out.

He woke to a light he had never seen. It lay across the boards in long, clean bars, and for some moments he did not move, because to move would have been to disturb something. The air was of a purity beyond any he could recall; it had the quality of water. Then, slowly, he became aware of the revolver, lying a yard from his hand like a thing dropped by a child, and of the strange fact that he regarded it with no more than a mild and interested wonder. He could not recover the rage. He sought it as one seeks a familiar object in a room, and it was not in its place.

He sat up. Verrall was already awake, kneeling by the hearth, and Nettie, her hair loosened, was leaning against the settle with her eyes upon him, those eyes in which he had once read so little and now read, with a swift, humbling clarity, everything: her fear, her fault, her bewildered tenderness for them both. Nobody spoke. It seemed that nothing needed to be defended, that the old dispute had been simply lifted out of the room like a heavy piece of furniture, leaving a space in which it was wonderfully easy to breathe.

"I came to kill you," said Leadford at last, and heard his own voice with surprise, for it was quite gentle. "I think I was mad. I think we all were."

"We were," said Verrall. He held out his hand, and Leadford, rising, took it, and was aware that the gesture, which a day ago would have been impossible or theatrical, was now only the plainest thing in the world. Through the open door the morning came in, immense and green-gold, over a country that seemed to have been washed and forgiven; and far off, over the hedges and the chimneys and the sleeping quarrel of Europe, he fancied he could hear a bird, beginning, with a kind of tentative joy, to sing.
