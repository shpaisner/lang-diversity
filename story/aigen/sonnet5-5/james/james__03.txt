He had learned it, in the end, not from Nettie at all, but from her sister, who delivered the fact on the doorstep in the manner of one handing over a parcel of which the contents were nobody's business but the sender's. Nettie had gone. She had gone, it appeared, on the Tuesday, by the afternoon train, in a hat that Leadford had never seen, and in the company of Mr. Verrall, whose name until that moment had signified for him merely a pleasant, careless, expensive young man who bowed from a motor-car.

He stood a long while afterward in the street at Clayhanger, with the smoke of the potbanks lying low across the roofs. It seemed to him that he was required to feel something proportionate, and that the feeling, like a debt discreetly postponed, had not yet presented itself. Above the chimneys, in the thin clearing of the dusk, hung the thing the newspapers had lately made so much of: a small, pale, ambiguous smear, which a month ago nobody had looked at and which now everybody pretended not to.

He looked at it, and for the first time in his life it looked, as he fancied, back.

II

"You'll not do anything foolish," said Parload, who kept his telescope in a back bedroom and his opinions in a state of cheerful ferment. They sat in that room among a litter of charts, and Parload's thin finger travelled across the paper with the tenderness of a man caressing a beloved's cheek. "It's a matter of weeks. They say the spectrum shows cyanogen. A gas, you understand. Poison, if one chose to be alarmed."

"I'm not alarmed," said Leadford.

"No," Parload conceded, with a glance that was almost compassion; "you've the other thing on you."

Leadford could not have said whether it was the friend's delicacy or his own pride that prevented the name from being spoken. Below them, in the street, a newsboy cried the latest from the Continent, where the Powers, it seemed, had begun to regard one another across their frontiers with that fixed and polite attention which precedes a quarrel. Everyone talked of it as of weather: it would come, or would not, and in either event one's boots would need mending. Leadford heard the cry, and the whole vast preparation of Europe, the drilling and the dispatches, seemed to him merely the larger and duller echo of his own small, private ruin. He rose, thanked Parload for the chart, and went out; and Parload, watching from the window, thought that he had never seen a man walk so exactly like one carrying something he meant to use.

III

The thing was bought on the Thursday, in a back shop at Hanley, from a person who asked no questions and appeared to require no answers. It lay in Leadford's coat-pocket with a weight altogether out of keeping with its size, and it made him, he noticed, extraordinarily polite. He gave up his seat to a woman in the tram. He was civil to the conductor. It was as if the possession of a single violent resolution had cleared the rest of his nature of all its small and customary ferocities, and left him, for the hour, almost gentle.

What he proposed was never very clearly stated, even to himself. It had to do with Verrall's face, which he wished to see altered; and it had to do, more obscurely and less bearably, with Nettie's, which he wished, perhaps, to see only once more, in order to learn what it had been all along that he had mistaken. He knew that the pair had gone to a house on the southern coast, a house lent by some relation, and that Verrall's people, with the large vagueness of their class, were counting upon a retrieval that would never come. It was, he reflected, a singular privilege of poverty to have nothing whatever to arrange. He had only to go.

IV

The night train to the south was crowded with persons who had evidently decided that the end of the world, if it must arrive, should find them in motion. In Leadford's compartment a stout clergyman, a commercial traveller, and a young woman with a hatbox sustained, between Rugby and the river, a conversation of such alternating dread and levity that Leadford, in his corner, seemed to be listening to the Age itself talking in its sleep.

"They tell us the tail will touch us Wednesday," said the traveller.

"And the Germans," said the clergyman, "will touch us rather sooner."

There was laughter, uneasy and brief. The young woman looked out at the dark fields, where here and there a farm-lamp burned with the small obstinacy of things that have no notion of being abolished. Leadford, whose hand lay closed in his pocket, felt toward these strangers an odd, remote tenderness, as toward people encountered in a dream who do not know they are in one. He thought: they are all waiting, and I alone am not, for I have something to do. And the thought, which should have given him strength, left him instead with a curious faintness, as though the one thing he had to do were precisely the thing the whole night was conspiring to make unimportant.

V

The house, when he found it at noon of the next day, stood back from a white road, with a lawn running down to a wall, and beyond the wall the pale insistence of the sea. It was a house of shutters and tranquil verandas, and its air of having been designed for happiness struck Leadford as a personal affront, almost an argument. He remained a long time at the gate. The sky had taken on a quality he could not name: a stillness, a faint, greenish thinness, as if the blue had been washed too often. The birds had stopped. Somewhere a dog began to howl and, finding no response, desisted.

Then he saw her. She came out upon the veranda alone, in a white dress, and stood with one hand raised to her eyes, looking at the heavens; and the gesture, so entirely hers, so entirely unaware of him, delivered him a blow beyond any he had prepared for. She was smaller than he remembered. She was, he saw with a pang that had in it neither triumph nor pity, merely frightened, a girl on a veranda in a strange light, wondering, as he was, what was going to happen.

He drew the revolver out. He observed, with detachment, that his hand was perfectly steady.

VI

It was then that the air changed. He afterward tried to say how, and could say only that it had become visible to him as a medium, as if he had passed all his life at the bottom of a clear sea and had at last been shown the surface. A green light, delicate, universal, without source, descended upon the lawn, the wall, the veranda, and the girl; and with it came a taste, a sweetness, an odd lightness in the lungs, as of breath drawn for the first time. Nettie turned her face toward him, and he saw her smile, not at him, nor at anything, but with the vague, grave amazement of one who has been spoken to.

He meant to say something. He meant, even then, to raise the pistol. But a great, soft weariness, not unpleasant, took possession of his limbs, and the gate, the road, the very noon seemed to bow slowly toward him, like courteous figures yielding a passage. The weapon slipped from his fingers. He had time to think, with perfect clearness, "How very much I have misunderstood," and then the green deepened into a silence that had no bottom, and he went down into it as one goes down into sleep.

VII

He woke to a morning of such purity that for some moments he took it for the recollection of a morning, a thing remembered from childhood and gently restored. He lay on the grass beside the gate, with the dew upon his sleeves; and above him the sky, rinsed and tender, was blue as he had never known the sky to be blue. The air was sweet. The sea beyond the wall spoke in a low and companionable voice.

He sat up slowly. The revolver lay a yard away in the grass, and he regarded it as one regards a tool belonging to some former and rather stupid trade. It seemed incredible to him that he had carried it; more incredible still that he had carried, with it, the whole elaborate, absurd structure of his injury. The anger had not been defeated. It had simply gone, as a fever goes, leaving the mind lucid and a little ashamed, and very much alive.

Then he heard steps on the gravel, and rose, and saw them coming together from the house: Nettie, and the young man, Verrall, bareheaded, his face grave and altered, as though he, too, had been washed. They stopped before him. No one spoke at once; and Leadford perceived that there was nothing to be explained, that each of them had been brought, by the same strange night, to the same bright and trembling threshold. Verrall put out his hand. Leadford took it. And Nettie, looking from one to the other with eyes that were full of tears and of something far larger than tears, said only, "Isn't it wonderful? Oh, isn't it all quite, quite different?"

"Yes," said Leadford, and found that he was smiling. "It is. It is entirely different."

Over the white road, in the dazzle of that first new morning, the world lay open before them, and not one of the three was afraid.
