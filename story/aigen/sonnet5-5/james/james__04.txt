The evening on which William Leadford learned that Nettie had gone was, by every outward sign, an evening like the others that Staffordshire had lately afforded him: the kilns of the Five Towns lay under their usual sulky canopy, the lamps along the Bursley road had been lit with their usual air of doing it under protest, and in the kitchen of the little house in Cheapside the kettle was singing to no one. It was the note, propped against the tea-caddy, that made the difference; and it made it, he afterwards thought, less by what it said than by the extreme neatness of its saying it. Nettie had always written a beautiful hand. She wrote, now, that she was sorry, truly sorry, and that he would in time understand; and Leadford, who understood already everything except the one thing, stood holding the paper as a man holds some small object he has been handed in a crowd and cannot think where to put down.

He had not, to be just, been blind. There had been, for a month and more, the person of Mr. Verrall—Verrall of the new motor, Verrall of the good coat, Verrall who owned, or whose father owned, a share in the very clay that Leadford daily turned. Young Verrall had appeared at the end of the lane as a sort of weather; one had remarked it, and one had gone on. That, precisely, was what Leadford now could not pardon: that he had gone on. He had gone on, with his wages and his little patient plans, as if a girl were a thing one could safely leave at a window.

It had been, in its way, a beautiful courtship, and that was the worst of it—that Leadford, thinking it over, could not find in it a single ugliness. Nettie had been, at seventeen, so exquisitely a creature of the better thing; she had held her small head with such an air of waiting for some future to arrive and be introduced; and he, whose whole fortune was a capacity for being patient, had mistaken her waiting for a sort of fidelity. He saw now that she had merely been waiting for the right carriage. Verrall's motor, throbbing outside her father's door, had been, to a nature so finely tuned to the prospect, not a vulgarity but a form of music.

He remembered, too, with the cruel exactness that sorrow lends the memory, certain small occasions: Nettie looking at his hands, once, in the lamplight, and then looking away; Nettie saying that it was so dreadfully far to anywhere from Cheapside. He had taken these for the idle weather of a young mind. They had been, he now perceived, the whole climate.

It was Parload who first spoke to him of the comet—Parload, his old schoolfellow, who had gone in, God knew how, for astronomy, and who kept a kind of vigil at a small observatory above the town. Leadford had climbed to him because there was nowhere else on earth he could bear to go. And Parload, with his thin neck and his stained fingers, had pointed him to a faint smear in the northern dark, saying that it was growing, that the learned were agreed it would pass very near, and that no one, in truth, knew what it would do.

"It may do nothing," said Parload.

"It may do a great deal," said Leadford, who was thinking of something else; and he looked up at the pale intruder with an obscure sense of kinship, as at a thing that also had been sent out of its course.

Below them the country lay dark, and the newspapers, which Parload kept in heaps, were full of Europe. There was a quarrel in the Balkans, a quarrel about a harbour, a quarrel of the great Powers conducted in the language of men who have already decided; and it came to Leadford, in the thin cold air of the hilltop, that the whole continent was moving with the same blind purpose as himself—toward an injury it called honour. He felt that he might have been the author of the entire situation.

He bought the revolver in Hanley, of a person who asked no questions and appeared, indeed, to regard the absence of them as a service rendered. It was a small thing, but it had the weight, in the pocket, of a conversation. Leadford did not say to himself that he meant to use it; he said only that he meant to see; and it is the peculiarity of such resolutions that they are most firmly held by those who do not name them.

He had learned, by the usual indirect means, where the pair had taken refuge: a house, it was reported, on the southern slope of the country, lent by some relation of Verrall's, where for the moment the world was kept at the distance of a garden wall. To reach it he must cross half of England; and he crossed it, through a succession of third-class carriages, in a state of lucid fever, watching the fields slide by like the pages of a book whose language he had forgotten.

The country, as he went, was strange. Men stood in the stations reading the sheets; a regiment, at one junction, sat on its kit with its faces turned toward some stupendous elsewhere; and everywhere, at the edge of every conversation, there hung that same sense of a thing about to be decided. The war, it was said, would be declared by the end of the week. The comet, it was said, would be overhead by the end of the month. Leadford, among them, felt almost respectable: his private catastrophe had at last found its public.

It was toward the close of a windless afternoon that he came up the long hill and saw the house. And it was then, precisely, that he noticed the light. It had not the colour of any light he knew; it lay on the hedgerows and on the pale road with a sort of greenish tenderness, as if the day were being seen through the glass of a very old bottle. He looked up. The comet, vast now, had taken the north; and out of it, or from the sky about it, there seemed to descend something that was not exactly a radiance and not exactly a vapour, but that touched the face like a question.

He had meant to feel, at the gate, a great hardness. What he felt instead was an extraordinary and quite unreasonable lightness of breath, as though some weight he had carried for years had been quietly lifted by an unseen hand and he had been, for politeness, left to notice the fact. He walked up the path. He saw, through the open window, Nettie at a table—her head bent, her hand arrested over some small work—and beyond her Verrall, standing, as it were, in the attitude of a man who has been interrupted in a thought.

They had both turned, and neither spoke; and Leadford discovered with a kind of wonder that the pistol was in his hand, and that it was, of all objects in the visible world, the most absurd. He regarded it as one regards the relic of a former self. It seemed to him a rather vulgar toy, the property of a man he had known slightly, and on whose behalf he could now feel only a fastidious regret.

"I came," he said at last, and his voice astonished him by its gentleness, "to do you some harm. I find I can't remember why."

Nettie rose. She was very pale, and the green light lay on her hair; and what he saw in her face was not the fear he had rehearsed, nor yet the scorn, but something that resembled his own surprise—the look of a person who finds that a door she has always taken for a wall has simply opened. Verrall, behind her, said something, a broken and quite unremarkable word; and it came to Leadford that Verrall too was altered, that the proud fine fellow of the motor was merely a young man, frightened and rather fine, and that he, Leadford, had been jealous of a figure of his own manufacture.

He laid the revolver on the sill. It made a small plain sound.

"It is all over, you know," he said—and he was not certain whether he meant the quarrel, or the war, or the long meanness of the world they had been born into. "I don't think any of us need go on with it."

Outside, the strange air moved among the roses; and far off, on the hill road, he heard a man begin, very unaccountably, to laugh—not in mockery, but as one laughs who has waked from a bad dream into the broad, ridiculous daylight of his own forgiveness. Nettie put out her hand. He took it; and the three of them stood there, in the green and gentle dusk, like persons newly introduced, in a house whose walls had somehow grown thin, looking out upon a country that seemed, for the first time in their experience, to be quite willing to begin again.
