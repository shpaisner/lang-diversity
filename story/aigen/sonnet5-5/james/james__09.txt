The evening on which William Leadford learned that Nettie had gone was, he afterwards reflected, an evening of singular and insolent beauty, as if the very sky had resolved to make his private ruin look a little shabby. He had come up from the works at Clayton by the ash-path behind the pit-banks, and the chimneys of the Five Towns stood against a yellowing west like the pipes of some enormous and sooty organ. On the table in his mother's kitchen lay a note, and beside it, in an attitude of mournful tact, his mother herself. The note was brief. It said that Nettie was sorry, that she was not, after all, what he had supposed, and that she had gone with Mr. Verrall, who would be kind to her; and he noticed, with the odd lucidity that attends a blow, that she had spelled "supposed" with a single p.

He read it twice, and the room, with its fender and its clock and its geranium, went on in its accustomed course, offering no sign that the universe had been touched. That was the horror of it. He had expected, somehow, a fracture; he found only the kettle.

"She'll not be back, lad," said his mother.

"No," he said, and heard his own voice as one hears a stranger's through a wall.

He went out again, without his supper, into that large and complicated dusk.

II

It was Parload who found him, an hour later, on the stile above the brickfields, and Parload, being a person of one idea, could not at once be diverted from it. He was a thin, preoccupied young man who kept accounts by day and by night kept an astronomer's vigil, and he had come, as it were, to carry the heavens under his arm.

"You've not looked," he said, "you've not looked at it these three nights."

"At what?"

"Why, at it." And Parload's finger, hardly less rigid than a telescope, pointed to the northeast, where, low above the smoke, hung a small and curiously greenish blur, a thing without definite edge, like a thumbprint on the glass of the night. "It is coming toward us. They say it will be the brightest in a century."

Leadford looked, because to look was easier than to speak. He saw the faint stranger, and felt toward it no wonder whatever, only a dull resentment that anything should presume to be coming when everything of consequence had gone.

"Nettie's gone off," he said at last. "With Verrall."

Parload's face passed through several phases of embarrassment. "I'm sorry," he said; and then, because honesty was his nature, "It may be that she was never quite—" He stopped.

"Never quite what?"

"Never quite for you, Will."

III

There followed some days in which Leadford moved about the world as a man moves in a house whose furniture has been secretly changed. He worked; he ate; he heard, in the dinner-hour, the talk of the men, which had lately acquired a deeper and more ominous note. The newspapers spoke of Germany, and of ships, and of an Eastern question that seemed to lie across the continent like a sleeper no one dared to wake. The foremen said there would be war by the spring; the men said it would be good for trade; and a pale socialist from Hanley said it would be good for nothing but the grave-diggers, and was told to hold his noise.

Leadford listened to these voices without belonging to them. It struck him, in his private darkness, as a kind of grim congruity that the large world should be preparing its quarrel at the very moment of his own; yet the congruity brought no consolation. He had no feeling for the nations. His whole sense of injury had narrowed to a single figure, whom he had seen but twice: a young man in good grey clothes, leaning on the rail of a motor-car, with the air of one to whom the future had been handed over at birth, wrapped, like a parcel, in silver paper.

He found that he could not think of Nettie at all. He thought, with a patience that alarmed him, of Verrall.

IV

It was a matter of two or three discreet inquiries, and a larger one of Nettie's elder sister, a woman with a flat and unforgiving mouth, who gave the address as one gives a verdict. They were at a house near Sevenoaks, lent by the Verralls' relations, where the young man was supposed to be reading for something. "Reading," said the sister, and her mouth said the rest.

That night Leadford sat a long while with a small, ugly pistol which he had purchased years ago from a sailor for a purpose he had forgotten, and which now lay in his palm as a fact lies in the mind, neither welcome nor to be dismissed. He did not tell himself that he meant to use it. He told himself, with the elaborate honesty of the wounded, that he meant to see; and in that word, which seemed so innocent, he failed to detect the whole dark labour of his intention.

The comet by now was a thing not to be ignored. It stood above the roofs, a pale and widening menace, throwing across the slates a light that was neither moon's nor lamp's, but something thinner and more searching. Even his mother had remarked it.

"It'll be the end of us," she said, with a decent economy of alarm.

"It'll not trouble us," he answered, and put the pistol in his pocket.

V

He reached Sevenoaks on the afternoon of the next day, and walked out by lanes whose softness appalled him, so charged were they with an order and a leisure in which he had never had a share. The hedges stood trim; the very cows had a look of being privately owned. It was the south of England, and it regarded him, in his Staffordshire boots, with the mild disdain of a drawing-room.

The house, when it appeared, was low and white among cedars, and had the air of having been loved by several generations without having been much disturbed by any. He stood a long time at the gate. The dusk gathered; and with it, enormous now and green as the light under deep water, the comet rose above the cedars and laid on the lawn a pallor that made each blade distinct and unreal.

Then he saw her. She was at the open window of a lighted room, in a gown he did not know, her head a little bent over some small occupation of the hands; and what struck him, with a pang more cruel than any he had prepared, was that she looked not guilty, not triumphant, but merely at home. She had passed, in a fortnight, into a condition for which he had no name and she, plainly, no apology.

He went up the drive. He was aware of the pistol as of a second, heavier heart.

VI

Verrall received him in the hall. He had, Leadford saw, expected nothing and was therefore not at all disconcerted; he stood in the lamplight with his hands in his pockets and a look of civil, inquiring good-humour.

"Ah," he said. "You'll be Leadford. Nettie said you might come. Won't you step in?"

It was the "step in" that undid him. He had come prepared for flight, for fury, for a pale and stammering shame; he had not come prepared for hospitality. The pistol, in his pocket, grew absurd.

"I've come," he said, and his voice cracked on the words, "to say what you are."

"Have you?" said Verrall gently. "Then do say it. But I think you'll find it's rather a cold evening for it." And as if by a sort of exquisite malice he added: "Have you seen the comet? Extraordinary, isn't it? One feels, somehow, that nothing's quite going to matter."

Nettie had come to the door of the inner room. She did not speak; she looked at Leadford with a large, grave, entirely unembarrassed compassion, and it was this, more than any insolence, which he could not endure. She pitied him. He had come to be either terrible or tragic, and she had made him merely sad.

His hand closed on the pistol. He drew it out, and it shook, and the three of them regarded it as people regard an unexpected guest.

VII

"Put it down, Will," said Nettie quietly.

He did not put it down. He stood in that low, rich, lamplit hall, with the great green pallor pressing at the window behind him, and understood, with a sudden and most strange clarity, that he was at the extreme point of something: that a step in one direction would carry him into a deed that no later wisdom could repair, and that a step in the other would carry him into a smallness that he could never forgive. Between these two he hung, and it seemed to him that the whole house, the cedars, the lawn, the quiet villages and the sleeping nations beyond them, hung with him, in a suspension without precedent.

And in that suspension something altered in the light. It was not the lamp. The window behind him had turned, of a sudden, to a sheet of luminous and delicate green, a green like the first leaf of spring seen through a glass; and the lamp-flame, he fancied, grew pale in it, and the faces of Verrall and of Nettie took on a strange, translucent gravity, as if they, too, were being asked a question.

"What's that?" said Verrall, and for the first time his voice had no ease in it.

Nobody answered. Leadford felt the pistol grow heavy in his fingers, heavier than iron, heavier than anything he had ever carried; and the air in the hall, so faintly sweet and strange, seemed to enter him with each breath, and to touch, one by one, the hard places of his rage, as a hand touches the shut doors of a house.
