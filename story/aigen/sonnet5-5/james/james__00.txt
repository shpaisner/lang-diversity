He had been, for the better part of three years, a person to whom nothing could with any decency be said to have happened, and it was perhaps for this reason that when the thing did happen, it presented itself to William Leadford less as a blow than as a vast and humiliating correction. Nettie had gone. She had gone on a Tuesday, between his dinner-hour and his return to the dim office in Hanley where he copied the designs of other men's teapots, and she had left behind her, propped against the clock on her mother's mantelpiece, a note so brief, so scrupulously unadorned, that he read it twice for the sake of the single word that might yet redeem it. There was no such word. There was only the name of Verrall, written large and almost kindly, as one writes the name of a person who is to be thanked. He stood in the small hot kitchen, a poor young man in a collar one size too liberal, and felt the whole narrow architecture of his hopes fold inward, quietly, like a wet umbrella.

It was Parload who first drew his attention to the sky, and Parload, as Leadford had long conceded, was a person for whom the sky was a form of private property. They climbed together that evening to the rough ground above Clayton, where the furnaces below sent up their sullen coronals, and Parload, pointing with a stubby finger toward a region that to Leadford appeared merely vacant, spoke of the comet. It was new, it was swift, and it was, in the opinion of persons who knew, composed of a gas not before encountered in the spectroscope. "It will come very near," said Parload, with the relish of one announcing a distinguished guest. Leadford looked where he was bidden and saw, or imagined he saw, a faint and slightly smudged pallor, like the print of a thumb upon a darkened pane. He was courteous, he was even attentive; but it was borne in upon him, with an irony he felt too fatigued to resent, that a universe in which such objects wandered might very well be indifferent to a clerk in Hanley whose young woman had taken the train.

The newspapers, in those weeks, spoke in a dialect that he had once found impressive. Germany, it appeared, was uneasy; France was watchful; there were ships, and a certain conversation in a certain chancellery that had been, in the phrase of the journals, "far from reassuring." In the public-house where he now took, with a kind of dogged experimental vice, his evening glass, men leaned upon the counter and gave their several opinions as to the likelihood of a war. Leadford listened without participating. He could not, he found, make the thing real; it was too large, it had no door at which one might knock. What was real was a small, definite, intolerable picture: a lamp-lit room somewhere, a table laid for two, and Nettie's head, with its familiar tilt, bent in attention toward a face he had seen only twice, and which had seemed to him on both occasions the face of a man who had never been obliged to count anything. Europe might drift if it liked. It was his own drifting that he watched, and it was drifting, with a horrid and increasing velocity, toward a single resolve.

The resolve took, as such resolves will, a material form. He came upon it in a pawnbroker's window in Burslem: a revolver, small, dark, a little ashamed of itself among the flutes and the wedding-rings, and ticketed at a sum which he possessed, to the last shilling, because he had been saving it for a ring of his own. He observed that the transaction involved remarkably little ceremony. The pawnbroker, a mild man with a cold in his head, asked no question that Leadford had not already answered to himself, and wrapped the object in brown paper with the tenderness one bestows on a pair of boots. Walking home, the parcel against his ribs, Leadford was visited by an odd and not disagreeable lucidity. He knew where they were. Nettie's sister, wretched and tearful and reluctant, had let fall the name of a house in the south, a house in a park, belonging to the elder Verrall; and there, it seemed, in a borrowed splendour, the pair awaited whatever it is that such pairs await. He would go. He did not ask himself for what, precisely; the going was the whole of the argument.

The train carried him through a country that seemed, as the afternoon declined, to have been composed expressly for the occasion: meadows of a bruised and brooding green, hedges whose every leaf was edged with a metallic exactitude, and over all a sky of a quality he could not at first name. It was not the sky of ordinary weather. It had an air of attention, of a vast, polite suspense, as though something behind it were waiting to be introduced. Fellow-passengers spoke of the comet in low voices, and one lady, who had evidently not slept, declared that the sun had looked that morning "quite peculiar." Leadford, in his corner, with the parcel upon his knee, regarded these remarks as one regards the conversation of persons in a neighbouring drama. He was struck, however, by a singular fact: that his anger, which in Staffordshire had been a hot and personal fire, seemed in this strange light to acquire a certain cold and ceremonial beauty, as if it too had been enlarged, and were become something that the heavens themselves might be understood to countenance.

He reached the village at dusk and was directed to the house by a boy who stared at him with the frank curiosity of the very young. The park gates stood open. Beyond them an avenue of limes ran up through a gloom that was no longer, strictly, gloom; for the sky had begun to pour down upon the landscape a luminosity of the faintest, most delicate green, so that the great trees, the lawn, the gravel, and at last the long pale front of the house itself seemed things seen through water. A window on the ground floor was lighted, more yellowly, and toward this, with a tread he scarcely recognised as his own, Leadford went. He had imagined the moment a hundred times, and in every version he had been, he now perceived, a figure of larger stature. The reality was a wet lawn, a pair of boots too heavy for the occasion, and, at the sill, a rift in the curtain through which he saw, with a clearness that was almost an insult, a table, a lamp, a plate of fruit, and Nettie.

She was not laughing. That was the thing which, for a long moment, he could not afterward forgive her, or himself, for having seen. She sat with her hands in her lap, looking at nothing, and the face that he had carried with him so many weeks as the face of a triumphant betrayer was only the face of a girl who was tired and a little frightened, and who, in the green hush that lay upon the world, appeared to be listening for something that would not come. Verrall stood by the mantel, not handsome now, not even insolent, merely young, and turning a paper-knife over and over in his fingers. Leadford drew the revolver from its wrapping. It was heavier than he had supposed. He raised it, and there passed over him, from the zenith downward, a stillness so entire that the very leaves ceased their small commerce; the green deepened, grew luminous, grew sweet, and in his nostrils there was a breath like no breath he had known, cool and strange and curiously tender. He thought, with perfect distinctness, "I shall not, after all, be able to do it"; and he thought it without shame, almost with wonder, as one notes the sudden lifting of a long fog. The weapon dipped in his hand. Within the room Nettie lifted her head and turned toward the window, and her lips parted. Then the great quiet closed over him, and he sank down upon the wet grass, gently, like a man who has at last been permitted to sleep.
