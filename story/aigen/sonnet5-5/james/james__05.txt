William Leadford learned the thing last, and learned it, as it were, from the outside, from the face of Nettie's mother at the door in Trafalgar Road, which was composed into such a deliberate and merciful vacancy that he understood before a word was spoken. He had come, as on every second evening for a year, with his collar clean and his hope a little threadbare. The small front room, with its harmonium and its antimacassars, seemed to have drawn itself up to receive him as one receives a person for whom bad news is waiting. The letter lay on the mantel beneath a china shepherdess. Nettie wrote that she was going to be happy, "in a way you would never have let me be, Willie." She wrote that Mr. Verrall, Edward Verrall, of the great house at the edge of the town, who had so lightly crossed her path in the spring, had understood her; and she begged to be thought of kindly. Leadford read it twice. What struck him was not the cruelty, for there was none, but the dreadful gentleness, the finish of it, as of a lesson taken from some better-bred correspondent. He folded the page small. "Thank you," he said to the mother, with a politeness that appalled him; and he went out into a street where the lamps were being lit under a sky that seemed already to be watching him.

It was in those days that the comet, which had been a matter for the learned, became a matter for the street. Leadford, perched at his high desk in the office of the pot-bank, with the ledgers opening their ruled columns before him like so many avenues of futility, heard it discussed by men who had never before lifted their eyes above a roof-line. There was a small green disc, it appeared, grown from a speck to a coin, and a clergyman in Burslem had proved from it the nearness of judgment. The newspapers, however, were occupied with a more earthly menace. There were notes between chancelleries, a German phrase that had been repeated and denied, and a line of reserves said to be mustering beyond a frontier of which no one in Staffordshire could have drawn the shape. Leadford read these things as a man reads who has been struck, with a fixed and abstracted attention, his own grievance enlarging itself until it seemed to him the true centre of the alarm. Let Europe burn, he thought; it could not burn more privately than he. And the green coin hung each night above the chimneys, a little larger, a little more composed, like a creditor who had decided to wait.

The revolver he bought in a back street in Hanley, from a person who asked no question, and he was struck, handling it afterwards in his lodging, by the absence in himself of any corresponding drama. It was merely a heavy and rather ugly object, which he wrapped in a handkerchief and laid in the bottom of his bag, between a clean shirt and a Bible his mother had given him. He had learned, by means that he preferred not to examine, that Verrall had taken Nettie to the south, to a house among the Surrey hills that belonged to some relation. He did not, he found, hate Verrall; he scarcely saw him. He saw instead a kind of lighted window, behind which, in an air of leisure and cultivated phrases, Nettie sat and was understood. It was toward that window that his whole poor nature leaned. The thing he would do there was dim to him. Yet it was settled, as such things are settled, by a process beneath the level of choice, and he gave notice at the office in a single sentence, and watched his employer's astonishment with the vague pity of one already departed.

The journey south had the quality of a long illness. He sat through the dull middle of England, past fields that wore the colour of old coins, and the carriage was full of talk, the war on everyone's lips, the comet in everyone's window. A stout gentleman in the corner declared that the Germans would not dare; a thin one that they would dare very much. A woman near the door wondered whether the green star would make it matter. Leadford contributed nothing. He observed himself as from a distance, a young man of narrow shoulders and no prospects, carrying in a bag the one significant fact of his career, and he was conscious, with a faint astonishment, of how little the world seemed to require of him. In London there were crowds and placards, a hoarse excitement in the lamplit streets. The sky above the river had turned to a strange luminous bottle-green, and it made every face beneath it look like the face of a drowned man. He walked through it without once raising his head. The night train took him out among dark hills, and a boy at a wayside station told him the road.

The house stood high, with a long lawn falling away in front and a line of beeches black against that unnatural sky; and Leadford, coming up through the wet grass toward eleven o'clock, perceived that the window of his imagining was real. It was a long window, open to the warm night, and the lamp within made a picture of the room: books, a bowl of late roses, a piano. At the piano sat Nettie, in a gown he did not know, and behind her, one hand lightly upon the chair, stood Verrall, tall and fair and absurdly young, saying something that made her turn her face up and laugh. It was the laugh that undid him. He had not known that she could laugh so, with so little reserve, as though a door had been thrown wide in her. He stood there with his hand in the bag, and was aware, with a clearness approaching anguish, that he had never once, in a year, made her do it. Yet the clearness did not alter the matter; it only made the matter more intolerable. He drew out the revolver, and the green light of the heavens lay along its barrel like a cold finger.

He did not afterwards remember crossing the terrace. He remembered only that the two faces turned, and that Nettie's, in the instant of recognition, passed through fear into something he had not provided for, which was pity. "Willie," she said. "Oh, Willie, you've come all this way." Verrall moved a step in front of her, with a pale, rather fine, ridiculous gallantry. "You had better give me that," he said. Leadford had begun to answer, to say the speech that, for three days and two hundred miles, he had been composing, when a sound went through the whole night, not a wind but the sense of a wind, a vast soft exhalation from above. The lamp burned strangely; the roses seemed to lean. And over the edge of the hill came a faint thin mist, of a green so delicate that it might have been the mere colour of the dark itself, and it drifted across the lawn, and through the window, and into his breath. He raised the pistol. His arm, however, would not hold it; it was suddenly very heavy, and the room, and the beeches, and the two white faces swam together. He thought, quite distinctly, "How tired I am," and the floor came up to meet him.

What he knew first on waking was the air. It was a quality of air he had no word for, as if, all his life, he had breathed through some thick and dusty stuff, now withdrawn. He lay upon the terrace stones, and the morning above him was of a limpid and tender blue; the beeches stood against it with every leaf separate, and a bird, somewhere, was giving to the silence a perfectly simple account of itself. The revolver lay a yard from his hand. He looked at it for a long time, with a sort of grave bewilderment, as one looks at the toy of a child long since dead. It was absurd; it was even, in some fashion that he could not quite reach, rather touching. Then he sat up, and saw them. Nettie and Verrall were seated on the low wall beyond, hand in hand, like waking children, and their faces, turned toward him, were washed clean of everything but a wondering kindness. He understood that he was not hated, and understood, with a larger shock, that he did not hate. The old anguish was there, but as a thing seen entire, with its small and sufficient causes, and no longer as a sentence. "Nettie," he said, and his voice was strange to him. "Mr. Verrall." There was nothing more to say, and it seemed, for the first time in his life, that nothing more was needed; and far off, over the hills, he thought he heard the world beginning, very quietly, to be different.
