I.

It was Nettie's sister, a thin, harassed girl with a shawl over her head, who gave him the news upon the doorstep, and she gave it with an air of having rehearsed it, as if the fact, to be borne at all, had needed first to be turned over many times in the kitchen. William Leadford stood in the thin Staffordshire drizzle and received it as one receives a parcel for which one has not the means to pay. Nettie had gone. She had gone on the Tuesday, by the afternoon train, and not alone; there was a name, and the name, uttered in a lowered voice, was Verrall's.

He understood her perfectly, and that was the cruelty of it. It was not that the thing was obscure; it was that it had been, for weeks, so fatally clear, and that he had declined to read it. He had seen the young man's motor-car in the lane; he had seen Nettie's face lifted to it, curious and starved and bright. He had put it down to girlhood. Now, in the narrow passage that smelt of damp and of the Reynoldses' supper, he saw that he had put it down to a good many things, all of them comfortable.

"Father says," began the sister, and stopped.

"I can imagine what your father says," said Leadford, and turned away into the wet, where the chimneys of the Potteries stood against a sky the colour of old pewter, and the whole district, with its kilns and its ash and its patient ugliness, seemed to him for the first time an accomplice.

II.

He did not go home; he went, by one of those obscure instincts that govern the wounded, to Parload's. Parload lived above a chandler's, in a room whose chief furniture was a telescope of modest pretensions, and he received Leadford with the abstraction of a man whose real interests lay some millions of miles from the person before him.

"You'll have heard," Leadford said.

"Heard?" Parload turned from the window. "Oh—Nettie. Yes. I'm sorry, Leadford. I am indeed." He said it kindly, but with a kind of absent sincerity, as one acknowledges a distant bereavement, and then, because he could not help it, he added: "You've not looked at it lately, I suppose? It has grown. It has grown remarkably since Sunday."

Leadford had not wished to look. He looked, however, being pushed gently to the eyepiece, and beheld a small, cold, greenish smudge, trailing a faint veil of light, hung in the black as if by an idle hand. It conveyed nothing to him. It was a thing in the sky; and the thing in his breast was larger, nearer, and more entirely his.

"They say it'll come within a certain distance," said Parload. "There's talk of a collision, though that's the newspapers. But the spectrum, Leadford—there's something in the spectrum—"

"I dare say," said Leadford. He stood a moment longer with his eye on the pale indifferent visitor, and thought that it, at least, was going somewhere.

III.

In the days that followed the district talked of two things, and Leadford, who could attend to neither, was nevertheless obliged to hear both. At the works, among the men at the benches, the comet was a joke that had begun to lose its savour; it had been looked at over tea-cans and found, on the whole, unthreatening. But the other matter had a different weight. The papers, spread on the sills at the dinner-hour, were full of Germany, of ultimatums, of fleets, of a certain tone in the Foreign Office that nobody believed and everybody quoted. Men who had never been farther than Stoke discoursed upon the Balkans with an authority that seemed to Leadford at once absurd and appalling.

"It'll come," said an old pot-painter, nodding. "It's coming. You can smell it."

Leadford, who could smell only the clay and his own humiliation, found that these large public disorders ran curiously together with his private one. Europe, too, was being left; Europe, too, was drifting off on some bright treacherous errand, with a swagger of motor-cars and a promise of the south, and there would be, at the end of it, somebody standing in the rain with his hat in his hand. He felt the grotesque kinship acutely, and was ashamed of it, as of a pretension. What was his small defeat, in the scale of the continent's? And yet it was the only scale on which he had ever been taught to feel.

IV.

The revolver he procured on the Saturday, from a man in Hanley with whom he had once had a trifling transaction, and the procuring of it was so easy, so wholly without the thunder one might have expected, that it struck him as the first intimation of how little the world resisted the determined. He carried it home wrapped in the Sentinel. In his lodging, under the gas, he unwrapped it, and it lay on the oilcloth with a small dull plainness, like a tool whose purpose he had chosen not to discuss with himself.

He had discovered, by inquiry and by an effort of cunning that surprised him, where they were: a house on the Kentish coast, taken for the season, borrowed, no doubt, from the large fortune of the Verralls. He pictured it with a precision he resented—white, with a lawn, with a sea beyond the lawn—and pictured Nettie upon the lawn, in a dress he had not paid for. The picture was not, he noted, one that moved him toward pity; it moved him toward the arithmetic of the thing, the clean subtraction. One man too many in the world, and the sum came right.

He sat a long while without moving. Outside, the night had gone oddly bright, a dull rose-coloured light lying along the roofs, and he did not go to the window to learn the cause. He knew the cause. It was exactly as if the heavens themselves had taken an interest, and he would not give them the satisfaction of being looked at.

V.

The train that bore him south on the Sunday was nearly empty, and the carriage, with its stale cushions and its smeared glass, became for the hours of the journey a kind of confessional in which he said nothing and was heard throughout. The country slid by, and he saw it as one sees things in fever: hedges, spires, a farmer with a cart, each wearing an excess of distinctness. Somewhere beyond Rugby he became aware that the light was wrong. It was the afternoon, and the sun was behind cloud, and yet the whole land lay under a faint unaccountable greenness, as if it were seen through water.

At a junction a porter said to another, "Funny weather," and the other, glancing up, said nothing at all, which was more than the remark deserved and less than the sky did. People stood about the platform with their faces lifted. Leadford did not lift his. He sat with the parcel on his knee and held, in his mind, the one fixed image, as a man in a flood holds to a post; but the post, he found, was not quite so firm as it had been in Staffordshire. Something had got into the air, a quality of suspension, a hush such as precedes speech in a large room. He told himself that it was nerves. He told himself, indeed, a great many things, in the manner of one who has begun to suspect that he is no longer the principal speaker.

VI.

He reached the place toward dusk, on foot, having left the little station behind him and followed a lane that ran between hedges to the sea. The house stood as he had imagined it, and that was the first strangeness: that the mind's picture should be so cheaply confirmed. White, square, a lawn, a low wall, a gate; beyond, the grey enormous breathing of the Channel; and above all, filling the whole upper air, the comet.

He had not understood. He had seen it through Parload's glass, a smudge; he had read of it as of a rumour. It was now a presence. It hung in the east like the lifted lid of some vast green eye, and the sea beneath it had taken on a colour that belonged to no known water, and the lawn, the wall, his own hand upon the gate, were all bathed in a light that was neither day nor any recognisable night. It was very still. No bird spoke. Far off, behind the house, a dog began to whine, and ceased.

Leadford drew the revolver out and held it, and it seemed to him a small, rather ridiculous object, an offering brought to a ceremony of which he had mistaken the nature. He thought of Nettie behind one of those lit windows, and found, with a distinctness that frightened him, that he could not at the moment recall the exact shape of her face. He thought of Verrall, and the name had become a mere sound. What remained was the gate under his fingers and the vast green silence pressing, softly, inexorably, upon the whole of the world; and he stood there, with his grievance still raised like a lamp, waiting for the sky to tell him what he was.
