I set down these words in a season of strange tranquillity, yet I cannot recall the black years before the Change without a shudder, as one recalls a fever-dream of some blind and noisome cavern. I was William Leadford, born under the smoke-clotted skies of the Staffordshire Potteries, where the kilns squatted like crouching idols and the soot lay upon the hedgerows like a leprous blight. My father was dead, my wages were pitiful, and my mind, starved of beauty, had grown a sullen, narrow thing, gnawing always upon its own small miseries.

There was but one light in that grey existence, and her name was Nettie. She was slight and quick, with hair of a dark warm brown and eyes that seemed to hold some promise of a world beyond the chimneys. For her I toiled, and for her I dreamed of a clerkship and a little house with a garden. Of the cosmos I knew nothing, nor cared.

Yet even then a new thing hung in the heavens. The newspapers spoke of it, and men in taprooms laughed uneasily at it: a wandering star, a comet, first a mere smudge among the telescopes, then a pale and growing menace. I saw it myself on certain clear nights above the furnace-glare, a small greenish blot upon the dark, like the opened eye of something that had waited unimaginable aeons to look upon us.

I did not heed it. I looked only toward Nettie, and toward the thing that was already, unseen, devouring me.

The thing had a name, and the name was Verrall. He was the son of a house above our station, a languid, handsome youth in good tweeds, who walked in the lanes with the careless ease of one to whom the world has always yielded. I saw him first beside Nettie at a gate near the Bursley road, and in that instant I knew, with the cold certainty of the damned, that I was supplanted.

She had been dazzled, I think, by the glitter of a life she could never otherwise have touched. I do not wholly blame her now, though then I burned to. Her letters grew brief, her smiles distracted, her hours unaccountable. I pleaded; I raged; I threatened. She answered me with a pitying coolness that was worse than hatred.

Then came the night I shall never forget. I went to her lodging and found it empty, the landlady staring at me with sly and gloating pity. Nettie was gone. She had fled by the evening train, and with her had gone Verrall, to London, to the Continent, to some bright place where I could not follow.

I stood in that dismal street while the rain fell greasily upon the cobbles, and above the broken roofs the comet glared, larger now, and sickly green. It seemed to regard my anguish with an alien and unblinking patience. Something cold and monstrous stirred within me then, and I resolved that I would find them, though I should wade through hell to do it.

In those days the whole of Europe seemed to lean upon the edge of an abyss. The newspapers I bought with my last coppers were thick with ominous names: ultimatums, mobilisations, fleets gathering in grey northern waters. Great powers glared at one another across their frontiers like starved beasts across a carcass. Men spoke of war with a dreadful, eager fatalism, as though some vast and blind intelligence were drawing the nations toward their doom, and they went willingly.

I felt this madness as a kinship. My own bitterness was but the lesser echo of the greater, and I found a sombre comfort in the thought that all the world was rotting as I was.

I sold my few possessions. From a pawnbroker in a stinking alley I bought a revolver, a small, heavy, oily thing, and I carried it against my ribs as one carries a malignant secret. In my fevered brain a picture shaped itself, fixed and ghastly: Verrall's handsome face, Nettie's pale, upturned one, and the flash and crack that should end them both, and perhaps myself.

The comet by now dominated the night. Astronomers wrote of its tail, its strange spectrum, the curious bands of green that betrayed some unknown gas. Few cared. The world was too busy preparing to murder itself to mark the lantern swinging toward it out of the void.

I traced them, by rumour and by stealth, to a house upon a lonely heath beyond the town. The journey was a nightmare of cold trains and colder roads. The nights now were never wholly dark. The comet filled a quarter of the sky, a vast and writhing banner of unholy emerald, and under its light the fields and hedges took on the aspect of a drowned landscape seen through deep and poisoned water.

Men whispered of omens. I met a carter who crossed himself and would not speak. I met soldiers upon the road, the first harvest of the gathering war, singing in thin, defiant voices that died away as they looked upward. Even the dogs crept belly-down along the walls, whimpering at the heavens.

Yet I felt no fear, only a hard and narrowing purpose. The pistol was warm against my side. I walked through the unearthly twilight like a man already half dead, and the green radiance lay upon my hands and made them corpse-coloured.

On the last evening the sky changed. A fine pale mist seemed to gather at the horizon, and the stars behind it took on a faint, tremulous shimmer. The air grew curiously sweet, and utterly still. No wind stirred. No bird called. The very silence had a quality of vast and attentive listening, as though the Earth itself held its breath.

I saw the house ahead, its windows yellow, and I quickened my steps.

I never reached the door. Even as I lifted my hand toward the gate-latch, the sky above the heath seemed to open. A greenish vapour, luminous and delicate beyond description, came pouring down upon the world like a slow and silent tide. I saw it settle upon the hedges and turn them to ghostly lace. I saw it drift across the road toward me, shining with a cold and living light.

I should have fled. Instead I stood rooted, gazing, and in that moment I felt the whole bulk of the universe press upon my little soul, vast and indifferent, and it seemed to me that I glimpsed gulfs beyond all human naming. I raised the pistol. My hand trembled. Then the vapour closed about me.

It had a faint sweetness, like crushed violets upon an unfamiliar wind. A great drowsiness fell upon my limbs. Through the thickening luminous haze I saw the lamplit windows swim and blur, and somewhere beyond them a single cry went up, thin and astonished, and was cut short. The gate swung past my fingers. The ground rose gently to meet me.

My last thought was not of Nettie, nor of Verrall, but a wild and wordless amazement that the end of all things should come so softly. Then the green tide rose above my eyes, and the sound of my own breathing faded into a silence deeper than any grave, and I knew no more.

I woke to a light such as I had never seen. The sky was a clear, rinsing blue, washed of every stain, and the air that poured into my lungs was cool and sweet as spring water. For a long while I lay upon the grass of the heath, unable to rise, wondering at my own stillness. The fever that had gnawed me for months was gone. In its place was a vast, clear calm, as of a mind long clouded and now at last unveiled.

Around me, others lay sleeping or slowly stirring: soldiers fallen in their ranks, a tramp curled beneath a hedge, a woman slumped upon a doorstep. Their faces, in the new morning, were strangely smoothed and gentle. When they woke they looked at one another with a dawning, trembling wonder, and no man struck another.

The revolver lay in the grass beside me. I looked at it, and the thing appeared suddenly to my understanding as it truly was: a small and ugly implement of a madness that had lifted. I could not even recall the full shape of my hatred, only its dull outline, like a distant, shabby ruin seen across a broad and peaceful plain.

I rose. The gate stood open, and in the doorway of that lonely house I saw them, Nettie and Verrall, pale, bewildered, stripped of every petty pride. They looked at me, and I at them, and in that long moment the old poison drained out of the world forever.

I laid the pistol upon the ground and went forward with open hands. Above us, the cosmic visitor had passed, leaving behind its subtle gift, and for the first time in the history of our race the Earth lay clean and wakeful beneath a sky that held no dread.
