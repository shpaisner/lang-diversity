I have set down this account many times in my mind, and each time the words have proved too small for the thing. I shall try once more, plainly, as a man of the Five Towns ought to speak.

I was born in the Potteries of Staffordshire, under the smoke of a thousand bottle-kilns that squatted over the clay country like the stunted idols of some forgotten and sooty faith. I was a clerk to a pot-merchant, and before that a lad in the sheds, and I knew no splendour save what I carried in my heart for Nettie Stuart. She was slight and quick, with grey eyes that looked past me toward things I could not give her. I was poor. I understood even then that poverty is a kind of blindness. It sees only the next shilling, and it cannot see the soul that is slipping away beside it.

It was in those months that I first heard of the comet. A pale smudge, the papers said, found by some astronomer in the outer dark. I paid it no heed. What was a wandering blur in the abyss to a man whose abyss was the pay-packet?

I remember now that it was already coming.

She went in the autumn. Verrall had come among us, a young man of the great houses, smooth of cheek and softly spoken, with the ease of one for whom doors have always opened. He had motors and ancestors, and he looked upon Nettie as one looks upon a rare glaze in a shop window. I saw them together twice. I told myself it was nothing, and I felt the dread settle in me like cold slip in a vat.

Then came the morning when her mother's door stood open and the house was hollow. A note lay upon the table in a hand I knew. It said that she was sorry, that she could not live as her mother had lived, that she was going to something larger.

I read it standing, and in that moment something in me hardened and turned black. I walked out into the grey street. Above the chimneys, in the paling dawn, hung a small greenish thing that had not been there before, or that I had not troubled to see. It was faint and cold, like the eye of a creature watching from beyond a pane of glass.

Through the winter it grew. Men stood in the streets at evening with their faces lifted to it, and the sight was a strange one: ten thousand grimy countenances turned upward, each bathed in a faint and sickly emerald. The astronomers wrote of tails and of gases, and the pulpits thundered, and the music-halls made jokes. Yet beneath all the noise ran a note of uneasiness, a vibration, as of some vast thing turning over in its sleep.

Nor was the comet the only thing that gathered in the sky. Across the sea the great powers bristled. Newspapers spoke of ultimatums and of fleets, and I read of frontiers and of concessions with the sullen, absent interest of a man whose own private war has already begun. The world, I thought, was going mad together, and I felt a bitter kinship with its madness. There were those who spoke of the end of all things. I wished it upon the world gladly, for I wished it upon Verrall.

I bought a revolver from a pawnbroker in Hanley. I did not ask myself what I meant to do with it. I only knew that the heavens were rotting above me and that I wished to settle one small account before the reckoning of the greater one.

War was declared in the spring, and the sky was by then the dominant fact of existence. The comet was a vast, sickly lamp, its head a green abomination, its streaming tail curved across the heavens like the plume of some cyclopean and indifferent god. I think we all felt that we had stopped being the masters of a world and become the inhabitants of a speck.

I had gone south after them, tracking Verrall's name from rumour to rumour, until I came at last to the country where his family's great house stood among black and silent trees. The roads were choked with men in uniform, and the trains went by with faces at every window, blank and hungry. Of the war I recall only fragments, shouted orders and trampling feet and a vast, unreal excitement.

I walked at night, and the unholy light made the hedgerows look drowned. Birds had ceased to sing. The air itself grew curiously thin and bright, with a faint taste of something metallic and sweet upon the tongue. Somewhere far to the east there was a mutter of guns, and it seemed to me no more than the grumbling of a dream. The revolver lay heavy against my ribs. I thought of Nettie's grey eyes, and I thought that I should look into them once, and then perhaps into nothing at all.

I reached the house in the last hour of that night. It stood against the green sky, tall and cold, its windows burning with a light that was not lamplight. I saw them then through the glass of a long terrace window: Verrall and Nettie, standing close, with their faces upturned toward the heavens like all the rest of us.

I raised the revolver, and my hand shook. A strange vapour was creeping across the lawn, a faint green mist that rolled over the grass without a sound and parted about the stone urns like water. It was upon my boots, and I breathed it.

The sensation was not pain. It was a profound loosening, as though a clenched thing in my skull had at last been permitted to open. The stars wheeled. The ground rose soft against my cheek. As my sight went dim I saw the sky above me no longer green but a deep and burning gold, and I understood, with a terror that was almost joy, that the Thing had come down upon the Earth and that every living soul was falling with me into the same vast and silent sleep.

I woke in clear light with dew upon my face.

For a long while I lay without moving, for I had never breathed such air. It was keen and sweet, and it passed into me like cool water into a parched mouth. The sky above was a pure, unstained blue, and the green abomination was gone, leaving only a tender radiance. I sat up. The revolver lay in the grass, and I looked at it as one looks at a dead insect, with wonder that it had ever seemed to matter.

Then I understood that I had been changed, and that all the world had been changed with me. The black knot of hate that had been my very self was gone. In its place was a vast, clear, trembling sorrow, and beyond the sorrow a light. I saw the long terrace and the figures upon it, and they were waking too. Verrall rose slowly, and in his face was no arrogance, only a naked bewilderment. Nettie lifted herself beside him, and her eyes met mine across the lawn.

I had come to kill them. I felt only a pity so huge that it hurt. We were all of us small things who had been tormented by little walls, by money, by ownership, by fear, and the walls were suddenly gone.

Far off, across the fields, the soldiers were rising among their fallen guns, and they were looking at one another and at the sky. Some wept. Some laughed. None of them lifted a rifle. I think in that hour I knew the old world was dead, and that something had touched us from the abyss that cared nothing for our nations, yet had left us, unaccountably, free.

I stood up, and I walked toward them with empty hands.
