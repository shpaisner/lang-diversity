I.

I was born in the Potteries, in that blackened corner of Staffordshire where the bottle-kilns rise like the stunted altars of some forgotten and sullen cult, and where the sky has not been seen clean in a hundred years. I set this down plainly because those who come after must understand what manner of creature I was before the Change. I was a clerk, poor and ill-fed, with a mind as sooty as the streets that bred it, and I nursed a bitterness I mistook for philosophy.

I loved Nettie Stanton. I use the word with shame, for what I felt was less love than a hungry, clutching claim, as of a drowning man upon a spar. She was slender and quick, with grey eyes that seemed to look past the grime of our lives toward some brighter country. I believed that country was one I might lead her to. I was wrong in that, as in nearly everything.

Often we walked together on the slag-heaps above the town, in the dusk, while the furnaces opened their red mouths below us. She spoke of fine houses and far places. I spoke of grievances and of the injustice of the world. Even then I think some dim knowledge stirred in her, that I would hold her fast to the pit in which I lay.

II.

It was in the autumn that Verrall came, a young man of the wealthy house at Checkshill, smooth, well-tailored, and idle, with the unthinking assurance of those for whom the world has always opened its doors. He saw Nettie, and the thing was done.

I cannot recall the particulars of my discovery without a sensation of cold dread. There was an empty room, a drawer left open, a thin note whose phrases swam before my eyes like creatures in a tainted pool. She had gone. She had gone with him, to London and the lights, and perhaps beyond those to places that I fancied as vast and luxurious abysses, swallowing her for ever.

I did not weep. Something far older and more malign than grief rose in me, a cold and patient thing that seemed to have waited in my blood since before my birth. I sat in my lodging through the whole of that night, and the lamp guttered, and shapes gathered in the corners of the room. In the small hours I understood that I should kill them both. The knowledge came calmly, like the revelation of an elder and implacable god, and I did not question it.

III.

My friend Parload, a thin and spectacled enthusiast, laboured at that time in the service of astronomy. He had the use of a small, ill-kept observatory, and to him I went in the days that followed, seeking not comfort but distraction from the thing that gnawed within me.

"You have not looked at the sky," he said, and there was an odd tremor in his voice. "Come."

Through the cold brass tube I beheld a pale smear against the blackness, with a faint tail trailing behind it like the veil of some drowned and spectral bride. It was a new comet, he said, and it fell steadily toward the sun, and therefore toward us. He showed me then the spectroscope, and the bright lines it had traced upon the glass. Among them lay a strange green band that no known flame had ever yielded, and that he could not name.

"Cyanogen," he muttered, "and something else. Something that does not belong to any element we know."

I felt a chill that had no earthly cause. In that moment the little thing in the heavens seemed to turn its attention upon me, upon Parload, upon the whole huddled race of men. Yet my private fury was stronger even than cosmic awe, and I left the observatory with my purpose undisturbed.

IV.

The newspapers that winter were full of dark and clamorous things. Over Europe a shadow was gathering, an ominous drift of armies and alliances, of treaties whispered in locked rooms and fleets that crept from harbour to harbour. In the cafés and the pothouses men spoke of war with a ghoulish eagerness, as though some ancient appetite had stirred in them. I read of Germany and of France, of the Balkans and of the ironclads, and I felt a monstrous kinship with the whole sick continent. We were all of us sliding toward the same gulf, each driven by his own small and shapeless hatred.

I bought a revolver from a pawnbroker in Hanley, a heavy and ill-balanced instrument that smelt of old oil and other men's despair. I carried it everywhere, wrapped in a handkerchief, and at night I would turn it over in my hands and whisper to it. Meanwhile the comet swelled in the sky. By January it was a ghostly and spreading lamp, and the common people looked at it askance, and muttered of omens.

I too saw an omen. It seemed fitting that the heavens should wear a pallor like my own mind.

V.

Of the night itself I must write with care, for the words are poor instruments against such a memory.

I had traced Verrall and Nettie to a house near Checkshill, a place of dark gables and sullen woods, and I went there on foot through the thickening dusk. The comet stood enormous overhead. It was no longer a thing of tubes and glass but a monstrous, brooding presence, and from it there poured a strange, pale-emerald radiance that made the hedgerows look like the ribs of drowned things. No bird sang. No wind stirred. The whole world seemed to hold its breath.

I came to the edge of the wood with the revolver in my fist. Then I saw the sky change. The stars were blotted out, and in their place grew a greenish veil, trembling and iridescent. It sank toward the earth, slow as a descending curtain, and with it came a faint, cloying sweetness in the air.

I remember thinking, quite clearly, that this was the end of men. I remember a great ringing in my ears, and the trees leaning toward me, and the revolver slipping from my hand. I remember, finally, a vast and pitiless calm, like the calm of the grave, rising up to engulf me.

VI.

I woke upon cold grass with the dawn already high.

For some moments I lay motionless, bewildered, waiting for the familiar weight of fury to settle upon my chest. It did not come. Where it had lived there was a strange, bright emptiness, as though a fever had broken in the night. The sky above me was of a purity I had never known, a clear and tender blue without a trace of smoke. The air tasted like water from a mountain spring.

I sat up. The revolver lay a yard from me in the dew, and I regarded it with something between wonder and disgust. It seemed a dead and filthy thing, the relic of some half-remembered nightmare. How had I come to carry it? How had I ever thought that blood could mend what was broken in me?

Around me lay the bodies of men, sleeping where the green vapour had struck them, a gamekeeper beside a fallen gate, a tramp beneath a hedge. As I watched, they began to stir. They rose slowly, with the dazed and luminous faces of children, and looked at one another and at the morning, and none of them spoke.

Then the true horror and glory of it broke upon me. The vapour had not killed us. It had cleansed us, burned out of our brains the old tangled evil and left us naked to the light. I thought of Nettie and of Verrall, and I felt only a great, aching pity. I thought of the armies and the fleets, and knew they would never march. The comet had passed over the world like a vast and alien hand, and the world, remade, lay waiting.

I stood up trembling in the new sun, and for the first time in my life I wept.
