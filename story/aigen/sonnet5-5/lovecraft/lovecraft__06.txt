I.

I set down these words in a calm that I could not have conceived in the years before the Change, and yet even now my pen falters when I recall the Staffordshire of my youth. It was a land of kilns and slag-heaps, where the sky hung like a soiled and sagging canopy above the Five Towns, and where the very rain descended black and bitter upon streets that seemed to have been dug rather than built. I was William Leadford, a clerk of small wage and smaller prospect, and I dwelt in a narrow house whose windows looked upon a wall of sooty brick.

It was in that wall's long shadow that I first knew Nettie. She was a slight, quick girl, with eyes of a changeable grey, and I loved her with the sullen intensity of a mind that had nothing else to love. I told myself that she was the one clean thing in that blighted country.

In those same months there appeared among the astronomers' circulars a thing which few of us heeded, a faint smear of light amid the stars, which the learned men named a comet. I remember seeing it first on a night of rare clearness, a pallid and malign speck low in the east. I felt then, absurdly, that it regarded me. I know now that I was not wholly wrong, though what regarded us was no malice, but something vaster and stranger than malice.

II.

It was Verrall who undid me. He was the son of a wealthy house at the crest of the hill, a smooth and idle young man who strolled through our smoky streets as a traveller strolls through the lanes of a barbarous province. He spoke to Nettie of books and distant cities, of lamplit terraces and warm southern seas; and I, who could offer her only the wall and the kiln, watched her gaze lift from me as a drowning man watches the last lantern of a departing ship.

She left on a dismal evening in autumn. I learned of it from a folded paper upon my table, its few lines blotted and hasty, and I read it by the guttering light with a horror that crept slowly from my fingers to my heart. She was gone with Verrall. She begged me not to follow.

I did not weep. Something colder than grief rose within me, something that had perhaps lain coiled in my blood since the first black rain fell upon my boyhood. I went to the window and looked out upon the night. The comet had grown. It hung above the chimneys, a wan green-white blemish with a faint plume trailing behind it, and in its light the slag-heaps looked like the barrows of a forgotten and unholy people.

III.

Over all the continent that winter there brooded a strange unease, as though the comet's slow approach had stirred some sleeping poison in the minds of men. The newspapers, which I devoured now with a feverish and aimless hunger, spoke of fleets and frontiers, of ultimatums and mobilizations, of conferences that dissolved in bitterness. Great powers glared across their borders like blind and swollen beasts. England, I read, was growing restless; Germany armed; the Balkans smouldered. And above all this the comet swelled nightly, until it dominated the heavens, and the astronomers whispered of a gas, a green and unknown emanation, in whose tenuous tail our globe must presently be immersed.

I cared nothing for it. My hatred had narrowed me to a single burning point. I sold what little I possessed, and with the proceeds I purchased from a furtive dealer in a back street a revolver, a squat and ugly engine of black metal. I carried it against my ribs like a second and colder heart.

I learned that Verrall had taken Nettie to a house upon the eastern coast, and thither I resolved to go. Men looked upon me strangely in the trains, for I fear my face had altered. The very air seemed charged, expectant, like the stillness that precedes the breaking of some cyclopean wave.

IV.

I came to the coast at dusk, after a journey whose details have dissolved in a fever of impatience. The sea lay leaden and sluggish beneath a sky that I scarcely recognised. The comet now filled a third of the firmament, a monstrous and unwinking eye, and its light had assumed a faint viridian tinge that lay upon the waters like the sheen on a corpse.

All along the road I met signs of the great agitation. Men stood in knots, muttering. Some knelt in the dust. Far to the south there came, from time to time, a dull sound like the booming of distant doors, and I was told by a trembling carter that war had begun, that the fleets had met, that the world was ending. I scarcely listened. The revolver was warm against my side.

The house stood alone upon a bluff, its windows lit. I stood a long while in the darkness of a hedge, and while I stood I became aware that the night was altering about me. A strange, faint, sweetish odour hung on the air. The wind had died utterly. Overhead the stars were blotted by a thin greenish haze, descending slowly as a veil descends, and in the silence I heard the gulls cry once, wildly, and then cry no more.

V.

I strode to the door and struck upon it. Verrall himself opened it, and behind him, pale in the lamplight, I saw Nettie. For an instant we three stood motionless, and I think that in that instant we were all conscious of the same fearful stillness without.

I drew the weapon. I remember Verrall's face, not cowering as I had imagined, but bewildered, and I remember Nettie's cry. I remember too that my hand, which should have been steady with hatred, trembled as though the air itself resisted it. For the green vapour had come in at the open door; it curled about the lamp, and the flame took on a strange emerald glory.

I breathed it. It was neither bitter nor foul, but light and cold, and it ran through my lungs like clear water. My rage, that black and ancient thing, seemed suddenly to loosen its grip and to shrink away, as a beast shrinks from a sudden light. The pistol grew intolerably heavy. I saw Verrall sink to his knees, and Nettie slip softly to the floor, and the walls of the room swayed and receded as in the first dizziness of a dream.

"It has come," I heard myself say, in a voice not mine; and the lamp became a star, and the star became a vast green silence, and I fell into it, down and down, through gulfs of dim and tranquil light.

VI.

I woke to a morning such as I had never known. I lay upon the threshold, and above me stretched a sky of an unutterable blue, washed utterly clean, without a trace of smoke or shadow. The comet was gone, or rather dissolved, and the very air shimmered with a new and delicate clarity. The sea beyond the bluff glittered like a field of gems.

I rose, and my first sensation was of a terrible lightness. The revolver lay at my feet. I looked at it with a slow, strange wonder, as one regards a toy of some savage child, and I understood, with a clarity that was almost pain, that the thing I had borne against my heart was a monstrous folly. The hatred, the jealousy, the narrow and grasping fear that had been the very substance of my mind, had vanished in the night as mist vanishes under the sun.

Verrall stirred nearby, and Nettie beside him. They looked upon me, and I upon them, and in that moment we saw each other plainly, for the first time, without the distortions of possession and pride. There was no anger. There was only a vast and solemn tenderness, and a shared astonishment that we had ever been so blind.

Far inland, I knew, other sleepers were awakening: soldiers upon abandoned fields, statesmen in their council chambers, weary workers in the black towns of my birth. The green breath of the comet had cleansed us all. Where once the mind of man had been a cavern of cramped and gibbering shadows, it now stood open to the stars. I wept then, for the first time in many years, and the tears were not of sorrow. And I looked upon the vast and shining world as upon a stranger whom I had always, in some forgotten dream, been destined to love.
