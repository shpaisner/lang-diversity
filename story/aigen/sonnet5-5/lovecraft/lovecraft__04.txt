I set down these matters in my old age, when the pottery towns of Staffordshire have been long since cleansed of their smoke, and when few remain who can credit what manner of creature I was before the Change. I remember the Five Towns as a hell of kilns, a sullen land where the soot lay upon the hedgerows like the ash of some elder world, and where a man like myself, poor, narrow, and clerking for a pittance, believed his small miseries to be the very pivot of creation.

Nettie was my whole heaven then. I do not now judge the girl; I judge only the blind thing that loved her with a proprietor's fury. When she fled with Verrall, that smooth son of fortune, with his motor-cars and his careless, glittering speech, something curdled in me. The house in which I lodged seemed to lean inward upon my skull. And above the chimneys, in the evenings, there burned a new and faintly greenish star, which the newspapers called the Comet, and which I regarded with a dull and personal hatred.

It grew nightly. By midwinter it was no mere star but a pale, malign eye, trailing a thin and ghostly fire across the heavens. Learned men wrote that it was harmless; I recall that I did not believe them, and that the disbelief gave me a perverse comfort. Let the heavens fall, I thought, since my own small heaven had fallen already.

Through those months I walked the Bursley roads at night, and the horror of my thoughts kept pace with the horror of the sky. The cheap journals which I read in the tavern spoke less of the Comet than of Europe: of fleets and frontiers, of ultimatums, of nations leaning toward one another like drunken men with knives. The world seemed to me one vast festering resentment, and I felt myself its fitting child.

There was a pawnbroker in Hanbridge who kept in his window, among the wedding-rings and rusted tools, a heavy revolver. I stood before that window for three evenings. On the fourth I entered. The man's eyes slid away from mine as he counted the coins, and I remember thinking that he, too, felt the sickness in the air, the sense of a vast and indifferent attention bent upon our little earth. I carried the weapon home beneath my coat. It lay upon the table through the night, and the green glimmer from the window played along its barrel as though the stars themselves approved.

I resolved to find them. Rumour gave me their lodging in the south, and with my last savings I took the train through a country strangely restless. At every station there were reservists in their dull uniforms, and women weeping with a curious dry quiet. Broadsheets proclaimed mobilisation. The carriage was thick with tobacco and with whispered words: Germany, the Channel, the fleets. Nobody looked upward, and yet all seemed to feel the pressure of the Comet, as one feels in a dark room the presence of something that does not breathe.

By the time I came to the hills above their dwelling, war had been declared. I heard the guns, or fancied that I heard them, a low and ceaseless grumbling beyond the horizon, like the stirring of some buried and angry god. The night was strangely bright, and the sky had assumed a hue I had no name for, an unclean, luminous emerald that stained the clouds and drew from the fields a sickly pallor. The very hedgerows seemed to shudder.

I remember a hollow lane, and shadows of men moving along it with rifles, and the harsh cry of an officer. I remember that I walked on, heedless, with my hand closed upon the revolver, and that as I crossed an open down there came a silence so abrupt that it seemed an assault. The larks, which had been crying wildly in the false dawn, ceased. The wind died. And down upon the world, from that dreadful, swollen eye, there descended a faint and shimmering mist of green.

I saw it first as a thin veil drawn across the lower sky; then it was upon the hills, creeping into hollows with the slow malice of a tide. I heard a soldier somewhere cry out in a voice of utter terror, and another begin a prayer. I raised my weapon at nothing, at the heavens themselves, and screamed a curse that I cannot now bear to remember.

Then the vapour reached me. It entered my nostrils with a sharp and curiously sweet coldness. I felt my knees give way, and the stars wheeled, and the hills ran together like wet paint. My last clear thought was of Nettie's face, and that I should never now be able to destroy it. Then the dark took me, and I knew nothing at all.

I awoke, I know not after what stretch of hours, to a light unlike any I had seen. I lay in wet grass, my cheek against the cold earth, and above me arched a sky of such profound and tender blue that for a moment I believed myself dead. The green had gone. The air, when I drew it into my lungs, was like draughts of living water, thin, clean, and sweet, with no taste of smoke in it anywhere.

I rose slowly. My body ached, but my mind felt strangely washed, as though some ancient, clotted darkness had been drained from every chamber of it. The revolver lay beside me in the grass. I looked at it for a long while, and it seemed a small, ugly, foolish thing, the toy of a sick child. I could not recall the shape of the hatred that had carried me so far. I knew that it had been mine; I knew its every detail; yet it was like the memory of a nightmare, vivid but wholly without power.

All about the down lay the soldiers, sprawled in the dawn as though slain, and yet breathing, stirring, beginning to sit up with wonder in their faces. One young fellow looked at his rifle, then at the men whom a night before he would have fired upon, and began to laugh, and then to weep. Nobody spoke of war. No officer gave an order. The guns beyond the hills were silent, and I understood, with a sense of awe that was nearly terror, that they would not speak again.

Then I perceived what the Comet had done. It had not destroyed us, as the prophets had cried; it had changed the very air, and with the air the secret chemistry of our minds. The old fevers of ownership and envy, the black engines of hate that had driven nations and clerks alike, lay dissolved like frost beneath the sun. And it came upon me, dizzying, that for all the ages of our history we had been walking in a poisoned dream, never guessing that the walls of the dream were so thin.

I stood in the clean morning with the larks beginning again overhead, and I thought of Nettie, and of Verrall, and of myself. I felt grief for the wasted years, but no hatred, and I wept for the man I had been. Far off I saw the smoke of a village rising, pale and innocent, straight into that fathomless blue. And I knew that though the cosmos had touched us with an indifferent hand, it had, by some unimaginable accident or mercy, touched us toward the light.

I flung the revolver far into the gorse, and walked down toward the world.
