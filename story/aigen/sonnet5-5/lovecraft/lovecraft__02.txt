I set down these matters in the long quiet after the Change, when the memory of that older and more brutish world has grown strange to me, like the recollection of some fever-country visited in childhood. Yet it was my country, and I must tell it as it was.

I was born in the Potteries of Staffordshire, among the bottle-kilns that crowded the valleys like the squat idols of some forgotten and smoke-loving race. Day and night they vomited a yellow murk that lay upon the roofs and slag-heaps and the sullen canal, until the very sun seemed a tarnished coin. I was a clerk of small wage and smaller prospect, and I lodged in a room whose wallpaper bloomed with damp. Yet I had one light in that black place, and her name was Nettie Reid.

I loved her as only a starved and narrow mind can love, with a fierce, brooding, proprietary hunger. I believed that she loved me. I believed it until the evening I learned that I had been a fool of the first magnitude.

Her name, I afterwards understood, was only one thread in a darker web, for in those months there hung above us all a thing of which no man spoke without a lowering of the voice.

It was the comet.

It had been first a mere blur on the photographic plates of observatories, a smudge upon the abyss. Then the newspapers took it up, and in the cold clear nights of autumn men began to lift their faces to the north-east and stare.

It was Verrall who took her from me.

He was the son of a house with money and a motor-car, a smooth, lean youth who spoke of Nietzsche and of the stars, and who carried the careless confidence of those who have never been hungry. I had seen him look at Nettie across the market-place, and I had dismissed the look. That was my second folly.

She left upon a Thursday. The note she left was brief and pitiless, and I read it standing in the doorway of her mother's cottage, while the kilns breathed their poison over the street behind me. I am going away with Mr. Verrall. Do not look for me. I could not have been happy.

The words went into me like cold iron. I cannot describe the madness that followed, the long nights of pacing and cursing, the thin gray rain upon the glass, the whisper that rose out of the ugly dark of my own mind. I would find them. I would end it.

Meanwhile the newspapers grew shrill. There was talk of Germany, of ultimatums, of fleets massing in grey northern waters. Men in the public houses spoke of war as one speaks of the weather, with a fatalistic relish. All Europe, it seemed, drifted like a rudderless hulk toward some roaring cataract, and few cared to look at the water ahead.

By November the comet was no longer a smudge. It hung in the heavens like a vast and baleful eye, and its tail had begun to sweep upward in a curve of ghastly pallor, the color of a drowned man's lips. At night it cast a faint shadow. The dogs of the district howled at it, and the old women muttered of judgment.

I did not care. I had gone to a pawnbroker in Hanley and purchased a revolver, a blunt, ugly instrument of blue steel, and its weight in my pocket gave me a dreadful comfort. From a mutual acquaintance I had learned that the pair had taken refuge in a lonely cottage in the south, somewhere beyond the Downs, and thither I journeyed by slow trains through a land already rattling with preparation.

On the platforms stood reservists with bundles, and the faces of the women were set and white. Once I saw a train of guns sliding past on flat trucks, their muzzles wrapped in sacking like the snouts of sleeping beasts. And always, above it all, rode that waxing horror in the east, no longer pale but touched now with a sickly and unfamiliar green.

The war came while I was upon the road, though I scarcely understood it. A rumor passed along the countryside that the German columns had crossed some frontier, that English battalions were marching to meet them, that the thing was begun. I heard it as a man in delirium hears the clamor of a street. I walked on through the darkening country, over chalk and furze and bare black hedgerows, and the revolver knocked against my thigh with every step.

That night there was no true darkness. The comet filled a third of heaven, and the whole landscape lay bathed in a livid emerald radiance, as though the world had sunk to the bed of some haunted sea. The trees stood rigid. No bird cried. No wind stirred. I felt, with a shiver that was almost ecstasy, that the earth had halted in its course to listen.

Then a strangeness came upon the air.

I noticed it first as a faint sweetness, a clean, thin odor unlike anything of chimney or field, and I felt my heart quicken. Overhead, streaks of unearthly green fire shot across the sky, and the ground seemed to hum beneath my feet with some vast, remote vibration, as though the planet were a bell struck in the void by an unseen hand.

I saw, far off upon the slope, a handful of soldiers standing quite motionless in the gleam. Then one of them sank to the turf, and another, and another, softly, as sheaves fall under the scythe.

I laughed, I think. I drew the revolver out and held it up against the terrible sky, a puny black thing against infinity. Then my knees melted, and the green light poured over me like water, and I knew no more.

Of the darkness that followed I can say only that it was not empty. Something moved in it, vast and unhurried, and I seemed to float among immensities in which the earth was a mote and my passions a spark too small for notice. I was shown, or seemed to be shown, the whole tortured history of my kind, its wars and greeds and small cruelties, and I was shown it without anger, as a physician surveys a delirium that is about to pass.

I woke in the gray of dawn, upon the cold turf, with the dew upon my face.

For a while I lay still, and wondered at the silence of my mind. The rage had gone. It was not subdued; it was gone, as a nightmare goes, leaving only the memory that there had been something terrible. The air was sweet beyond description. The sky was a high, pure, tender blue, and the comet had vanished, leaving no trace but a faint glow of rose along the east.

I sat up. The revolver lay in the grass beside me, and I regarded it with mild amazement, as one regards the toy of a foolish and savage child. I picked it up, and it seemed to me a thing obscene, and I buried it in the chalky soil with my hands.

Along the slope, the soldiers were rising one by one, English and German alike, for the field had been a meeting of both. They stood and looked at one another, and I saw in their faces a wonder that was neither fear nor hostility. One young fellow in a grey tunic held out his hand to a man in khaki, and the man took it, and they stood so, like children who have wakened from the same dream.

I understood then that the green vapor had cleansed the very air of the world, and with it the clotted passions of its people. A great and merciful intelligence, or perhaps only a great and indifferent law, had broken the old madness as a hand might break a fever.

I rose, and walked down toward the cottage, and my heart was full of a sorrow that was also a clear and quiet joy. I thought of Nettie. I thought of Verrall. And I knew that I should find them not as enemies, but as fellow wanderers in a world made new, to whom I might offer, at last, an unclenched hand.
