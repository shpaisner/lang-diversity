I.

It was in the autumn of that year, when the chimneys of the Five Towns lay like black fingers against a sky the colour of old pewter, that I first heard the thing spoken of. I was William Leadford, a clerk at a pot-bank, and I had no learning and little hope. My lodging in Staffordshire overlooked a hollow of slag and stagnant water, where nothing grew but a few blighted thistles. I remember that the men at the works spoke of the comet idly, as men speak of a distant calamity that cannot touch them. A new star, they said, had been found creeping among the old ones, and the astronomers were troubled by its spectrum.

I cared nothing for it then. My whole soul was bound to Nettie Willersley, whose fair head and quick, restless eyes had drawn me with a devotion I now recall as almost morbid. We had walked together on the cinder paths. She had listened, or seemed to listen, while I spoke of a future in which I should rise above the grime that had shaped me.

Yet even then I felt something hovering behind the common things of life, as one feels a presence in an empty room. On clear nights, when the furnace-glare did not drown the heavens, I would look up and fancy that the stars themselves were arranged in a watchful pattern, waiting upon an event.

II.

The blow fell in November. Nettie was gone, and with her went Verrall.

He was the son of a wealthy house, smooth, easy, and gracious in the manner of those who have never known want. He had come among us idly, as a visitor to a menagerie, and had looked upon Nettie as one looks upon a curious and pleasing specimen. I learned of the flight from a trembling note left with her mother, and the words seemed to crawl like pale insects across the page. She spoke of "a larger life," and of "a world beyond Clayton."

I do not know how long I stood in that cold passage. I know only that something black and viscid rose within me, a loathing so vast that it seemed to belong to the universe rather than to myself. The very walls seemed to lean inward, and the gaslight sank to a sickly yellow.

That night I walked far upon the moors above the town. Overhead, low in the northern sky, there hung for the first time a faint smear of luminance that I knew must be the comet. It was small and pale, yet in my fevered state I thought it regarded me, as an eye regards a creature it has not yet decided to crush.

III.

Through the winter the stranger grew. The newspapers, which once relegated it to a paragraph, now gave it columns, and men began to look upward with a new and furtive uneasiness. Learned persons wrote that the thing was harmless, a tenuous veil of gas, a nothingness of vapour. Others, whom the respectable journals mocked, whispered that it carried some alien element which the air of our world had never known.

Yet the comet was not the only shadow gathering. In the same months Europe seemed to sicken. Statesmen spoke in the smooth voices of men who conceal knives; fleets moved, frontiers bristled, and every cafe and workshop hummed with talk of the coming struggle. A kind of dull and general madness crept over the nations, as though some influence from the heavens were already working upon the minds of men, drawing out their hatreds as a poultice draws out corruption.

I took no part in these debates. My own hatred was a narrower, more intimate thing, and it fed upon itself in solitude. I lost my post. I ate little. At night I sat in my cold room and watched the comet's growing radiance through the grimy window, and I planned, with a patience that now horrifies me, what I would do when I found them.

IV.

I discovered where they had gone through a cousin of Nettie's, who spoke with the sly pleasure of the gossip. They had taken a villa upon the southern downs, far from the smoke of my native county. With the last of my savings I bought a revolver of cheap manufacture, and I went south by a slow, shuddering train.

The land through which I passed seemed unreal. The fields lay under a strange, pallid light, for the comet now rivalled the moon, and its tail sprawled across a quarter of the sky like the pale tentacle of some drowned and dreaming thing. Dogs howled at the stations. Travellers spoke in hushed voices of the war that must surely break upon us within days.

At a village inn I learned the road to the villa. I walked it by night, beneath that sourceless and sickly glow. The hedgerows were dead black walls; the chalk beneath my feet gleamed like bone. The revolver lay in my pocket, heavy as a stone. I felt no pity, no doubt, and, which I found strangest, no fear of the vast thing above me.

V.

The villa stood white and secret among the beeches, one window lit. I crept to the garden wall and looked in. Nettie sat there beside the fire, and Verrall stood near her, speaking lightly. For a moment I saw them as figures in a picture, small and fragile, and an abyss of cold seemed to open in my reason.

Then the air itself altered. A faint, acrid odour drifted across the garden, sweetish and strange, such as no earthly chemistry had ever yielded. The comet's light had turned from silver to a ghastly and luminous green. It seemed to pour down like a slow tide, and the beeches took on the hue of drowned things at the bottom of a sea.

Far to the east there came a dull rumbling, as of vast engines, and I knew, with the certainty of a dreamer, that the nations had at last begun to slaughter one another. Yet the sound seemed small and remote, a child's quarrel beside the silent majesty above.

I raised the revolver. My hand shook, and a drowsiness began to steal through my limbs, soft and irresistible as the approach of death. The green radiance thickened. I tried to cry out, to curse them, to pull the trigger, but my fingers loosened of their own will.

VI.

I remember falling. I remember the cold grass against my cheek, and the great green sky wheeling above me, and the feeling that the whole world was sinking into a deep and luminous well. The rumble of war grew faint and ceased. In that last moment I thought I saw, far beyond the comet, the dim outlines of vast and patient shapes, and I understood that mankind had long been watched from some outer dark, and that now, at last, a hand had been laid upon us.

Then there was nothing.

When I woke, it was dawn, and the air had a clarity that I had never known. The very breath I drew seemed new, as though I had lived all my days in a poisoned cellar and had at last been carried into the open. The sky was washed to a delicate blue. The beeches shone. Birds sang in a chorus that seemed to hold a meaning I had never grasped before.

I rose slowly and found the revolver lying in the dew. I looked at it for a long while, and a strange horror came upon me, not of the weapon, but of the creature who had carried it there. The hatred that had burned in me like a furnace was gone. In its place stood a vast and trembling clarity, as though a veil of soot had been torn from my mind.

Across the lawn, Nettie and Verrall came out of the house, pale and wondering, and looked at me. I saw no enmity in their faces, nor did I feel any in my own. We stood in silence beneath that purified heaven, and each of us knew that the world we had known was ended, and that something stranger and greater had taken its place.
