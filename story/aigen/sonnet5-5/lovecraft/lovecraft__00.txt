I.

It is not easy to set down the matter of my youth without a shudder, though the terror that gathered about it was of a kind the world has since forgotten how to feel. I was born in the Potteries of Staffordshire, in that blackened district where the bottle-kilns stood like the stunted idols of some nameless cult, and where the sky was never wholly seen. The smoke lay upon the valleys as a pall. The canals crawled green and oily between the slag-heaps, and the rows of brick cottages sagged under a soot that no rain could wash away.

My name was William Leadford. I was a clerk of small wage and smaller prospect, and I had been bred to a life that seemed merely a slow descent into the same grey earth that fed the kilns. Yet I possessed one light, and it was a girl named Nettie. She had a quick, dark, restless beauty that seemed alien to those streets, and I loved her with the whole bitter intensity of a man who owns nothing else.

I believed, in my folly, that poverty was the worst of dooms. I did not yet know how vast, cold, and indifferent was the universe in which our small miseries were enacted, nor what visitor was already swinging toward us out of the black abyss beyond the planets.

II.

It was in the early autumn that men began to speak of the Comet. At first it was a smudge upon the photographic plates of observatories, a faint blur among the stars. Then it grew, and the newspapers gave it columns, and the learned men argued over it in measured and uneasy voices.

I remember standing one night on a ridge above Bursley, when a rare rift in the smoke let the heavens show. There it hung, a pale and baleful thing, trailing a tenuous banner across the constellations. It seemed less a body than an intention. Something in its slow approach affected me like the sight of a great, patient creature stalking its prey across a darkened field.

The spectroscope, they said, revealed a strange green line, the signature of some unknown gas. Learned men whispered of cyanogen and of poison; the vulgar spoke of the end of the world. Preachers thundered in the chapels, and the gin-shops filled. Yet the coming horror, like all horrors too large for comprehension, produced less of terror than of a sullen, feverish license. Men grew careless of their lives and savage in their passions, as though some influence already fell upon them from that spreading, spectral light.

I, who was absorbed in my own small tragedy, scarcely heeded it. For Nettie had begun to look beyond me.

III.

Her name was linked to that of Verrall, the son of a wealthy house, a smooth and gracious young man whose very clothes seemed to belong to a better order of creation. I saw them together once, laughing in a carriage, and the sight seared itself into my brain.

Then came the evening when I climbed the stairs to her lodging and found the room bare, the grate cold, and a letter on the mantel. It was brief. She had gone, she wrote, and I was not to follow; she could not live in the grey place, and Verrall would give her the sunlight I never could.

I read it many times, and the words seemed to crawl upon the paper like pale worms. I recall that the window was open, and that the comet hung in it, vast now, a ghastly emerald smear above the chimneys. It appeared to regard me with a cold and ancient amusement.

Something broke within me. The meek clerk who had borne his small indignities passed out of existence, and in his place stood a creature of cramped and murderous purpose. I would find them. I would find them if I walked to the world's edge. And I would show Verrall what the dark kilns had bred.

IV.

Meanwhile, the larger world drifted toward its own madness. The newspapers, when I could bear to read them, spoke of ultimatums and mobilizations, of fleets gathering in grey northern waters, of the Continental powers bristling like beasts about to spring. England and Germany eyed each other across the narrow sea with a hatred that seemed to have no cause beyond its own momentum.

It was a kind of cosmic joke that, as the heavens prepared to deliver their strange judgment upon our planet, mankind should busy itself with sharpening its petty knives. Reservists were called, and railway stations seethed with uniforms. Men marched and sang with a hollow gaiety, like sleepwalkers drawn toward an abyss they refused to see.

I took no side in their quarrel. I bought a revolver from a pawnbroker in Hanley, a squat and greasy thing with a worn butt, and the man who sold it gave me a look I have never forgotten, as of one who sold a rope to a drowning man. I loaded it in the dim back room of my lodging. Then I put on my hat and walked out into the dusk, southward, toward the road along which I had learned they had gone.

V.

I walked through a night that seemed to have lost its proper nature. The comet had swollen to a monstrous thing, a luminous veil that drew itself across a third of heaven, and its light was no longer white but a sick, shifting green, like phosphorescence upon a stagnant pool. By it the hedgerows looked unreal, their leaves drained of colour and their shadows wrong, cast in directions where no lamp stood.

Few creatures stirred. I passed farmsteads where dogs howled without ceasing, and cattle huddled in the corners of fields, their eyes reflecting the emerald glare. Once I met a band of drunken soldiers who cursed and sang by the roadside. Once I saw a woman kneeling in the dust, praying to a sky that gave no answer.

The air grew strange. It had a thin and metallic sweetness, and the stars at the horizon seemed to waver as though seen through water. My hatred burned steady within me, but I felt, dimly, as one feels a change of weather in the bones, that something far greater than my wrath was about to occur, and that my wrath was a tiny, insect thing beside it.

I came at last to a hill above a quiet valley, and there, in the window of a distant inn, I saw a light.

VI.

I do not know how long I stood upon that hill. The revolver was in my hand, and its cold weight was the only reality left to me. Below, a figure moved across the lit window, and I knew the set of those shoulders, and I knew that I had found them.

Then the sky opened.

I can describe it only as a descent. The green light thickened and fell as a mist falls, soundless and soft, flowing down the slopes in slow, luminous tides. It filled the valley like water filling a bowl. I saw it touch the hedges and the roofs of the inn, and I heard, from far away, a vast sighing, as though the whole breathing world had let go its breath. I raised the pistol, and my arm grew heavy. The pale vapour reached my knees, my breast, my eyes. There was a taste of strange sweetness, and a sensation as of falling through endless, gentle space; and the last thing I knew was the green dark closing over me like a lid.

Then there was waking.

I lay in wet grass beneath a sky of such clear and tender blue that I wept at the sight. The smoke was gone from my mind as from the heavens. The revolver lay beside me, and I looked on it with a mild wonder, as a man looks on a toy he has long outgrown. My hatred had vanished utterly, and in its place was a quiet, luminous sanity, a terrible and glorious clarity, in which I saw Verrall and Nettie and myself as three bewildered children, and the whole old world as a nightmare from which we had been delivered. The Comet had not destroyed us. It had awakened us. And I rose, trembling, to greet the first morning of a changed humanity.
