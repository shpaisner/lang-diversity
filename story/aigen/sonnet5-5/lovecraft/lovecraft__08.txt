I.

It is not without a shudder that I set down, in the evening of a changed world, the memory of what I was before the vapours came. I, William Leadford, was then a clerk in the pottery-towns of North Staffordshire, a region of blackened kilns and bottle-shaped ovens that squatted against the sky like the shrines of some smoke-loving and forgotten god. The very air tasted of sulphur and coal. The canals ran the colour of old ink. In a narrow brick house in Swan Bank, I lived upon a pittance, and I nursed a bitterness that seemed to me then the only honest thing in my nature.

I loved Nettie Stewart. I say it plainly, though the word was a poor one for the black and knotted thing that had grown within me. She was slight and brown-eyed, with a trick of looking sideways that made every plain remark seem a secret. I had walked with her upon the Sunday hills above Clayton, where the wind bore the stench of the furnaces away, and I had believed her mine as one believes in the permanence of a hearth.

Yet even then, above the smoke, there hung a nameless disquiet. The older men spoke of it in the public-houses, and the newspapers spoke of little else: a strange new star, faint and greenish, creeping nightly among the constellations.

II.

It was Verrall who took her from me. He was the son of a wealthy house, lean and assured, with the careless graces of a man who has never counted his shillings. He had come to the district upon some errand of his family's, and he looked upon our grimy streets as a traveller might look upon the huts of a barbarous tribe.

I do not know how he first found her. I know only that in the late summer her letters grew short, and then ceased. Then one evening I climbed the stair of her mother's house and found the old woman weeping among the cheap china dogs. Nettie was gone. A note, left upon the mantel in a hand I scarcely knew, spoke of "a larger life" and begged me not to follow.

I stood in that low parlour, and a coldness passed through me, as though some vast and patient thing had breathed upon my neck. I did not weep. I looked through the window at the red-lit smoke above the kilns, and I swore that Verrall should answer for it. Beyond the chimneys, in the dusk, the green star burned a little brighter than the night before.

III.

Through that autumn I wandered like a man poisoned. I lost my situation, having struck my master across the face for some trivial rebuke, and I sank into a life of cheap lodgings and sleepless walking. The comet grew nightly. It was a bloated, ashen-green eye, trailing a pale and spectral veil across the heavens, and the learned men wrote that Earth must pass through its tail.

Meanwhile, from beyond the sea, came murmurings of a darker kind. Europe, that ancient continent of crowns and cannon, was drifting toward war as a derelict drifts toward the reef. The papers spoke of fleets and ultimatums, of frontier incidents, of Powers that bristled and bared their teeth. In the taverns the men grew sullen and restless. I heard them cheer for battles not yet fought, and I felt that the whole age was shuddering toward some abyss, hurried by a malign tide it could neither see nor resist.

I cared nothing for Europe. My hatred had narrowed to a single bright point. By the aid of a sour acquaintance I learned that Verrall had taken a house in the south, and that Nettie dwelt with him there.

IV.

I bought a revolver in a pawnbroker's shop near the Potteries station. The man who sold it looked at me a moment too long and asked no questions. It lay in my pocket like a cold and patient toad, and I took a strange comfort from its weight.

The journey southward remains in my memory as a fevered dream. The trains were crowded with soldiers and reservists, and at every station there rose the same rumour: that war had been declared, that the guns were already speaking across the frontiers. Men shouted from the carriage windows. Women wept upon the platforms. In the sky, even by day, a pale and unwholesome luminosity lingered, and when night came, the comet stood overhead, huge and loathsome, shedding a greenish pallor upon the fields.

I remember a hush upon the land, an unnatural stillness in which dogs whimpered and cattle stood huddled in the corners of their meadows. The very birds seemed to know. I alone, of all that fevered multitude, was untroubled by the omen; my mind held only the picture of a lean face, a pair of brown eyes, and the sound of a trigger drawn back.

V.

The house stood upon a wooded slope, a pale and graceful thing of gables and lawns, remote from any road. I came to it at nightfall, on foot, my boots heavy with the mire of the lanes. The heavens had grown strange. The comet had spread across half the sky, and the clouds themselves seemed lit from within by a sickly emerald fire. The trees stood black and motionless against it, and the shadows upon the lawn had a greenish edge, as if the world were drowned beneath some stagnant sea.

I crept through the shrubbery, the revolver clenched in my hand, and I heard, far to the east, a dull and rolling thunder that was not thunder. The guns, I knew, had begun. Yet it seemed remote and meaningless, like the muttering of dreamers in another chamber.

Through a lighted window I saw them. Verrall sat by a fire with a book upon his knee, and Nettie stood near him, her hand upon his shoulder. They seemed so small, so domestic, so entirely unaware of the monstrous radiance gathering above their roof. A great laugh rose in my throat, but I swallowed it. I went toward the terrace doors.

VI.

I do not know by what means I entered. I remember only the sudden glare of the lamplight, and the faces turning toward me, white with astonishment. Nettie cried my name. Verrall rose, and in his eyes there was less fear than a weary and contemptuous pity, which maddened me beyond all reason. I raised the revolver.

And then the air changed.

I cannot describe it, though I have tried in many manuscripts since. It was as if the night itself drew breath. A faint sweetness stole through the open doors, a scent like no flower of earth, and the lamplight shivered and turned green. I saw the flames in the grate leap and sink and burn with a pale and ghostly lustre. Beyond the windows the whole sky had become a vast curtain of living emerald, rippling, folding, descending, and the stars were quenched behind it one by one.

My hand shook. The revolver grew impossibly heavy. I tried to speak the curse I had rehearsed through so many sleepless months, but my tongue lay thick and strange in my mouth. I saw Verrall sway and put a hand to his brow. I saw Nettie sink slowly to the carpet, her eyes wide and wondering, like a child's. The thunder of the distant guns faded, and a profound and cosmic silence settled over the earth, the silence of a world unmade and waiting to be written anew.

I fell. The floor rose gently to meet me, and the last thing I knew was the green light pouring over us all, indifferent, ancient, and utterly without malice. Then my hatred, my jealousy, and the black little self that had harboured them dissolved like smoke upon an unimaginable wind.
