I set down these things with a hand that still trembles, though the years since have been kind, and though what came at the end was, by every outward measure, a mercy. But I was a creature of the Potteries then, born under the smoke of the Five Towns, and a man cannot recall the soul he wore before the change without a shudder, as one might recall a cellar in which he once lived contentedly with rats.

My name was William Leadford. I was a clerk in a dim Staffordshire works, where the kilns bellied up their sulphurous breath against a sky the color of old pewter. I lived in a narrow house of blackened brick, and I loved Nettie Stewart with a love that was chiefly hunger. She was slight, quick-eyed, and restless, and she looked past my poverty as one looks past a smudge upon a windowpane, toward some brighter thing beyond.

That brighter thing had a name. It was Verrall, young master of the great house upon the hill, whose motorcar had lately been seen too often in the lanes beyond the pits.

I did not then know that the very heavens had begun to take notice of us.

It was my friend Parload who first showed me the comet. He was a thin, stooping creature who kept a rickety telescope in his attic and spoke of orbits with the tenderness other men reserve for their children. On a cold night in the autumn he led me up a stair that smelled of damp plaster, and set my eye to the brass.

Out of the black there swam a faint smudge with a pale, fan-shaped tail, and something in its attitude oppressed me. It seemed not to travel but to lean toward us, patient and incurious, like a drowned face rising beneath ice.

"It grows," said Parload softly. "Each night it grows. And the spectroscope shows a strange green line in it, Leadford, a line no one can name."

I laughed, but the laugh died unfinished. Far below, in the streets of Hanley, I heard the tramp of a drunken crowd shouting for war against Germany, and the sound seemed puny and mad beneath that silent, widening stranger. Men were whetting their knives for one another while a vaster thing swung down upon them all.

Nettie left in the first week of November.

I found her room empty, her little trunk gone, and upon the table a note whose phrases I shall not copy. It was kindly, which was the worst of it. It spoke of a larger life, and of Verrall, and of forgiveness. I stood a long while in that chill chamber, and I felt the walls and ceiling grow strange, as though the room were a shell and I a blind, bleeding thing within it.

Something black unfolded in me then. I will not call it grief. Grief is human and has dignity. This was an ancient and reptilian thing, which had slept in my marrow since before the first cities, and it woke with a slow, delighted yawn.

I went to a pawnbroker's and bought a revolver for eleven shillings. The shopman, a yellow, wizened fellow with a cast in one eye, wrapped it in brown paper with the courtesy of one who has sold many such parcels and knows their destinations. The sky above his shop had turned a sickly, oily green about its edges, and the comet hung in it by day, a pale and swollen wound.

I followed them to London, then onward by roads I scarcely remember, driven by a rage that had abolished sleep. Everywhere the world seemed to sicken with the same sullen fever as myself. Newspapers screamed of ultimatums. Troop trains crawled northward, packed with silent boys whose faces were already gray with the thing that waited. In the market towns the people gathered in knots, staring upward, whispering of portents, and drinking steadily.

The nights became unnatural. The comet's glow lay upon the fields like phosphorescence upon a corpse, and the hedges, the ricks, the very hands of men took on a faint, ghoulish verdigris. Dogs howled without cause. Birds fell silent in the daytime and wheeled about in aimless, soundless circles.

Parload had written me, a letter I read in a ditch by that unholy light. He said the tail of the comet would touch our air within days. He said that no one could foretell what the strange gas might do. Then, in a trembling postscript, he said that he was no longer afraid.

I crushed the letter in my fist. I had no room in me for fear. I had room only for the weight of the pistol against my ribs, and for the two faces I meant to find.

I found them at an inn on the edge of a bleak heath, and the hour was that of the comet's coming.

I remember the inn as a low, rambling place of crooked gables, its windows lit with a feeble yellow that the sky made shameful. I remember the wind dying away all at once, as if some vast hand had been laid across the mouth of the world. I remember the silence, which was not the absence of sound but its murder.

Then the air itself changed. A thin and luminous vapor, green as the eye of some deep-sea thing, came crawling across the heath. It lapped about my boots and rose along the stone walls. It was cold and sweet, with a faint odor like crushed leaves in rain. Far off I heard a cry go up from a hundred throats, and then no cry at all.

I drew the revolver. My hand, I recall, was perfectly steady. I looked up at the heavens, where the whole sky had become a cataract of pale emerald fire, and I understood, with an awful clarity, how small and ridiculous a thing was the hatred I carried.

Even so, I did not lower the weapon. I breathed the green air once, deeply, as a drowning man breathes water, and the earth swung out from under me.

I woke at dawn, in wet heather, with the revolver lying beside me like a dead and foolish toy.

The sky was a blue so clean and tender that I wept before I knew the cause. The green was gone. The air tasted like the first morning of creation. And within me, where the black reptile had coiled, there was only a vast and quiet space, scoured and sweet, as a house is after fever has left it.

I rose. All about the heath lay sleepers, tramps and soldiers and country folk, stirring slowly in the grass like the newly born. None of them shouted. None of them reached for a weapon. They looked upon one another with a grave and dawning wonder, as though each saw, for the first time, that his neighbor possessed a face.

I walked toward the inn. Verrall stood in its doorway, pale and hollow-eyed, and Nettie beside him. I saw them plainly then, as two bewildered and frightened children, no more vile than myself. Verrall met my gaze and did not flinch, and I did not raise the pistol, which I had left among the heather.

Above us, the last frail thread of the comet's tail dissolved into the morning, like a breath fading from a mirror. I cannot say what vast and nameless intelligence, if any, had passed so near us in the dark. I know only that it had unmade the madness of my kind and left no explanation, and that I have never since dared to ask it for one.
