I. The Potteries

In the year of which I write, the county of Stafford, between the hours of six in the morning and eight at night, lay under a canopy of smoke produced by some four thousand bottle-shaped kilns. Beneath this canopy, in the town of Clayton, lived William Leadford, a clerk in the offices of a pottery works, aged twenty-two, earning eighteen shillings a week, and possessing no other capital than a stubborn heart and a certain bitter intelligence.

This heart had been given entirely to Nettie Willersley, the daughter of a respectable but reduced householder, whose cottage stood at the edge of the Bleak Hill road. Leadford had calculated that, by dint of the severest economy, he might marry in eleven years. Unhappily, Nettie had calculated otherwise.

On the evening of the 4th of October, he found the cottage door ajar, the fire dead, and old Willersley seated before a letter, which he held as one holds a dead bird. The letter was brief. Nettie had gone, and with her had gone Mr. Verrall, the only son of the wealthy family at the Hall, a young man of twenty-five whose gloves cost more than Leadford's weekly wage.

"They have taken the London train," said the old man at last.

Leadford did not answer. He stood motionless, and had any person observed his face by the light of the single candle, he would have remarked that it had acquired, within the space of a minute, the hardness of fired porcelain. Then he turned and went out into the smoky dark.

II. Parload's Telescope

Two nights afterwards, wandering without aim along the ridge above the town, Leadford encountered his friend Parload, a fellow clerk, tall, stooped, and nearsighted, who devoted his scanty leisure to astronomy. Upon a wooden platform in a neighbour's garden stood Parload's instrument: a refractor of three inches aperture, purchased by instalments, and mounted with the ingenuity of poverty.

"Come," said Parload, who cared little for the griefs of the heart when the heavens were open. "You shall see something worth your while."

Leadford applied his eye to the eyepiece. In the field of view hung a pale and feeble smudge, no larger than a thumbnail upon a window, with a faint tail slanting away from it.

"It is a comet," explained Parload with the gravity of a professor. "It was found by an observatory in the south only last month. It is now distant about two hundred million miles, and it approaches at a rate which I estimate, from my observations, at upwards of a hundred thousand miles a day. Do you perceive the line in its spectrum? The astronomers say it is green, and that it is the line of an unknown gas."

"And what will it do?" asked Leadford, without lifting his head.

"Nothing, perhaps. It will pass near the Earth; nothing more. A comet weighs less than a whisper."

Leadford gazed a long time at the small and dying light. It seemed to him strange that anything so remote could be so calmly indifferent to the sorrows of Staffordshire.

III. The Gathering Storm

It was the misfortune of this year that the sky was not the only region in which clouds assembled. Every newspaper that Leadford opened in the clerks' room spoke of fleets and frontiers. The German Empire and the British had exchanged notes upon the subject of naval construction, and these notes had grown more curt with each exchange. France mobilised her reserves on the Vosges. Russia moved her regiments toward the Prussian border at a rate of twelve trains daily. In Whitehall, it was reported, the Cabinet sat until three in the morning.

At the pottery the foreman posted a notice that orders for the Continental trade were suspended. Three hundred workmen were discharged on a single Saturday, and Leadford, at the foot of the staircase, heard them muttering in the dialect of the district, some cursing the Germans, others the masters, and some, more simply, the times.

The Staffordshire newspaper, meanwhile, printed on its fourth page a short paragraph about the comet, now said to be visible to the naked eye as a hazy star in the constellation of Virgo. On its first page it printed the price of rifles.

Leadford read both with an equal and curious detachment. His private catastrophe had already arrived; the public catastrophes seemed to him merely the larger copies of a smaller pattern. In his waistcoat he carried a letter from Nettie, three lines long, which he did not need to take out and read, for he knew every word of it, as one knows the stones of a wall against which one has been flung.

IV. The Purchase

In the third week of October, Leadford drew from the savings bank the sum of four pounds, seven shillings, which represented fourteen months of patient thrift and the entire foundation of his imagined marriage. Of this sum he expended two pounds ten at a pawnbroker's in Hanley, where he acquired a revolver of American manufacture, six chambers, with a box of cartridges numbering fifty.

The pawnbroker, a prudent man, inquired no questions. Leadford walked home through the rain with the weapon wrapped in newspaper, and in the empty room which he rented over a chandler's shop he opened the parcel and examined it by the light of a paraffin lamp. It weighed, as he afterwards ascertained, one pound and fourteen ounces; yet it seemed to him heavier than all the pottery in the county.

He had learned, through a cousin who served at the Hall, that Verrall and Nettie were lodged in a small house near Surrey, within a mile of the river at Shepperton, and that the young man's father had refused any communication. Leadford resolved to go.

He packed a bag, wrote a note of resignation to his employer, and paid his rent. As he closed the door, he glanced through the little window at the sky. The comet was plainly visible now: a pallid, feathery blur, with its tail extended like a gauze scarf across a fifth part of the heavens. It had doubled in size within a fortnight, and it appeared to him, in his exalted humour, that it had turned its face toward the Earth with the deliberation of a living thing.

V. The Road South

The journey from Staffordshire to London, which in ordinary times occupied four hours, occupied on this occasion nearly eleven. At Rugby the train was stopped for two hours to permit the passage of a military special, consisting of thirty-one wagons laden with artillery and horses. At Willesden, soldiers in khaki stood along the platform, and a newsboy cried a rumour that German cruisers had been sighted in the North Sea.

Leadford passed these events with the abstraction of a sleepwalker. His fellow travellers, a corn merchant, two ladies, and a clergyman, spoke of war with horror and of the comet with jocularity. The clergyman declared that such objects had been seen before, in 1066 and in 1811, and that the world had survived them. The corn merchant added that the comet's tail was nothing but a little vapour, and he consulted his watch.

"A vapour," repeated Leadford, aloud, and the others looked at him curiously.

He arrived at Euston at midnight. London was strangely illuminated, for the comet cast upon the roofs a dim and sickly radiance, the colour of old verdigris, in which the gaslamps seemed to burn with a reddened and almost embarrassed flame. The pavements were crowded with idlers, their faces turned upward, and from some distant quarter came the sound of a band, playing with a feverish gaiety.

Leadford crossed the city on foot, his hand closed around the weapon in his pocket. He reached the river road at dawn, hungry and hollow-eyed, and he took the path along the Thames toward Shepperton.

VI. The Green Dawn

On the evening of the third day following, the 17th of November, as nearly as Leadford could afterwards reckon, he stood upon a rising ground above the little house in which Nettie and Verrall were concealed. It was a plain brick dwelling with a slate roof and a garden of withered chrysanthemums, and a lamp burned in the window of the lower room.

The sky was now a spectacle of which no pen of the period could give an adequate account. The comet filled nearly the whole of the eastern heaven: a vast, luminous head, as large as ten full moons, from which the tail streamed across the zenith in a pale and trembling arch. The air was windless and strangely warm. Over the fields lay a stillness so profound that Leadford heard distinctly the tick of his own watch.

He drew the revolver. He counted the chambers, as a man counts the beads of a grim rosary. Through the window he perceived a figure moving, a woman's figure, bending over the fire, and his breath stopped.

At that instant the light changed. The pallor of the sky deepened to green, a rich, liquid, emerald green, flooding the garden, the roofs, the river, and the trees, until his own hands appeared to him the hands of a drowned man. A sound like the distant sighing of a great wind rose from the north, though not a leaf stirred. The air itself seemed to thicken, and a delicate vapour, faintly sweet, crept along the ground about his feet.

Leadford raised the revolver, but his arm felt strangely heavy. He took one step toward the house, then another, and he beheld the stars above him blurring like lamps seen through water.

"Parload," he murmured absurdly, "it is the green line."

The weapon slipped from his fingers and fell silently into the grass. The ground rose gently to meet him, and the green light closed over the world.
