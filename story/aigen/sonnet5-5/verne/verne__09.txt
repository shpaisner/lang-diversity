I.

In the district of North Staffordshire, where the five towns of the Potteries lie beneath a perpetual canopy of smoke, there lived in the closing years of the last century a young man named William Leadford. He was twenty-two years of age, of middling height, and possessed of a spare, sinewy frame; his employment was that of a clerk in a manufactory of earthenware, at a wage which the mathematician would have described as the limit of subsistence. Some four hundred kilns, each exhaling its sulphurous breath, darkened the valley of the Trent; and in this atmosphere, charged with coal-dust and clay, Leadford had passed his whole existence.

Yet one thing in that grey country shone for him: Nettie Verrall, the daughter of a respectable family, whose features he had admired for three years with a devotion proportioned to his poverty. He had walked beside her on the Sunday road to Trentham; he had spoken, he believed, of the future.

On the evening of the fourteenth of October, on returning from the works, he found his mother standing at the door with a face the colour of ashes. Nettie had gone. She had quitted her father's house at dawn, and the testimony of a porter at Stoke station left no room for doubt: she had taken the London train in the company of Edward Verrall, the young gentleman whose name she bore only by coincidence of cousinhood, and whose fortunes were as ample as Leadford's were scanty.

The clerk heard the narrative without speaking. Then he sat down upon a kitchen chair, and the clock above the dresser counted forty seconds aloud.

II.

While this small tragedy was enacted in a Staffordshire kitchen, the heavens were preparing a spectacle of a far different magnitude.

In the preceding spring, the observatory at Cambridge had announced the discovery of a new comet in the constellation of Draco. Its motion was rapid; its distance, computed by the best methods of that day, amounted to some three hundred and forty million kilometres, a space which light itself requires nearly twenty minutes to traverse. The spectroscope, that marvellous instrument which analyses the very substance of the stars, revealed in its tail the bright bands of cyanogen, together with certain lines which no chemist could identify, and which pointed toward a vapour unknown upon the Earth.

The learned world was divided. Some professors, with the serenity of men whose studies are secure, declared that the earth would pass through the tail of the comet without inconvenience, as a ship passes through a bank of mist. Others, less tranquil, spoke of poisoning of the atmosphere. The public, however, took scant interest; for the newspapers of that season were occupied with a nearer menace.

In the chanceries of Europe, the diplomatists were exchanging notes with that exquisite politeness which precedes disaster. The great powers had increased their armaments; fleets assembled in the North Sea; and in the columns of the press there appeared, with ominous regularity, the words "ultimatum" and "mobilisation." Thus the nations of the world, alarmed by neither the comet nor their own folly, advanced toward a common abyss.

III.

Leadford passed three days in a condition which the physician would have named stupor, and the poet despair. He went to his work, he copied invoices, he returned; but he neither ate nor spoke. His mother, a woman who had borne poverty with the fortitude of a Roman matron, regarded him with silent terror.

On the fourth day there arrived a letter. It was written in Nettie's hand, upon paper of a quality he had never himself been able to afford, and bore the postmark of a town in the south of England. It contained no more than some twelve lines. She begged his forgiveness; she affirmed that she had acted for the best; she added, with a cruelty unconscious of itself, that he would one day thank her for sparing him a life of privation.

Leadford read it twice. Then he rose, folded the paper with extreme precision, and placed it within his pocket-book.

"Mother," said he, "I am going to London."

"With what money, William?"

"With eleven pounds and seven shillings, the whole of my savings."

She did not oppose him. It is a singular fact that those who love us best often discern, in the countenance of the afflicted, a resolution which no argument could shake. She merely packed his valise, adding a woollen scarf and a loaf; and the young man, kissing her brow, departed through the smoke toward the station.

IV.

The express from Stoke to Euston accomplishes the distance of two hundred and ten kilometres in somewhat less than four hours. Leadford, seated in a third-class compartment among commercial travellers and a corpulent clergyman, scarcely perceived the passing of Rugby, of Bletchley, of Watford. His reflections were bitter, and his imagination, inflamed by the fever of jealousy, constructed a hundred scenes of vengeance.

His fellow-passengers conversed of the Continent. A traveller in cutlery, lately returned from Hamburg, asserted that the German reserves had been quietly called to the colours; a second, who dealt in hops, declared that the Russian railways were crowded with troops. The clergyman observed that Providence would arrange all.

"And the comet, sir?" inquired the youngest of the party.

"The comet," replied the clergyman, "is a very small affair."

Leadford raised his eyes toward the window. It was now four o'clock, and the winter twilight was descending upon the fields of Hertfordshire. Low in the northern sky, above a bank of cloud, there hung a pale and ghostly streak, like a stroke of chalk upon a slate. It was the first time he had truly beheld the wanderer. It appeared to him, in his bitterness, not as a portent but as an indifference, a vast and silent thing which cared nothing for the sorrows of a Staffordshire clerk.

He turned from it with a shrug, and the train plunged into the tunnels of Camden.

V.

Arrived in London, Leadford directed his steps toward the lodging of a former colleague named Parload, an enthusiast of astronomy who inhabited a garret in Clapham, and who had, by a fortunate coincidence, been the confidant of Nettie's infidelity. Parload received him with a telescope under his arm.

"Verrall has taken a house at Lynton Cross, in Kent," said he. "I know it from the girl's own letters. But come, Leadford, you are fatigued, and you are not yourself. Look rather at the heavens!"

Through the little brass instrument, Leadford observed the comet. The head was now a luminous nucleus of a greenish hue, surrounded by a nebulous envelope; and the tail, which a week before had measured barely two degrees, extended across a third of the firmament.

"It approaches at the rate of some forty kilometres in the second," said Parload, with the pride of a man who owns a share in the universe. "In six days it will touch our atmosphere."

"And the war?" asked Leadford, absently.

"It will begin before the comet ends. But what is the war, compared with this!"

Leadford said nothing. Before departing, however, he descended to a pawnbroker's shop in the Wandsworth Road, and there purchased for the sum of two pounds, with ten cartridges, a revolver of American manufacture. He placed it in his coat with a deliberation that would have alarmed his mother, and which he would have been at a loss to justify to himself.

VI.

At daybreak on the following morning, Leadford took the railway to Kent. The sky, curiously, had not brightened with the sunrise; a strange and glaucous light hung over the landscape, as though the very air had been tinged by some distant emerald. The birds were silent. The hedgerows, the roofs, the spires of the villages, all appeared as objects in an aquarium, and the few travellers on the platform at Sevenoaks raised their faces toward the zenith with an uneasy and instinctive reverence.

The comet had changed. Where before there had been a pale streak, there now blazed a vast green fan, whose vertex was hidden behind the horizon, and whose rays, spreading like the ribs of some celestial instrument, covered half the vault of heaven. It cast upon the earth a light which was neither day nor night, but a third condition of the world.

Leadford walked the eight kilometres from the station to Lynton Cross with the quick, mechanical stride of a man bent upon one object. His hand rested upon the weapon in his pocket. Before him, at the end of a lane of beeches, he distinguished a small villa with a pointed gable, and in its garden a figure in a pale dress, which he knew.

At that moment a faint, fine mist began to descend. It was not rain; it had no moisture; it seemed rather a luminous dust, a subtle exhalation, which gathered along the hedges and coiled across the road about his ankles. He drew breath, and found it sweet, cool, and singularly exhilarating, like the air of mountains he had never seen.

He advanced three paces more. The revolver, withdrawn from his pocket, grew curiously heavy in his hand. The lane seemed to lengthen; the green light deepened to a limpid splendour; and in the stillness, far off, he heard a sound like the sighing of an immense and gentle sea.

"Nettie!" he cried; but the word dissolved upon his lips, and the earth, without violence, tilted slowly toward his cheek.
