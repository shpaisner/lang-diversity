I. The Empty Chair

On the evening of the fourteenth of October, in the year of which I write, William Leadford returned to his lodging in the Potteries. It lay in that district of Staffordshire where, over some nine miles of brick and slag, the kilns of Burslem, Hanley, and Stoke send up their smoke by day and glow like furnaces by night. He had worked eleven hours at a wage of twenty-two shillings a week. He carried in his pocket a small ring of Birmingham silver, bought with the savings of eight months.

The ring was never to be delivered.

On the table, weighted by a teacup, lay a half-sheet of paper in a hand he knew too well. Nettie Stanton had written it with a haste that blotted the ink. She told him she was going; that she had been a fool to promise; that Mr. Verrall, whose father owned the great works at Clayton and the fine house above the town, could give her a life Leadford could not give her in a hundred years of labor. She begged him not to follow.

Leadford read the note three times, with the deliberate slowness of a man who doubts the evidence of his eyes. He then folded it into eighths and sat for two hours without moving. The kilns of Hanley reddened the window. A cart passed on the cobbles, and a dog barked. Somewhere in the house a clock struck eleven.

It is a curious fact of human nature that grief, when it comes upon a man of his station, seldom expresses itself in tears. It hardens, like clay in the oven, and takes a fixed and brittle form. By midnight, Leadford's sorrow had taken the shape of a single, silent resolve.

II. The Stranger in the Heavens

While this small tragedy was enacted in a Staffordshire back street, the astronomers of Europe and America were engaged upon a larger matter.

In the preceding spring, a photographic plate exposed at the observatory of Heidelberg had revealed a faint, nebulous smudge in the constellation of Leo, where no such object had been catalogued. Measurements were made and repeated. By midsummer the computation was beyond dispute: a comet, previously unrecorded, was advancing toward the sun with a velocity exceeding thirty miles in the second. Its nucleus was estimated at some forty thousand miles in diameter, and its path would carry it within a very small distance of the terrestrial orbit.

The learned journals were divided. Some savants declared, with the calm of men who have never been wrong in public, that the earth had passed through cometary tails before without injury. Others, more prudent, observed that the spectroscope had detected in the visitor's light the bands of cyanogen, a gas as deadly as it is familiar to the chemist. A few ventured to compute the hour of closest approach, and fixed it, within a margin of some days, for the close of the month.

Leadford, as he walked to the works on the following morning, glanced upward, and for the first time truly saw it. It hung low in the east before the sunrise, a pale green disk with a faint plume streaming behind it, like the ghost of a lamp left burning in a deserted house. He regarded it without wonder. It seemed to him merely one more indifferent thing in a world that had grown suddenly very small.

III. The Drift Toward War

If the heavens were uneasy, the earth was no less so.

It was a season in which the newspapers of every capital seemed to compete in the composition of menaces. A quarrel over a strip of territory in the East had grown, by incalculable stages, into a correspondence between chancelleries. The fleets of Great Britain and Germany were reported to be coaling. The railways of the Continent had received mysterious instructions concerning rolling stock. In Paris, in Berlin, in St. Petersburg, men of grave aspect and impeccable linen pronounced the word honor with a frequency that should have alarmed the sober.

In the Potteries, these great matters reached the workers by a roundabout route, chiefly by the evening papers and the harangues of a certain socialist cobbler who held forth every night at the corner of Marsh Street. Leadford heard him once, and found the man's rhetoric as empty as the bellies of those who applauded him. Yet he could not wholly dismiss the sense of a vast machinery, ill-oiled and ill-governed, grinding slowly toward a catastrophe that no single person desired and none seemed able to prevent.

"It will be war by Christmas," said a fellow-workman, rinsing the clay from his hands.

"There'll be no Christmas worth the name," replied another, and pointed upward, where the comet, now doubled in size, shone through the haze of the chimneys with a faint and sickly luster.

Leadford said nothing. His mind was occupied with a lesser war, and a nearer enemy.

IV. The Instrument of Vengeance

On the twenty-fourth, having drawn his week's wages and a small sum from his savings-box, Leadford presented himself at a pawnbroker's in Hanley, where, after some quiet negotiation, he acquired a revolver of the Webley pattern, with six cartridges in a paper packet. The pawnbroker, a thin man with a cast in one eye, asked no questions. In those days, a man in Leadford's condition was easily recognized and as easily ignored.

He proceeded that night by the mail train to London, and thence, by a series of slower conveyances, toward the southern county where, as he had learned from a servant of the Verralls, the young man had taken a furnished house for his companion. The journey occupied nearly the whole of a day. Through the window of the third-class carriage there passed, in rapid succession, the iron landscapes of the Midlands, the pasture of the Home Counties, and at last the chalk and hop-gardens of the south.

The reader will perhaps expect to find in the traveler some ferocity of countenance. There was none. Leadford was pale, silent, and extremely exact in his movements. He ate his bread and cheese with method. He counted his change twice. Yet within the lining of his coat, against his ribs, the revolver lay as heavy as a conviction, and he was conscious of it at every jolt of the carriage.

At a wayside inn that evening, the landlord remarked upon the strange light in the sky. The comet, he said, had grown so large that his cattle would not settle. Leadford looked out over the darkening fields. The thing had swollen to a third the width of the moon, and its light lay on the hedgerows with the tint of old copper left in water.

V. The Night of the Green Sky

The eve of the comet's nearest approach was marked by phenomena which no almanac had foretold.

By nine o'clock the stars in the east had gone out, not blotted by cloud, but dimmed by a luminous haze that spread upward from the horizon. The air was perfectly still. Leadford, walking the lane that climbed to the house of his rival, perceived that the very shadows had altered. They fell upon the road not black but violet, and the leaves of the hawthorn shone with a metallic green.

From the south there came, at intervals, a dull concussion, which he took at first for thunder. It was not thunder. Afterward he learned that on that night, along a frontier a few hundred miles distant, the first guns of the long-expected war had spoken, and that the cannoneers, stupefied by the sky, had fired half-heartedly and soon ceased.

Somewhere a church bell began to ring, and then another, without any evident purpose, as though the hands that pulled them had resolved to make some answer to the heavens. A man ran past Leadford in the lane, shouting incoherently, and vanished into the green dusk.

Leadford halted before a gate. Beyond it, at the end of a short drive of gravel, stood a house with lighted windows. He drew the revolver. His hand was quite steady. He observed, with the curious detachment of the crisis, that the barrel gleamed like a thing made of emerald, and that the air about him had acquired a faint, sweetish pungency.

VI. The Awakening

He had advanced perhaps twenty paces up the drive when the atmosphere itself seemed to change its character.

It is impossible to describe with perfect accuracy a sensation for which language provides no precedent. The vapor, which the astronomers afterward identified as a hitherto unknown gaseous element, had descended upon the earth's upper air and was now sinking through the lower strata, mingling with every breath. Leadford felt neither pain nor suffocation. He felt, rather, a sudden loosening, as of a tight cord cut within the chest. His limbs grew heavy. The gravel rose slowly toward him, and he perceived, with no alarm whatever, that he was lying upon it, with the revolver beside his open hand.

Above him the sky had become a vast and tranquil green. The comet's head filled a quarter of the heavens, and its radiance fell upon his face like a benediction. His last thought before consciousness left him was not of Nettie, nor of Verrall, nor of the war; it was a strange, wide wonder at the smallness of everything he had carried so long.

How many hours he lay there, he could not afterward tell. When he opened his eyes, the dawn was breaking over the Channel, clear as water and washed of every stain. The air tasted of cold springs. A blackbird sang in the hedge with a sweetness he had never heard in any bird. He rose slowly to his knees and looked at the revolver on the gravel. It seemed to him a foolish, ugly little object, like a toy left out in the rain by some quarrelsome child.

He did not take it up. He remained kneeling, with his hands upon his thighs, and watched the light climb over the quiet world. For the first time in his life, he felt that he understood it.
