I.

In that portion of the county of Stafford which the geographer calls the Potteries, and which the inhabitants, with less exactness but greater feeling, call simply "the Five Towns," there rose, in the closing years of the last century, some thousand kilns whose smoke formed a permanent roof over the valley. Beneath this roof, in a street of blackened brick that descended toward the canal, lived William Leadford, a clerk in a pottery warehouse, aged twenty-three, who earned eighteen shillings a week and possessed a temper worth considerably more.

He loved Nettie Stuart. This was no secret. She was eighteen, grey-eyed, and light of step, the daughter of a gardener who had fallen into decline. William had loved her through three winters, and had resolved to marry her when he should be earning a pound a week. Unhappily, he had calculated everything except Mr. Edward Verrall.

Verrall was the son of a wealthy house at Checkshill, in the south, a young man of leisure whose motor-car, whose gloves, and whose fluent speech presented a spectacle that no warehouse clerk could rival. One October evening in the year of which I write, Leadford presented himself at the Stuart cottage and found it empty. A note lay on the table. It said, in Nettie's hand, that she was gone, that he must not follow, and that she was sorry.

Leadford read it three times. He measured the room with his eyes as though the dimensions of the disaster could be established by rule and compass. Then he sat down upon the stair and remained there until the lamp burned out.

II.

At that time the whole astronomical world was occupied with a body whose existence had been established some months earlier by the photographic plates of an observatory in the Southern Hemisphere. It was a comet. Its course, calculated with precision by the most eminent computers of Europe, showed that its nucleus would pass within a distance of the Earth's orbit hitherto unequaled in the annals of science.

The figures were these. The head measured, by estimate, some forty thousand kilometres in diameter. The tail, already visible as a faint plume, extended over many millions of leagues. As to the spectrum, the observers at Greenwich and Pulkovo reported a bright band in the green, which, after laborious comparisons, was attributed to a compound of carbon and nitrogen. Beyond this the learned could affirm nothing.

Leadford learned these particulars from a certain Parload, an assistant at a small observatory near Bursley, and an acquaintance of his schooldays. Parload was a thin man with a stoop, who regarded the heavens through a four-inch refractor and the earth not at all.

"It will be seen with the naked eye by Christmas," said Parload, one night, on the hill above the town. "You will not trouble about it, Leadford?"

"I have other business," said Leadford.

"Business!" cried the astronomer, gesturing at the sky. "Look! There is a thing that will alter the world, and you speak of business!"

Leadford looked, but saw only a pale smear among the stars, no larger than a thumbprint, and found it, for his part, quite contemptible.

III.

The year ran toward its close, and with it ran Europe, as a loaded wagon runs down a hill when the drag-chain has parted.

The newspapers, which Leadford read now with the dull rage of a man seeking his own wrongs in the wrongs of nations, were filled with ultimatums, mobilizations, and the launching of ironclads. The Powers confronted each other across the North Sea, and across the Rhine, with the stiffness of fencers who await only a pretext. The Admiralty ordered the construction of new cruisers; the Reichstag voted fresh credits; and in every capital, orators declared that peace was best preserved by the multiplication of cannon.

In the Potteries the effect was immediate. Orders from the Continent ceased. The warehouse reduced its staff, and on the first Monday of November, Leadford received a week's wages and a letter of regret. He walked out into a street full of idle men, who discussed with equal warmth the comet, the Kaiser, and the price of bread.

"There'll be war by spring," said a puddler, spitting.

"There'll be the end of the world by Christmas," said another, "if the parsons are right."

"Then let it come," said Leadford, and he heard in his own voice a harshness that startled him. For three weeks he had learned only that Nettie was lodged at Checkshill, in a cottage near the park, under the protection of Verrall. On that very evening, in a pawnbroker's shop on Hope Street, he spent eleven shillings on a revolver of American manufacture and a box of cartridges.

IV.

The distance from Hanley to the village of Checkshill is, by the railway, about a hundred and fifty miles; and the journey, which a Parisian would accomplish in four hours, occupies in England the better part of a day, with changes at Stafford, at Rugby, and in the grey sprawl of London.

Leadford travelled third class, in a compartment where the lamps gave a yellow light and the windows ran with moisture. Beside him, a commercial traveller in hosiery slept with his mouth open. Opposite, a woman wept silently into a shawl, and spoke to no one. Outside, the fields of Warwickshire, the cuttings of the Chilterns, and the lights of suburban stations passed like the images of a dream.

Toward midnight the clouds broke. Leadford, wiping the pane, saw the comet.

It was now a prodigy. Its head burned in the east with a cold, greenish radiance, brighter than Venus, and from it the tail swept upward across a third of the heavens, like the beam of some prodigious lighthouse. The fields beneath it had a strange pallor. The sleeping traveller woke, looked, and murmured a prayer. The weeping woman raised her head.

Leadford alone remained unmoved. He felt the weight of the revolver against his ribs and counted the cartridges in his mind: six. There were, he reflected with a certain exactness, precisely two persons to be dealt with. The arithmetic was satisfactory.

V.

He reached Checkshill at dawn, on foot, having walked the last nine miles from the railway. The village lay in a fold of the Surrey hills, among pines and heather. On its summit stood the mansion of the Verralls, a long façade of red brick whose windows reflected the fading comet like the eyes of some watchful animal.

There was a strange stillness in the air. No bird sang. The wind, which had blown from the south-west all the night, had fallen to a perfect calm. Leadford observed, without understanding, that the pines stood motionless as columns, and that the light, which should have strengthened with the sunrise, had instead taken on a faint tint of emerald.

Near the park gate, a cottage stood half hidden among laurels. A figure moved at the window. It was Nettie.

Leadford drew the revolver and advanced across the gravel. His step was firm; his hand did not tremble. At the moment he reached the porch the door opened, and Verrall himself appeared, in shirt-sleeves, pale, unshaven, and visibly amazed.

"Leadford!" he cried. "For God's sake, do you not see the sky?"

Leadford raised the weapon. But he saw that Verrall was not looking at him. He was looking upward, with an expression of terror and wonder, and Nettie, behind him, had fallen on her knees. Leadford turned his head.

The heavens were green.

VI.

It was not the green of foliage, nor of the sea; it was a pure, translucent emerald, such as no pigment has ever rendered, and it filled the whole vault from horizon to horizon. The sun rose through it, shrunken and strange. The comet's tail, which had for weeks stretched across the night, had enveloped the Earth, and the atmosphere, in its upper reaches, was already transformed.

Leadford felt the gas before he understood it. A fresh, keen odor reached his nostrils, comparable to nothing in his experience. He drew a breath, then another, and perceived with astonishment that his arm, which held the revolver, had grown heavy. The weapon slipped from his fingers and fell upon the gravel without a sound.

"Nettie," he said. He meant to say something more, but the word was all that remained.

He saw Verrall sink against the doorpost, his hand extended, and Nettie lying like a child asleep upon the step. He saw the laurels bow, though no wind stirred them. He saw, far off, a flight of rooks fall from the sky like stones, and the smoke of the village chimneys cease to rise. Then the green light closed over him, softly, as water closes over a diver.

Of what followed he knew nothing, for the space of an unmeasured time, neither hour nor day. There was neither dream nor pain. There was only a deep and tranquil darkness, in which the hatred he had carried from Staffordshire seemed to dissolve, grain by grain, like salt in a vessel.

When at last he opened his eyes, the sky above him was blue, of a depth and purity he had never seen, and the air was sweet beyond all description. He lay upon the grass, and the revolver beside him was a small and foolish thing of iron. He raised himself on one elbow. Nettie sat near him, awake, her face wet with tears, and in her eyes he found no shame and no fear. Verrall stood a little way off with bowed head. And Leadford understood, with a clarity that was almost painful, that the world he had known was ended, and that another was beginning.
