I.

In that district of Staffordshire which the potteries have blackened for a century, where the kilns lift their bottle-shaped chimneys above the clay-coloured streets like the masts of a motionless fleet, there lived in the autumn of the year in question a young man named William Leadford. He was twenty-two years of age, five feet seven inches in height, and in possession of a weekly wage of eighteen shillings, which he earned as a clerk in the counting-house of a firm of earthenware manufacturers. Of this sum, six went to his landlady, two to the tram and the tobacconist, and the remainder to those small ambitions of the poor, which are costly precisely because they are small.

His chief ambition was named Nettie Stanton. She was the daughter of a foreman, and she had grey eyes, a quick tongue, and a decided taste for the things that eighteen shillings could not buy.

On the evening of the fourteenth of October, Leadford presented himself at the Stantons' door at the usual hour of seven. He was received by the foreman, who looked at the floor, and by the foreman's wife, who looked at the ceiling. Nettie was not there. A note lay upon the mantelpiece, folded to the dimensions of a visiting card. It contained eleven lines. The name of Verrall, son of the landowner whose motor-car had lately been seen in the lanes of Clayton, appeared in the third.

Leadford read it twice, with the exactness of a clerk. Then he returned the paper to its place, bowed to the company, and went out into the street, where the smoke of two hundred kilns hung motionless in the damp air.

II.

While this small catastrophe was consuming one man in Staffordshire, a larger one was being calculated in the observatories of the world.

On the first of the preceding December, a photographic plate exposed at the Cape of Good Hope had recorded a faint nebulous speck in the constellation of the Southern Cross. It was nothing, a smudge of light of the twelfth magnitude. But astronomers are men who love smudges. Within a fortnight, the observatories of Greenwich, Pulkovo, and Lick had verified its motion; within a month, the orbit was computed.

The figures were these. The body was travelling at a velocity, relative to the Sun, of some forty miles in the second. Its nucleus, estimated at a few hundred leagues in diameter, trailed a tail whose length, when fully developed, would exceed forty millions of miles. And its path, projected with all the rigour of Newtonian mechanics, would cross the orbit of the Earth at a point which our planet would reach in the last days of the coming month.

The spectroscope added a further curiosity. In the light of the comet's tail, besides the familiar lines of carbon and hydrogen, there appeared a band, green and unclassified, which corresponded to no element known upon the Earth. The learned men of Paris named it provisionally X; those of Berlin said it was merely an error of the instrument; those of Cambridge wrote nothing, which in Cambridge signifies grave reflection.

The newspapers, of course, were less reserved. They printed diagrams. They printed the opinions of bishops. And they printed, in the columns adjoining, other intelligence which the public, by a singular perversity, found infinitely more absorbing.

III.

For Europe, in that same autumn, was drifting like a ship without a helmsman toward the rocks.

The facts were as plain as the multiplication table. Germany had laid down her ninth great battleship in four years. France had lengthened her military service by twelve months. Russia was building strategic railways toward her western frontier at the rate of eleven miles in the week. In the Balkans, a quarrel about a boundary which no one in London could have found on a map had brought four armies to within sight of one another. And in every capital, ministers were declaring in measured tones that nothing was further from their wishes than war, while their colleagues of the War Office were requisitioning horses.

In the pottery towns, the effect of this agitation was singular. Trade slackened; the great kilns were fed less often; the men talked in the public-houses of an approaching struggle with a mixture of dread and a curious, sullen eagerness, as of men who are tired of their own lives and would welcome any change, even a fatal one.

Leadford shared this sentiment, though for reasons peculiar to himself. He had been dismissed from the counting-house for an inattention which his employers attributed to the political situation and which was in truth the work of a single grey-eyed girl. For nine days he walked the streets of Hanley and Burslem without any definite object. On the tenth, he received a letter. It bore a London postmark and a handwriting he knew.

IV.

The letter was brief. Nettie wrote that she was well; that she did not expect forgiveness; that Mr. Verrall had taken a house for her, and that she begged William to think of her no more. She gave no address. But the envelope, of heavy cream paper, bore on its flap the embossed name of a stationer in Maidstone, in the county of Kent.

A man of a more philosophical temper would have burned the paper. Leadford, who was not philosophical, calculated. He possessed four pounds, eleven shillings and some pence, the remains of his savings. The third-class fare from Stoke to London was eleven shillings and fourpence; from London to Maidstone, three more. There remained, therefore, a sum which would suffice either for a week's lodging or for an instrument of a different description.

He chose the instrument.

In a pawnbroker's shop in Hanley, between a concertina and a tray of wedding-rings, lay a revolver of the American pattern, with a six-chambered cylinder and a barrel of four inches. The pawnbroker, a prudent man, asked no question; he asked only seventeen shillings, and added a box of cartridges for three more. Leadford counted out the coins with a hand which, he observed with detached interest, did not tremble.

That night he took the train. As it drew out beneath the glare of the furnaces, he looked from the window and perceived, low in the south-west, a pale and slender streak, like the stroke of a chalk upon a blackboard. It was the comet. It had grown, in a month, to the length of the Moon's diameter; and it seemed, to his exhausted mind, to be pointing directly at him.

V.

The journey occupied the night and the better part of the morning. At Euston, Leadford breakfasted on a roll and a cup of tea; at London Bridge, he was jostled by a crowd of reservists hurrying to their depots, their boxes upon their shoulders and their faces curiously gay; at Maidstone, he arrived at about three in the afternoon, under a sky of lead.

The town was full of soldiers. A regiment of yeomanry was watering its horses at the river; placards on the walls proclaimed the calling out of the Territorials; and on the pavement before the post-office, a small crowd was reading a telegram which announced that Germany had presented an ultimatum.

Leadford passed through them without a glance. By a fortunate chance, the stationer whose name he had read upon the envelope was a talkative man; and for the price of a box of pens, Leadford learned that a young gentleman and lady, answering to the description, had taken a villa upon the high ground two miles to the north, near a hamlet called Lenham Heath, and that the young gentleman was reputed to be wealthy and the young lady to be unhappy.

He set out on foot at four o'clock. The road climbed between hedges of hawthorn and oast-houses with their white cowls; and as the sun declined, the air assumed a strange stillness, as though the whole country were holding its breath. The birds fell silent one by one. The cattle in the fields huddled together with their heads to the ground. And in the east, above the dark line of the Weald, the sky began to take on a tint he had never seen before, a delicate and disquieting green.

VI.

At seven o'clock, Leadford stood before the villa.

It was a modest house of red brick, embowered in laurels, with a lamp already lit behind the curtains of the lower room. A gravel path, forty paces in length, led from the gate to the door. Leadford counted the paces. He had drawn the revolver from his pocket and held it against his thigh, and the metal was warm from his hand.

Above him the heavens were transformed. The comet's tail, now spread across a third of the firmament, had passed beyond the stage of a mere streak; it was an immense curtain of pale emerald fire, rippling in slow undulations from the horizon to the zenith, and casting upon the earth a light in which the laurels, the gravel, and his own outstretched hand appeared of the colour of the sea. No wind stirred. No sound came from the village. Only, far away to the south, there rolled a dull and continuous murmur, which a trained ear might have taken for the first guns of a European war, and which was perhaps no more than the blood in his own temples.

He reached the door. He raised his hand to the knocker. And at that instant, the green light deepened to a brilliance so intense that the lamp in the window paled to a speck. A faint sweet odour, like that of newly-cut grass, rose from the ground. Leadford drew a breath of it, and then another, and felt the pistol grow strangely heavy in his fingers, as if the whole weight of his anger were running out through them into the earth.

He staggered, put out a hand to the doorpost, and the world went softly, wholly green.
