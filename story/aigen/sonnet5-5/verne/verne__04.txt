I. The Potteries

On the evening of the fourteenth of October, in the year of which I write, the kilns of Clayton in Staffordshire were sending their sulphurous smoke toward a sky already heavy with rain. William Leadford, a clerk in a pottery-works, who earned twenty-six shillings a week and read more than was good for a man of his condition, climbed the narrow stair of his lodging in Brick Street. A letter lay upon the deal table.

It was brief, and its handwriting was that of Nettie Stuart, the young woman whom he had loved from boyhood, and to whom he had, with the awkward constancy of the poor, devoted every thought. She had gone, said the letter. She had gone with Mr. Verrall, the son of a family whose carriage had for some months been seen in the neighbourhood. She asked to be forgiven, and begged him not to follow.

Leadford read the page three times. Then, without a word, he folded it into his pocket-book, extinguished the candle, and sat in the darkness. Below, the clogs of the potters clattered on the setts; a train whistled toward Stoke. Of the tumult within the young man's breast, neither the street nor the town knew anything. Such passions, however, are not mastered by silence; they are merely stored, as steam is stored in a boiler whose valve has been wedged.

II. The Stranger in the Heavens

While Leadford brooded, the astronomers of Europe were directing their instruments toward a point in the constellation of Leo. A faint object, first photographed at the observatory of Cambridge, had been found to advance at a rate which the calculators at Greenwich estimated at some two million miles in the day. It was a comet; and, unlike the venerable wanderers whose returns are inscribed in the tables of Halley and Encke, it had come from the abyss of space to which no record reached.

The spectroscope, that marvellous interrogator of light, had revealed within the nucleus a green band, which the learned declared to be the signature of cyanogen, a gas which, in a laboratory flask, is sufficient to destroy life. The newspapers, with the prudence of those who sell sensation, assured their readers that the tenuity of a comet's tail was such that the whole of it might be contained in a thimble.

Yet the public ear was less attentive to the skies than to the earth. In Berlin, in Paris, in London, ministers exchanged notes of increasing frigidity. Fleets were manoeuvring; reservists were counted; the Balkans smouldered. Europe, which for forty years had boasted of its civilisation, appeared like a vast powder-magazine in which each nation held a lantern and accused its neighbour of carelessness.

III. The Purchase

Three days later, Leadford had left his employment. He had no clear plan, but he knew where Verrall's family house lay: a villa of red brick and ornamental gables on the slopes beyond Trentham, some eleven miles to the south.

In a pawnbroker's shop in Hanley he purchased, for the sum of two pounds seven shillings, a revolver of American manufacture, six chambers, with a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker, a bald man with a squint, regarded him curiously.

"For rats, I suppose?" said he.

"For rats," replied Leadford.

He placed the weapon in his coat. Whether he would employ it upon Verrall, upon Nettie, upon himself, or upon none, he could not have said. Anger in such a mind resembles the needle of a compass near a magnet: it trembles, swings, and settles nowhere. He took the afternoon train. Through the dripping window he saw the blast-furnaces, the slag-heaps, and the canals of that blackened region pass in review; and when at last the country opened into pasture, he perceived above the western hills a pale smear in the twilight. It was the comet. Already it equalled in brilliance the brightest star, and its tail had begun to lengthen like the stroke of a pale brush.

IV. The Road to Trentham

He alighted at a small station, where a single porter, reading by the light of a lamp, informed him that war was spoken of in the morning papers.

"With whom?" asked Leadford, indifferently.

"With Germany, sir, they say. Or it may be about the Dutch. I did not rightly read it."

Leadford walked on. The road, deep in mud, wound between hedges that gleamed with rain. The comet was now directly overhead, and so remarkable had its aspect become that labourers stood in the gateways of the fields with their faces upturned. A nucleus of greenish white, as large as a dinner-plate, trailed a vapour which extended nearly across a quarter of the firmament. By its light a man might have read the milestone, which declared: Trentham, 3 miles.

An old shepherd, leaning on his crook, addressed him with the freedom of rural solitude.

"They say it'll be here Tuesday, master. They say it'll touch us."

"Who says so?"

"Them as know."

Leadford said nothing. Inwardly he marvelled that a world on the brink of conflagration, and perhaps of a celestial catastrophe, could occupy his mind so little. The face of Nettie, laughing, with a strand of hair across her cheek, effaced the universe.

V. The Villa

At nine o'clock in the evening he stood before the iron gates of the villa. Lights burned in two of the windows. Through the shrubbery he could discern the silhouette of a conservatory, and within it, moving among the ferns, a figure which he recognised by its carriage before he could see the features. It was Nettie.

At her side was a young man in a light overcoat, who bent toward her as one speaks to a creature that is possessed. Verrall! Leadford's hand closed upon the butt of the revolver. He had calculated, upon the road, the distance at which a pistol of that calibre might be reliable: twelve yards, perhaps fifteen. The conservatory was no more than ten from where he crouched, behind a laurel.

Yet he did not raise the weapon. Some obscure prudence, or perhaps cowardice, or perhaps a final remnant of tenderness, restrained him. He saw Nettie lift her face to the sky; he saw Verrall follow her glance. Both stood motionless, and the light of the comet fell upon the glass of the conservatory, turning it to a sheet of pale emerald.

From the direction of the town there came, thin and far, the sound of a bugle, and then of shouting. The reservists were being summoned. In the stillness that followed, Leadford observed that the birds, which had been chattering in the shrubbery, had fallen silent, all at once, like an orchestra obedient to a baton.

VI. The Green Dawn

It was now eleven o'clock, and the heavens presented a spectacle that no human eye had previously beheld. The comet had grown to the dimensions of a vast bow, which spanned the zenith from the north-west to the south-east; and the stars, one after another, were being extinguished behind a luminous veil. The air itself seemed altered. A stillness settled upon the park, not that of night but of a held breath; the leaves, the grass, the very raindrops on the laurels were tinged with a delicate and unearthly green.

Leadford rose from his hiding-place. The revolver, heavy as a stone, hung in his hand. Through the glass he saw that Verrall and Nettie were no longer speaking. They had turned toward the window, and their eyes, widened and strange, were fixed upon the sky with an expression neither of fear nor of triumph, but of a profound and solemn astonishment.

Then Leadford perceived, high above the elms, a pale drifting mist, which descended slowly, as a veil falls from the hand of a bride. It was not smoke; it was not cloud. Where it touched the topmost branches, they seemed to glisten. A faint, sweetish odour reached him, like that of crushed almond. He drew a breath; and with the breath came a singular lightness, a sensation of such sudden and impossible clarity that he thought for an instant he must be dying.

The revolver slipped from his fingers and fell silently into the wet grass. He did not stoop to recover it. He lifted his face to the descending radiance, while far away, on the plains of Europe, the guns that were to have spoken first fell mute, and the great green silence of the comet gathered all the world into its keeping.
