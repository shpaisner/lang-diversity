I.

It was on the evening of the 9th of October, in the Potteries of North Staffordshire, that William Leadford learned that Nettie Stuart had left Clayhanger. The town lay under its habitual canopy of smoke, drawn out of two hundred bottle-kilns, and the lamps were already lit along the Trent road when the letter came into his hands. It was brief: four lines in a hand he knew too well. She had gone, it said, with Mr. Verrall, the son of the owner of the Verrall Works, and she begged that he would not follow her.

Leadford, twenty-two years old, a clerk at nineteen shillings a week, read it standing in his lodging-house passage. The gas-jet hissed. In the kitchen a clock struck seven. He folded the paper in four, then in eight, as if by reducing its dimensions he might reduce its meaning, and went upstairs without a word to his landlady.

Let it be said plainly that he was neither a hero nor a villain, but a young man of ordinary education and extraordinary pride, whom poverty had made sullen. He had loved Nettie for four years. He had calculated, with the precision of a bookkeeper, the day on which his wages might support a household. Now the figures were useless. He sat on the edge of his iron bed until midnight, and in the darkness a single sentiment, hard and bright as a pottery glaze, formed itself in his mind: that Verrall must answer for this.

II.

While this small tragedy was enacted in a Staffordshire attic, the heavens were preparing a larger spectacle.

On the 28th of the previous month, Professor Hartmann at the observatory of Heidelberg had photographed, in the constellation of Draco, a faint nebulous body not catalogued in any chart. Within a fortnight the telegraph had carried the discovery to Greenwich, Pulkovo, and Harvard. The calculations were made; the elements of the orbit were verified; and the conclusion, announced with the sober reserve of science, was this: that the object was a comet, moving at a speed of some thirty-five miles in a second, and that its path would carry it within a very small distance of the Earth.

The newspapers, of course, outran the astronomers. Within a week the comet was a topic in every public-house of the Five Towns. Leadford heard it discussed by the men at the Works, who laid wagers upon its tail as they might upon a horse. Some said it would burn the world; others, that it would poison the air; a Methodist preacher in Hanley declared that it was the besom of the Almighty.

Leadford listened to none of it. Each night, nevertheless, he walked upon the hill above the town, and there, between the chimneys and the stars, he perceived the visitor: a small and greenish smear, hardly larger than a thumbnail, yet growing, evening by evening, with a patient and mathematical certainty.

III.

The state of Europe, meanwhile, resembled that of a boiler whose safety-valve has been secretly screwed down.

It would have required a better political economist than Leadford to explain why the Powers armed themselves. Yet the facts were visible to any reader of the penny press. Germany had increased her fleet; England replied with new dreadnoughts; the Balkan frontier bristled with bayonets; and the great Empires, linked by treaties as a chain is linked by iron rings, awaited only the failure of a single link. In the Staffordshire coalfields, the colliers spoke of conscription. In the Works, orders for table-ware dwindled, and orders for shell-casings, mysteriously, did not.

On the 14th of October, Leadford was dismissed. The firm of Verrall was "reducing its establishment." He received his wages, one pound four and sixpence, and he understood perfectly well that the elder Verrall had read his name in the matter of the son, and had found a convenient economy.

He walked home along the canal. The water, black and oily, reflected the comet, now visible even in twilight, and he regarded it without fear. What was the destruction of a world to a man who had already lost his own? A dull, almost scientific curiosity possessed him. He wondered whether, should the Earth indeed be struck, Verrall and Nettie would perish together, and the thought gave him no satisfaction at all: he wished to be present.

IV.

Information as to the fugitives was not difficult to obtain. A cousin of Nettie's, a dressmaker in Burslem, supplied, for the price of two shillings and a promise of discretion, the address of a villa in the south of England, where Verrall had installed his companion.

Leadford resolved to go. His capital amounted to three pounds seventeen shillings. From a pawnbroker in Shelton he purchased, for fifteen of these, a revolver of American manufacture, with six chambers and a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker, a precise old man with spectacles, tested the mechanism before him, and observed that the weapon was in excellent order.

"It will not miss," he said.

"No," replied Leadford, "I do not suppose it will."

The journey occupied the greater part of two days, partly by rail, partly on foot, the trains being retarded by the movement of troops and the requisition of rolling stock. At Crewe he saw soldiers sleeping on the platform among their rifles. At Rugby a newspaper declared that an ultimatum had been dispatched. At every station men stood in groups, looking upward, and the comet, by the night of the 17th, had grown to the dimensions of a great luminous fan, spread across a quarter of the sky, its nucleus a burning emerald, its tail streaming outward like a banner unfurled by some invisible wind.

V.

On the 18th, towards evening, he reached the country of the Downs.

The landscape was singular. The sun had set in a copper sky, but the darkness that followed was not darkness; the comet shed a pale green radiance that robbed the fields of colour and turned the hedgerows to ghosts. Birds had fallen silent. Cattle stood motionless in the meadows, their heads lifted toward the zenith. Not a breath of wind stirred.

Astronomers have since recorded the phenomena of that night with admirable exactitude: the sudden fall of the barometer, the disturbance of every compass in Europe, the strange spectrum, in which the bands of carbon were joined by a bright line unknown to any laboratory. Leadford knew nothing of these things. He knew only that his heart beat heavily, that the road was long, and that the revolver lay against his thigh with a weight out of all proportion to its size.

At a crossing he met an old shepherd, who stood gazing into the sky with his crook clasped to his breast.

"It's the end, young man," said the shepherd, quite calmly.

"Perhaps," Leadford answered. "How far to Hollingwood House?"

The old man pointed along the lane, and said that it was a mile, no more; and Leadford went forward, bareheaded, under that unnatural heaven.

VI.

The villa stood upon a slope, white and square, among cedars. A single window glowed yellow, an ordinary human light, diminished by the green splendour above. Leadford halted at the gate. The revolver was in his hand; he did not remember drawing it.

At that moment the whole sky changed. The nucleus of the comet, which for an hour had hung low in the west, seemed to dilate; the tail swept across the zenith; and from the vault of heaven descended a vapour, pale, luminous, and shot with threads of emerald, which settled upon the earth as silently as snow. It lay upon the cedars. It crept along the lane like a tide. Leadford saw it flow across the gravel toward his boots, and felt, with the clarity of a man observing an experiment upon himself, that it was not a thing to be feared.

He lifted his head. Far away to the north-east, beyond the Channel, a red glow trembled on the horizon, and a low, continuous murmur, that might have been thunder or the first guns of war, rolled over the Downs and was lost. Then the murmur ceased, as though hushed by a great hand.

The air entered his lungs sweet and cold, like the water of a mountain spring. The weapon grew heavy, heavier; his fingers loosened; it dropped upon the gravel without a sound. He thought of Nettie, and of Verrall, and of the long, bitter arithmetic of his grievance, and found that the figures, one by one, were dissolving. A strange peace, vast as the sky, flowed into his limbs.

"What is this?" he whispered.

No answer came. The green light closed over him, the stars went out, and William Leadford sank upon his knees in the roadway, and then lay down, as a child lies down, and slept.
