I. The Potteries

On the evening of the fourteenth of October, in the last years of the old order, William Leadford walked home along the road that descends from the heights above Bursley into the smoking valley of the Trent. Below him, in the dusk, the bottle-shaped kilns of the Staffordshire Potteries thrust their black throats toward heaven, and the furnaces, scarcely a hundred paces apart, painted the low clouds a sullen orange. Leadford was twenty-two, a clerk of small wages and smaller prospects, lean in body and bitter in mind.

He carried in his pocket a letter. It was short, written in a round, schoolgirl hand that he knew better than his own:

Dear William, do not look for me. I am going with Mr. Verrall, and you must not think too hardly of me. N.

Nettie Stewart, the gardener's daughter, whose slender figure he had cherished through three winters of hopeless economy, had quitted her father's cottage in the night. She had gone in the motor-car of Verrall, the heir of the great house, whose fortune was reckoned in acres and whose conscience, it was said, in nothing at all.

Leadford read the letter beneath a gas-lamp, then a second time, then crushed it in his fist. He did not weep. A man of his temper converts grief into a harder metal. When he reached his lodging, he sat for three hours without lighting a candle, and the only sound in the room was the distant clangor of the rolling-mills, which did not pause for the sorrows of any man.

II. The Stranger in the Heavens

It was in the same month that the astronomers of Europe first agreed upon the existence of a new body in the constellation of Leo. Discovered by photographic plate, it was at first a mere smudge of light, some three hundred millions of kilometres distant. Calculation soon showed its path to be a very elongated ellipse, and the ephemeris computed at Greenwich and Paris placed its nearest approach to the terrestrial orbit within a few months.

The spectroscope, that faithful interrogator of the stars, yielded a singular answer. Among the familiar lines of carbon and hydrogen appeared a band of green, which Professor Hartmann at Potsdam attributed, with some hesitation, to an unknown gas of extraordinary tenuity. The newspapers, abandoning their customary indifference to science, announced it as "The Green Comet."

Leadford, however, took no interest in it. He read of it, if at all, with the contempt of a man whose private universe has fallen to pieces. What were the cyanogen and the hydrogen of a distant wanderer to him, when Nettie was lost?

Yet each night, when he left the dim office where he copied invoices, he saw it a little larger above the chimneys of the Five Towns, a pale and baleful thread, like a scratch upon dark glass. And a strange foreboding accompanied him, which he could not name.

III. Parload's Telescope

There lived at that time, in a cottage near the hill of Clayton, a young man named Parload, an enthusiast of astronomy, who had fitted up in his garden a telescope of modest aperture. He was Leadford's friend, though a friend of odd, abstracted habits. One clear November night he invited the clerk to the instrument.

"Look, Leadford," said he. "Observe the head. It is not a solid body, but a cloud of vapour, within which a nucleus gleams. Observe the tail! In twelve weeks it will be one hundred millions of kilometres long, and the earth will pass through its attenuated substance."

"And what then?" asked Leadford, applying his eye to the glass.

"That I cannot tell. Some say nothing; some say a conflagration. I say only that the spectrum of this gas is unlike any known to chemistry, and that it will mingle with our atmosphere."

Leadford beheld the greenish glimmer, trembling in the field of the telescope. Then he turned away.

"Meanwhile," said he, "the world has other business. Have you read the Continental telegrams?"

Parload had, indeed. The press was full of ultimatums, mobilisations, and frontier incidents. The Great Powers, like men descending a dark staircase, were feeling for steps that were not there. Fleets were gathering in the North Sea; the Balkans smoked; and in every capital, ministers spoke of peace in the voice of those who prepare for war.

IV. The Pursuit

By December Leadford had been dismissed from his employment for insolence, and having nothing left to lose, he determined to find the lovers. Information, purchased with his last savings from a discharged servant of the Verrall household, directed him to a villa on the southern coast, where the fugitives were reported to be living.

He took the night train. Beside him sat a commercial traveller, who discoursed learnedly upon the probability of war, and a clergyman, who maintained that the comet was a divine admonition. Leadford answered neither. At a pawnbroker's in the Euston Road, where the train deposited him at dawn, he bought a small revolver and six cartridges, and weighed the weapon in his hand as he had once weighed the ledger of his hopes.

"Is it for sport, sir?" inquired the pawnbroker.

"For justice," replied Leadford.

The man looked at him, and discreetly gave him a discount.

All that day Leadford wandered through the fog of London, a city that seemed to him populated by sleepwalkers. Crowds besieged the newspaper offices. Regiments marched toward the stations with bands playing. At night, above the lamps, the comet shone with a distinct emerald tinge, and its tail, measured by Parload's reckoning, now embraced a third of the sky.

V. The Green Dawn

On the morning that the earth was to enter the tail, a great stillness fell upon the land. The Continental telegrams had announced the first shots on the frontier; the evening journals proclaimed war. Leadford, having traced the lovers to a seaside lodging, found the house empty, the door open, and only a woman's glove upon the stair.

He wandered out upon the cliffs. Beneath him the sea lay motionless as oil, and the sky had assumed a hue which no painter has recorded: a pale, luminous green, as if the whole vault were a vast sheet of glass lit from behind. The air itself seemed thinner and sweeter. Gulls hung in it without a cry.

Along the road below, a column of soldiers, newly mobilised, halted without command. The men stared upward. A horse sank on its knees. A bugle fell from a hand.

Leadford drew the revolver from his pocket and found that his fingers had no strength to hold it. A languor, gentle and irresistible, ascended through his limbs. He thought, quite clearly, of Nettie, of Verrall, of the smoking kilns of Bursley, and then these images ceased to have weight. The weapon slipped from his palm and rattled upon the chalk.

"So," he murmured, "it is the end."

He sank upon the turf, and the green light closed over him like water.

VI. The Awakening

How long he lay there, Leadford could not at first determine. It was the sensation of cold upon his cheek which recalled him, and the sound of a lark, a sound he had not heard since childhood. He opened his eyes.

The sky was of a blue so profound, so transparent, that it seemed newly made. The green had vanished entirely. The sun, rising over the sea, flooded the cliffs with a light of astonishing purity, and every blade of grass bore a drop of dew that shone like a gem. He sat up. His head was clear, clearer than it had ever been, and he felt within him a lightness, as though some old and rusted machinery had been cleansed and oiled.

He looked at the revolver lying in the chalk. It appeared to him a poor, ugly, foolish thing. A little way off, the soldiers were rising from the road, rubbing their eyes, speaking to one another in low, wondering voices. Not one reached for his rifle. A young officer was helping a trooper to his feet; he laughed, and the trooper laughed with him, and neither knew why.

Leadford rose and walked to the edge of the cliff. Beneath him the sea sparkled; beyond, far off, the coast of France lay distinct as a map. And in his heart, where hatred of Verrall had burned for so many weeks, he discovered only a great and tranquil pity, for Verrall, for Nettie, and above all for himself, who had been so long a prisoner of his own narrow rage.

"The gas," he said aloud. "Parload said it would mingle with the air."

He understood, dimly yet surely, that the comet had altered the very substance of the atmosphere, and that with it something had altered in the minds of men. The old quarrels, the old frontiers, the old jealousies, all seemed like the pasteboard scenery of a theatre from which the lights had been withdrawn.

He picked up the revolver, and with a steady arm hurled it far out over the cliff. It turned once, glittering, and fell without a sound into the sea. Then William Leadford, poor clerk of Staffordshire, turned inland toward the waking world.
