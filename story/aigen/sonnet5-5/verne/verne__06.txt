I.

In the autumn of that year, which the almanacs of the period record as the last of the old order, the district of the Five Towns in Staffordshire presented to the traveller an aspect at once sombre and laborious. Some two hundred bottle-shaped kilns, each vomiting its column of sulphurous smoke, rose above a landscape of slag-heaps, canals the colour of tar, and brick terraces blackened by half a century of coal. In one of these terraces, at a rent of six shillings and sixpence a week, lived William Leadford, clerk to a manufacturer of earthenware, aged twenty-two, possessed of a good head, a bad coat, and a hopeless ambition.

On the evening of which I speak, Leadford climbed the hill behind Clayton. His friend Parload, a clerk like himself but an astronomer by passion, had set up on that elevation a refracting telescope of three inches' aperture, constructed largely of cardboard and optical glass purchased in instalments.

"Look," said Parload, "and tell me what you see."

Leadford applied his eye. Among the stars of the southern sky there floated a small, pale disc, faintly nebulous, trailing the suggestion of a tail.

"It is a comet," said Parload, "discovered three months ago. Its distance is now about a hundred and ten million miles. It approaches at a velocity of some twenty miles in the second, and by my reckoning, which I have verified four times, it will pass within a few thousand leagues of the Earth before the end of the year."

"And what will happen?" asked Leadford.

"Nothing, say the newspapers. Something, say I."

Leadford raised his head. Beneath him the furnaces glowed red across the valley, and his thoughts, indifferent to celestial mechanics, returned to Nettie.

II.

Nettie Stuart was nineteen years old, the daughter of a gardener, and she had been the object of Leadford's love since both were children at the Board School. It must be confessed that his courtship had been conducted less in words than in resolutions: he intended to rise, to earn, to marry. Her father's cottage stood a mile beyond the last chimney, in the grounds of Lady Verrall's park at Checkshill.

It was there that the misfortune arose. Lady Verrall's son, Mr. Eadward Verrall, a young man of twenty-five, elegant, indolent and abundantly provided with money, had returned from Cambridge and had observed the gardener's daughter. Of the progress of this acquaintance Leadford knew nothing until the afternoon of the fourteenth of October, when he received at his office a note in a hand he knew. It contained eleven lines. Their substance may be given in a sentence: Nettie, wearied of poverty, begged him to forget her, and had left that morning for London in the company of Mr. Verrall.

Leadford read this document three times. Then he folded it with extraordinary care, placed it in his pocket-book, and resumed his copying of invoices. A casual observer would have perceived no change save that the pen, in forming the figure seven, pierced the paper.

At six o'clock he walked home through the rain. The gas lamps, haloed in the mist, gave to the streets the aspect of a submerged city. In the room he rented he sat without light until midnight, and when at last he rose, it was with a face from which all youth had been withdrawn, and in which a resolution, cold and exact, had taken its place.

III.

During the weeks that followed, the cosmos and the continent conspired alike to deepen his gloom.

The comet, which in September had been a curiosity, had in November become a portent. Its head now measured, according to the observatories of Greenwich and Paris, nearly forty thousand miles in diameter; its tail extended across a fifth of the heavens. Spectroscopic analysis, practised by Parload on a rented instrument, revealed in the light of the visitor a hitherto unknown band of green, which the learned of Europe attributed variously to cyanogen, to hydrocarbons, and to an element wholly new.

"It is a gas," said Parload, pale with excitement. "Whatever its nature, the Earth will plunge through the tail of this body. Our atmosphere will mix with it."

"Will it poison us?"

"The Astronomer Royal says no. Others say yes. The honest answer is that no man knows."

Meanwhile, in the chancelleries, the same indifference to heaven prevailed. Germany and England quarrelled over the markets of the world. Fleets of iron were laid down at Kiel and Portsmouth; the Balkan frontier bristled with bayonets; in the newspapers the word ultimatum appeared with a frequency that no prudent reader could mistake. Armies of six million men awaited merely a signal. Leadford, who read these tidings in the smoky light of a public library, felt in them a strange accord with his own spirit. The world, like himself, was preparing for a murder.

IV.

On the evening of the second of December, Leadford entered a pawnbroker's shop in Hanley, and for the sum of thirty-five shillings, the whole of his savings, acquired a revolver of American manufacture, six chambers, calibre .38, with a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker, a prudent man, inquired no further than the payment.

He then wrote to his employer a brief resignation, settled his lodgings, and addressed a last letter to Parload, who was at that moment busy with his lenses. He did not mention Nettie. He wrote only: I am going to London. If the comet strikes us, I shall not be sorry.

At the railway station of Stoke, the clerks, the porters and the passengers moved under the oppression of that universal anxiety which precedes either a catastrophe or a miracle. Placards announced that the Reserve might shortly be called out. A newsboy cried the latest despatch from Vienna. Beside the booking office a group of workmen debated the question of the comet with the gravity of theologians, and one, an old potter with white lungs, declared that the end of the world was at hand and that he, for one, would not regret it.

Leadford took his seat in a third-class carriage. As the train drew out, he passed his hand over the weapon concealed within his coat, and for a moment, through the window, the sky above the kilns appeared to him strangely luminous, as though lit from within by a pale and unaccustomed dawn.

V.

London, which he reached after midnight, was a vast cauldron of lamps and fog. In a coffee-house near Euston he learned, from a talkative cabman, that Mr. Verrall had been seen with a young person at a villa on the heights of Surrey, near Shere. The information cost him a shilling. The journey cost him another day.

He arrived at that village on the evening of the sixth. The weather, until then overcast, had cleared with a singular abruptness. A bitter frost prevailed. Along the lanes the hedges stood stiff and white, and above the hills the comet hung in a sky that had assumed an unnatural tint, a green of astonishing delicacy, such as no painter has rendered and no poet named.

He observed, as he walked, the behaviour of the villagers. They stood at their doors in silence, looking upward. A dog howled somewhere toward the Downs. A clock struck nine, and its note was oddly muffled, as though the air itself had thickened. Leadford remembered Parload's computation: the Earth was to enter the tail of the comet that very night, between ten and eleven o'clock.

At a turn of the road he perceived, beyond a plantation of firs, the lighted windows of a villa. Through the trees came the sound of a piano. He recognised the air; it was one that Nettie had sung in the cottage at Checkshill. His fingers closed upon the butt of the revolver, and he advanced.

VI.

The gate was unlatched. Leadford crossed the lawn, whose frozen grass cracked beneath his boots, and arrived under the window of the drawing-room. The curtains were imperfectly drawn. Within, in the glow of a lamp, he beheld Nettie seated at the instrument, and behind her, with one hand upon the back of her chair, Verrall, smiling, careless, handsome, entirely unaware of the figure in the garden.

Leadford drew the revolver. The weapon was heavy and very cold. He raised it, and the barrel, he noticed, wavered against the pane.

At that instant the light changed.

It was not a sudden thing. The sky, already green, deepened toward an emerald of overwhelming brilliance; the stars vanished; the very frost upon the grass glittered like crystallised verdigris. A great stillness fell upon the earth. Even the wind, which had stirred faintly among the firs, ceased. Then, from the horizon, there advanced across the heavens a pale vapour, delicate as gauze, which seemed to descend upon the world with the gentleness of a veil.

Leadford drew breath, and the air was strange: sweet, cool, and curiously vivifying, like that of a mountain summit at dawn. A tremor passed through his limbs. The revolver slipped from his fingers and fell noiselessly into the grass. Within the room, he saw the music cease; he saw Nettie turn, and Verrall sink slowly into a chair.

"So it has come," murmured Leadford, and the words seemed to issue from a great distance.

His knees yielded. He lay upon the frozen lawn, beneath a sky of green fire, and the last thing that he perceived was not rage, nor sorrow, but an immense and incomprehensible peace descending upon all the sleeping world.
