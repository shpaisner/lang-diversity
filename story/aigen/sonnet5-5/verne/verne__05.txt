I.

On the evening of the 9th of October, in a year which the historian will find it convenient to leave unnamed, Professor Marhold of the observatory at Greenwich pointed his great equatorial toward a region of the heavens lying some four degrees south of the star Delta Aquarii. There, upon the photographic plate, he found a small, hazy, greenish stain that had not been there a month before.

Its elements were soon calculated. It was a comet, travelling at a velocity of about forty-two kilometres a second, at a distance from the earth of some one hundred and ten million leagues, and diminishing that distance by roughly two million leagues a day. Its nucleus was scarcely distinguishable. Its spectrum, however, showed bands that no known substance could account for: a line of green, singular and brilliant, which the spectroscopists of Europe disputed for weeks without agreement.

The learned world was moved, the public a little amused. The journals printed diagrams; the music-halls produced comic songs.

Yet it is not with the heavens that this narrative has first to do, but with the earth, and with a very small portion of it: that district of Staffordshire known as the Potteries, where, under a sky perpetually dimmed by the smoke of a thousand bottle-shaped kilns, the clay of England is turned into porcelain and the lives of men into something much coarser. It is there that we must find our hero, William Leadford, a clerk of twenty-two years, who owned in the world one black suit, nine shillings a week, and a hopeless, consuming ambition to be something other than he was.

II.

Leadford lodged in a narrow house in Clayton, number 14, in a street composed of forty-two dwellings exactly alike. His mother, a washerwoman, had died the previous spring. His father, a potter's painter, had perished long before of the lead that lurks in the glazes. Of family he retained only a few ill-assorted memories; of education, a smattering acquired in night-classes; of hope, a single possession, the love he bore to Nettie Stuart.

She was the daughter of a gardener and was eighteen years old. She had brown hair, a quick, restless eye, and a mind which, though untaught, was perpetually in search of brighter horizons than those afforded by Clayton. Leadford had loved her since boyhood. On Sunday afternoons they walked together upon the high ground beyond the town, whence the whole smoking basin of the Potteries lay spread below them like a vast cauldron. There he spoke of the day when he should rise, by study, to a position worthy of her.

Nettie listened and said little. Her silence, which he took for tenderness, was in truth the silence of one calculating. The position of which he spoke was, by the most generous reckoning, twenty years distant.

Then, in the early autumn, a new figure appeared upon the scene: Mr. Verrall, the son of a wealthy house at Lord Redcar's estate, young, well-dressed, and idle, who had taken to riding past the gardener's cottage at the hour when Nettie hung out her linen.

III.

The catastrophe, when it came, was accomplished with a rapidity worthy of a mechanical contrivance. On the morning of the 23rd, Leadford presented himself at the cottage and found the gardener, a heavy man, standing at the gate with a letter shaking in his hand. Nettie had gone. She had left at dawn, by the 6:40 train for London, in the company of Mr. Verrall; and the note, written in a hand that Leadford knew as he knew his own, said merely that she was sorry, that she could not endure the life before her, and that she would be happy.

For some minutes Leadford stood motionless. Then he walked, without hat, without purpose, to the high ground where he and Nettie had so often stood. The Potteries lay beneath him as always, grey, smoking, indifferent. And above them, though it was scarcely three in the afternoon, he perceived something that made him lift his eyes: a pale, ragged smudge, hanging in the southern sky like a thumb-mark on a window.

It was the comet. It had already become visible by daylight to the sharp-sighted.

He gazed at it with a strange hatred, as though it too had conspired against him. He did not know that this vapour, this insubstantial nothing, was about to be the arbiter of his fate, and that of every creature on the globe.

IV.

Meanwhile the political condition of Europe grew daily more menacing. It was a season when the great powers, burdened with armaments whose cost exceeded that of the finest cathedrals ever raised, regarded one another across their frontiers with the unwinking stare of dogs at a bone. Germany, England, and France, the three principal contestants, had each prepared their fleets; the Tsar's empire, vast and ill-governed, mobilised along its western borders; and the newspapers of every capital announced, with a unanimity otherwise unknown to them, that war was inevitable.

The causes were trifling. A disputed coaling station on an African coast; an insult offered to a consul; a tariff upon iron. Yet from such matters, the statesmen assured their peoples, depended the honour of nations. In the Potteries, the working men, deprived of employment by the stagnation of trade, were enrolled by thousands in the reserve, and marched through the smoky streets behind a drum.

Of the comet, few took serious account. It was too faint, too distant, too vague. The learned, to be sure, had computed that its tail would sweep the earth within some five weeks; but they added, with the tranquillity of men who have never been hurt by anything, that the tenuity of such matter rendered contact without consequence. The people laughed, and went on preparing to slaughter one another.

V.

Leadford, for his part, did not enrol. He had conceived another purpose.

From a pawnbroker in Hanley he procured, for the sum of seventeen shillings, a revolver of foreign manufacture, with six chambers and a barrel somewhat corroded. Learning, through a cousin of the gardener's, that Verrall and Nettie had established themselves in a country house near the Kentish coast, he set out upon the 3rd of November, with a little linen in a bundle and nine pounds, the whole of his savings, sewn into the lining of his waistcoat.

The journey was accomplished partly by rail and partly on foot, for his money failed him sooner than he had calculated. On the fourth night, in the neighbourhood of Maidstone, he found himself upon an empty road, under a sky that no longer resembled any sky he had known. The comet had grown to monstrous proportions; it filled a quarter of the heavens, and its nucleus, a disc of pale verdigris, shed a light upon the fields so strange that the hedges seemed cut from metal. The air was perfectly still. No dog barked, no bird stirred.

Leadford walked on, his hand upon the weapon in his pocket. He spoke aloud, though there was nobody to hear him, and what he said was not for repeating.

VI.

It was at about four in the morning of the 12th of November that the earth entered the tail of the comet.

Leadford, perched upon a gate a mile beyond Maidstone, saw first a change in the stars. They fluttered, dimmed, and vanished one by one, as though a curtain were being drawn across the sky. A faint hissing, like the escape of steam from a distant valve, ran along the ground. Then the heavens turned green, an intense, luminous, emerald green, such as no painter ever mixed and no poet ever described, and the whole landscape, trees, road, and sleeping farmhouses, was bathed in it.

He sprang down, drew his revolver, and then, absurdly, laughed. For the air that now entered his lungs was not like air at all. It was light, sweet, and extraordinarily keen; it sang in the blood. A giddiness seized him. The weapon slid from his fingers. He saw, in a last flash of clarity, the meaninglessness of his journey, the poverty of his hatred, the narrowness of every quarrel that had ever divided men; and he thought that somewhere, far away, ten thousand soldiers were also sinking to the ground, their rifles falling from their hands.

He lay down upon the grass. The green light closed over him like water. Of the next hours, he knew nothing.

When at last he opened his eyes, the sun was rising in a sky of pure, unclouded blue, and the world, to his astonishment, was new.
