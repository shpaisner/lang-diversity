The morning the newspaper first gave the comet a column to itself, William Leadford read it standing in the doorway of the saggar-maker's shed at Rawdon's, with his tea going cold on an upturned crate. He was twenty-two, narrow in the chest, with a face that had never quite decided to be handsome. He earned eighteen shillings a week, and of those he gave his mother twelve.

"A smudge," said old Tomlinson, looking over his shoulder. "They say it'll be a smudge as big as the moon by Christmas. And what's it to do wi' us, lad? Will it mend a kiln?"

Nobody laughed. The men at Rawdon's spoke of the comet as they spoke of the weather, with an air of private grievance, as of something that would make no difference except for the worse. Beyond the shed the chimneys of Clayton stood in their own smoke, and the canal lay the colour of strong tea. It was a town that had never looked up. William, who had lately begun to look up, because Nettie Stuart had once told him that a star was only a kind of lamp, folded the paper and put it in his pocket for her.

He did not know yet that he would not be giving it to her. He knew only that she had not been at the corner of Hope Street on Tuesday, nor on Thursday, and that her mother's face, at the door, had been like a shut shop.

II.

The letter came on the Saturday, in a hand he had taught her himself on the back of a bill.

It was short. It said that she was sorry, and that she would be sorrier if she pretended. She said that she had never had anything, and that Mr. Verrall had offered her a great deal, and that William was not to think she had been deceived, for she had not. This last sentence he read four times, because it was the one that hurt him, and because it was so like her to be honest at the wrong moment.

Verrall he knew by sight. The Verralls owned the big house at Lonsdale Park, a long, pale, ornamental house among beeches, and Edward Verrall, the son, had a motor-car and a way of lifting his hat to working men that was worse than not lifting it. He was twenty-six. He had been to Cambridge. He had once, at the Mechanics' Institute, spoken about the Empire with his hands in his pockets.

William sat down on the stairs. In the kitchen his mother was ironing; he could hear the thump of the iron and the small hiss of the damp cloth. He sat there a long while, and when at last she called to ask whether he wanted his dinner, he found that he could answer her quite steadily, and that this steadiness was a new and rather dreadful thing in him.

III.

By October the comet had a tail. It stood in the south-western sky after dark, a faint pale feather above the gasworks, and on clear nights the people of Clayton came out on their steps to look at it, and then went in again, a little ashamed.

The newspapers had other matters. In Berlin a minister had made a speech; in Paris another had replied to it; in the Balkans something had been signed and something else torn up. The Clayton Signal printed a map with arrows. At the Foresters' Arms, where William went now more often than his mother liked, a man who sold insurance explained that war was a matter of arithmetic, and that the arithmetic was nearly done.

"Let it come," said a stout carter. "There's too many of us."

William said nothing. He had no opinion about Germany. He had an opinion about Edward Verrall, which was precise and detailed, and which he turned over in the evenings as he had once turned over his hopes. He had found out that Nettie was at the Park, or at any rate that she had been seen in the motor on the Newcastle road, wearing a hat that cost, they said, more than he made in a month. He had found out, too, that Verrall was to go abroad in November, and that she was to go with him, and that nobody in Clayton thought this remarkable.

He bought a revolver on the nineteenth, from a pawnbroker in Hanley, for seven and six. It was a small black foolish object, and the man put six cartridges in a twist of paper without asking a single question.

IV.

On the night of the comet's nearest approach, the air over the Potteries was strangely still. The kilns had been drawn early, because of a strike of the carters, and for once the smoke hung only in thin ropes. William walked out along the Newcastle road with his hands in his pockets, and the revolver knocked against his hip at every step.

He was not excited. That surprised him. He had expected, in such a case, to feel something large and theatrical, and instead he felt cold, rather hungry, and extremely conscious of his boots, which let in the water at the left toe. The sky was a curious colour, a deep bruised green low down, as though a lamp stood behind the hills. The hedges looked cut out of paper.

At Lonsdale Gates he stopped. A light burned in the lodge. Along the avenue the beeches stood very black and distinct, and above them the comet had grown into something he could no longer pretend not to see: a great pale head, with a sheen upon it like the inside of a shell, and the tail laid across half the heavens.

He thought of his mother with the iron. He thought of Nettie, saying I should be sorrier if I pretended. He took the revolver out and looked at it, and for a moment it seemed to him merely an ugly small tool, not quite sufficient to the occasion.

Then he put it back, and went through the gates, up the long gravel, towards the house.

V.

He did not reach the door.

Somewhere on the drive, among the beeches, he became aware of a smell, faint and sweetish, like a field after rain, and of a slackness in his own limbs that he took at first for weariness. The green in the sky deepened. It seemed to come down among the trees like a tide. He saw his hand before him on the gravel, and it was green too, and beautiful, and he sat down quite gently at the foot of a tree without having decided to.

He heard a bird, absurdly, in the dark. He heard, far off, the hooter at Rawdon's begin and break off in the middle, as though the man at the lever had let go of it. A dog barked once and was quiet. He had a sense, very clear and not at all alarming, that the whole of England was settling down on its side like a tired animal, and that the war, the arithmetic, the speeches, and the seven-and-sixpenny pistol were settling with it.

"Well," he thought, with a kind of mild contempt for his own earlier fury, "that's that." And he was astonished to find that he did not know what he meant.

The green light grew until it was not light at all but a sort of clear water, and he lay in it. His last distinct thought was of Nettie's hat, and it made him smile.

VI.

He woke to a sky of such cleanness that for some minutes he lay and merely looked at it.

It was morning. The beeches above him were full of a fine pale gold, and the leaves, which had been rusty the night before, seemed to have been rinsed. The air tasted new. His limbs felt light and entirely his own, and when he sat up, the ache that had lived under his breastbone for six weeks, so long that he had taken it for part of his anatomy, was gone. He searched for it, as one feels for a tooth that has been drawn, and could not find it.

The revolver lay beside him in the grass. He picked it up and examined it with a puzzled interest, as a man might look at a toy belonging to somebody else's child. It was incredible to him that he had walked three miles with it. It was more incredible that he had wanted, with his whole body, to do a thing so small, so stupid, so unlike anything that mattered.

Up the avenue the house stood in the sunshine, its windows open. Someone was standing upon the terrace, a man in his shirt-sleeves, with his face lifted to the sky. It was Verrall. He was not looking at William; he was looking at the light, with the dazed, humble face of one who has been ill and has recovered.

William stood. He felt no hatred, and he searched himself honestly for it. What he found instead was a vast, aching, wondering tenderness, for Verrall, for Nettie, for his mother and the carters and the stout man at the Foresters' Arms, for the whole smoky, quarrelsome, bewildered country lying under that astonishing morning.

He put the revolver down in the grass, and walked up the drive with his empty hands open.
