The news came to William Leadford on a Thursday, in the kitchen of his mother's house in Hanbridge Row, between the last mouthful of tea and the lighting of the lamp. It was brought by Mrs. Pratt from next door, who stood in the doorway with her arms folded in her apron and conveyed, in the manner of one performing a public duty, that Nettie Stewart had been seen at Knype station on Tuesday evening with a portmanteau and Mr. Verrall's son. Leadford's mother looked at the teapot. Leadford looked at nothing. He was twenty-two, a clerk at Rawdon's pottery at eighteen shillings a week, and had been "understood" to Nettie for two years. He said, "Thank you, Mrs. Pratt," in a voice so reasonable that his mother glanced at him in alarm.

Afterward he sat with his boots on the fender and heard the clock. Through the thin wall came the cough of the lodger, and beyond it the long, grey, perpetual murmur of the district: kilns, shunting engines, a dog. He tried to feel what a man ought to feel. What he felt was a kind of astonished tidiness, as though a drawer in his mind had been pulled out and its contents laid in rows. The cheap ring. The Sunday walks to Trentham. Her remark, only a fortnight since, that Mr. Verrall had "such a way of talking, as if everything were a joke and nobody minded."

He did not sleep, and at six he was at his desk, adding columns.

At Rawdon's they spoke of nothing but the comet and the Germans, and spoke of them in the same tone, as of two disagreeable relatives expected shortly. Old Mr. Gedge, the head clerk, kept the Staffordshire Sentinel folded beside the ledger and read from it at intervals, aloud, whether or not anyone attended. The comet, it appeared, had been photographed at the Cape, and was increasing in brilliance. The Foreign Secretary, it further appeared, had made a statement of the greatest gravity, which consisted largely of the statement that nothing could be said.

"They'll be at it before Christmas," said Gedge. "France and us and the Russians against the lot. And this thing in the sky looking on."

"It's gas," said the packer's boy, who had been to a lecture. "It's only gas, they say. A tail. We'll go through it."

"Through it!" Gedge licked his thumb. "And what will it do to us?"

Nobody knew. Leadford, bent over an invoice for four gross of toilet-ware to a firm in Hull, found that the large questions of the age had acquired no more power over him than the weather in another county. A war might come; the earth might be gassed. These were matters for persons who had not lost something. He was aware, dimly, that he was being selfish, and was aware also that the knowledge did him no good whatever. He wrote "Hull" very carefully, three times, on the wrong line.

Nettie's father lived in Brunswick Street over a shop that sold wool and, in the window, a single faded photograph of the late Queen. Leadford found him in his shirtsleeves, a long grey man, a foreman whom the potbanks had used up and discarded, sitting by a dead fire with a letter held away from his eyes as though it might go off.

"She's written," said Mr. Stewart. "You may as well read it."

It was a short letter, in a hand grown suddenly large and generous. She was well. She was happy, which was underlined. She hoped her father would not think too hardly of her, and Will least of all; she had never been fit for the life they would have had, and it was kinder to say so now than later. Mr. Verrall was going to take her abroad. She enclosed two pounds.

"Two pounds," said Mr. Stewart. He laughed, a dry small sound. "I'd like to know the rate."

Leadford put the letter down. He noticed that the two sovereigns lay on the mantelpiece beside the clock, and that the old man had not touched them, and that neither of them would. It was this, oddly, and not the letter, that he felt in his throat.

"Where's the house?" he said. "The Verralls'. Where does he stop when he's not in London?"

Mr. Stewart looked at him with a sudden shrewdness. "Don't be a fool, lad," he said. "You've your mother."

He bought the revolver on Saturday, in Hanley, from a pawnbroker who asked no questions and gave him six cartridges in a twist of newspaper. It cost seventeen and six. Leadford carried it home in his breast pocket and was astonished at its weight, which seemed out of proportion to its size, as if it contained, in compressed form, the whole of what he meant to do.

That evening, for the first time, he looked at the comet. It hung over the chimneys of Etruria, above the red haze of the furnaces, no longer a smudge but a thing with intention, a pale green stain drawn out into a veil. People stood at their doors to look. Children were allowed up late. Somewhere a man was singing hymns, rather badly, and a woman laughed at him.

Leadford sat on his bed with the weapon on his knee and rehearsed the matter practically, as he would have rehearsed a difficult balance. There would be the train to London; there would be the address, which he could get from Verrall's father's agent, with some tale. There would be a door. He tried to see Nettie's face when it opened and could not; he could see only the hand, the large new hand of her letter. He understood that he was not, as he had supposed, thinking about justice. He was thinking about being seen.

He put the revolver under the mattress and lay down on top of it, fully dressed, and did not undress for four days.

By the Wednesday the newspapers had gone mad, and the country with them. The Germans had mobilised on a frontier that most readers could not have found; a Cabinet sat through the night; Rawdon's closed at noon because half the hands had not come, and the other half stood in the yard talking in low, excited voices, like people at a funeral who have been told there is to be a will. The comet filled a quarter of the sky after dusk. It was no longer the colour of anything he had a name for.

Leadford went to London. He did not decide to; he found himself at the booking-office, putting down his money, with the revolver heavy against his ribs, and he was borne south among soldiers, reservists, women with bundles, and a clergyman who prayed audibly at Rugby. At Willesden the train stopped, and did not move for three hours. Men got out and walked along the line. Leadford walked with them.

It was past midnight. The country lay open, dim and faintly luminous under that strange sky, a landscape of allotments and gasometers and small dark houses. Somebody said the Germans were in Belgium; somebody else said the tail had touched the upper air that afternoon, and that a ship at Southampton had seen the sea turn pale. He heard it all as one hears rain. He was walking toward a house in Surrey whose name he had written inside his hat, and he walked with the steady sullen pace of a man who has nothing left but an appointment.

The house stood back from the road behind a screen of rhododendrons, long and white, with lamps burning in the lower windows. It was nearly dawn, though the dawn was wrong: the east was not grey but a thin, clear, unnatural green, and the birds, which should have been loud, were silent. Leadford stood at the gate. He was tired past the point of feeling tired, and his boots were full of the road.

There was a lawn, and on the lawn, in the green half-light, two figures at a table. They had brought out chairs, as if for a picnic or a vigil. He knew the shape of her head at once. Verrall was leaning back, laughing, perhaps at nothing, and Nettie had a shawl about her shoulders and was looking up at the sky with her mouth a little open, like a child at a conjuror. She looked younger than he remembered. She looked frightened, and he saw that she was holding Verrall's hand not out of love but out of need.

He drew out the revolver. His hand was quite steady, and he marvelled at that. He went through the gate and across the gravel, and his feet made a noise, but they did not turn.

Then the air changed. He felt it first in his throat, a sweetness, faintly metallic, like the smell after a thunderstorm. The green deepened along the lawn, ran in low pale wreaths between the shrubs, and a great drowsy gentleness fell upon him. He saw Verrall's head go down upon his arm. He saw Nettie turn her face toward him, startled, and saw that she knew him, and that it did not matter. The revolver slid out of his fingers and lay in the gravel, small and foolish. He thought, with perfect clearness, Mother will be wondering, and then the whole bright world tilted gently and let him down.
