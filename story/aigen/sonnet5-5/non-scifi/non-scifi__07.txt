I.

In that last autumn before the sky changed, William Leadford was twenty-two, and he earned nineteen shillings a week as a clerk at Mellor's pot-bank, entering the weights of clay and the counts of saggars in a ledger that was older than he was. He lived with his mother in a cottage whose back windows looked upon a canal, a bone-mill, and a ridge of slag the colour of a bruise. Everything in the district had a coating of fine grey dust, and so, he sometimes felt, had he.

He had one possession of any brightness, which was Nettie Willersley. He had loved her since the Sunday-school treat when they were both fourteen, and he loved her now with a dogged, sullen fidelity, as a man loves a thing he has no money to keep. On Thursdays, when the work allowed, he walked with her along the canal path, and talked to her of Socialism and the stars, and of how things might be altered. Nettie listened, her head a little on one side, and said that it was all very interesting.

There was a comet that autumn. It had been a smear in the Mechanics' Institute telescope in September, and by October the evening papers gave it a column. Leadford looked at it through the smoke above the kilns, and thought it did not matter in the least.

II.

On the first Sunday in November he went to tea at the Willersleys' and found the blinds half down.

Mr. Willersley, who taught drawing at the Board School and had always carried himself as if conferring a favour upon the earth, sat by the fire with a sheet of notepaper trembling in his fingers. Mrs. Willersley wept in the kitchen with the kettle forgotten. Nobody said anything at first. Then the old man held out the paper, and Leadford read it standing, with his cap still in his hand.

Nettie wrote that she was sorry, that she could not bear the narrowness of things, and that she had gone with Mr. Verrall, who would be good to her. She hoped they would forgive her. She hoped William most of all would understand.

He understood perfectly. He had seen Verrall once, a tall young man in a very good overcoat, motoring through Hanbridge with the manner of one passing through a foreign country. Verrall's father owned the land and half the royalties under it, and the son had apparently taken a fancy to some part of what lay on the surface.

"Well," said Mr. Willersley thinly, "you may as well have your tea."

Leadford said he thought not. He walked home along the canal in a rain that came sideways out of the dark, and was not conscious of getting wet.

III.

The weeks that followed were without colour. He went to Mellor's, wrote down his weights, and sat in the evenings across from his mother, who knew better than to speak. She said once, to the stocking she was darning, that there were plenty of girls. He did not answer.

The papers, which he now read to the last advertisement, were full of the Continent. A quarrel over some frontier, a speech in Berlin, a speech in Paris, the Navy Estimates, a Reservist's death in a Balkan town. At Mellor's the men talked of it at the dinner hour as they might have talked of a cup final, with a relish that had something dreadful underneath. Old Podmore, who had been at Tel-el-Kebir, said it would come, and that it would be the young who paid. The men nodded and went back to their benches.

And over all this the comet grew. It had a head now, and a tail like the smoke of a ship below the horizon, and a greenish tinge that the astronomers in the newspapers explained and disagreed about. Women stood at their doors in the dusk to look at it. Leadford glanced at it as at a stranger who has intruded upon a private grief. It seemed to him that the whole heaven had joined in a conspiracy to make small the one thing that was great.

IV.

In the second week of December a letter reached him from a cousin in Swindon, who had seen Nettie and Verrall at a hotel. Leadford read it twice and folded it into his waistcoat.

On Saturday he drew his wages, walked into Hanbridge, and bought for seven-and-six from a pawnbroker in Lichfield Street a revolver with a cracked walnut butt and five cartridges in a twist of paper. The pawnbroker did not ask him anything. He put the thing in his coat and found that it weighed almost nothing, which surprised him.

His mother was at the door when he returned. She looked at his face, and then at his pocket, and, being a woman who had buried a husband and two children, said only, "Will, you'll want your muffler."

"I'm only going to Trentham," he said.

"Aye," she said.

He travelled third class, in a carriage that smelt of wet sacking, and reached the village at dusk. Verrall's father kept a house there, and Verrall, so the cousin's letter said, was home for the holidays with his bride, as the servants were required to call her. The lane to the park was black with hedgerows. When Leadford looked up he saw the comet standing across half the sky, enormous and pale green, its tail like the beam of some great lantern. He found that he was walking faster, with his hand upon the walnut butt.

V.

At the lodge gates there was a small crowd, which surprised him: a postman, two labourers, a woman with a shawl over her head, all looking upward. Somebody said that the evening paper from Stafford had reported mobilisation in Germany. Somebody else said it was the end of the world, and was told to hold his noise.

"It's the gas," said the postman. "They say we're to go through the tail tonight."

Leadford pushed past them and up the drive. There was a smell upon the air that he could not name, faint and sharp, like the smell after a thunderstorm, and the light had grown strange. It lay upon the lawns and the cedars as moonlight lies, yet it was green, and the long windows of the house, which glowed yellow, appeared by contrast poor and small. Through one of them he saw a woman's shape pass and repass. He knew the set of the shoulders.

He drew out the revolver. His hand was quite steady, and he thought how curious that was. He had planned a speech, and could not now recall one word of it. Behind him on the road someone cried out, and a dog began to howl, and then fell silent, and then the cry behind him broke off also.

A great weariness came over him, sweet and unreasonable. His knees gave. He thought, with perfect lucidity, that he had not yet done what he came to do. Then the grass was against his cheek, cold and fragrant, and the green light closed over him like water.

VI.

He woke to the sound of a blackbird.

For some time he lay without thinking, as one lies in the first moment of a holiday. The sky above him was a pale, clean blue, with a few small clouds, and the air he breathed seemed to have been washed. There was dew on his sleeve. His cheek ached where the gravel had pressed it. Presently he sat up and saw the revolver lying a yard away, a small, ugly, ridiculous object in the grass.

He looked at it for a long while. He could remember every hour of the weeks past, the ledger, the canal, the pawnbroker, and yet they seemed to belong to a man whom he had known well and pitied, and who had been mistaken about nearly everything. The hatred had gone out of him as the fever goes. It had not been driven away; it had simply become impossible, like a language he had forgotten.

He got up. Along the drive the postman and the labourers were rising from the gravel, one by one, and looking at each other with a kind of wondering shyness. The woman in the shawl was laughing, softly, to herself, and then the postman laughed too, and put out his hand to help her up.

A door opened at the house. Two figures came out upon the terrace, Verrall bareheaded, and Nettie beside him in a grey dress, her hair undone. They saw him. Leadford crossed the lawn toward them, leaving the revolver where it lay, and as he came near he saw that Nettie's eyes were wet, and that Verrall, who had never in his life been seen to hesitate, held out both hands.

"Leadford," Verrall said. "Come in. There is so much that we must say."

And Leadford, who an hour earlier would have killed him, found that he wanted nothing in the world but to go in.
