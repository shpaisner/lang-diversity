The letter came on a Thursday, by the second post, and William Leadford read it standing in the passage with his cap still on, because his mother was in the kitchen and he did not wish her to see his face until he had decided what it should be doing.

It was a short letter. Nettie had never written long ones, even in the days when she wrote them gladly. I am going away, Willie, and you must not follow me. It is no use. I am not what you think and I never was. Mr. Verrall has been very good. Do not be hard on me. There was no address. There was a blot after the word hard, as though she had hesitated there, and he studied that blot for a long time, with a sort of bitter scholarship, trying to determine whether it meant tenderness or merely a bad pen.

He had known, of course. A man who earns twenty-eight shillings a week as a clerk at Swynnerton's pot-bank and walks out on Sundays with a girl whose father keeps a chandler's shop in Trent Row does not suppose himself to be a rival to a gentleman with a motor-car and a fine tenor voice. He had known it as one knows the date of one's own death, as a fact too large to be looked at.

"Is that the post?" his mother called.

"Only a bill," said William, and put the letter in his pocket, where it lay against his thigh all evening like a warm coal.

His mother's kitchen in Hobb Lane had one window, one lamp, and a black-leaded range in which she took a pride that William, tonight, found almost indecent. She sat knitting. She did not ask about the bill. She was a woman who had buried a husband and two children and had arrived at a philosophy of not asking.

On the table lay the Staffordshire Sentinel, and its principal column, which William read three times without taking in a word, concerned the comet. It had been discovered in the spring by an astronomer in South Africa. It was now visible to the naked eye in the small hours, a faint smudge near the Great Bear, and the Astronomer Royal was quoted as saying that there was not the slightest cause for alarm. The paper placed beside this assurance a paragraph on the Moroccan difficulty and another on the new German naval estimates, and the effect was curious: one felt that the heavens and the Foreign Office were both being very reasonable, and that it would all end in a catastrophe.

"They say it's to come close," his mother remarked, without looking up.

"They say a deal of things."

"Mrs. Hallam says it'll set the world afire."

"Mrs. Hallam," said William, "would say the same of a new vicar."

His mother clicked her needles. After a while she said, quite gently, that there was bread and dripping if he wanted it. He said he did not. He went upstairs and sat on the edge of his bed, and the whole of Hobb Lane seemed to be breathing on the other side of the wall.

On Saturday he went to the meeting at the Labour Institute in Hanley because he could not bear the house, and because Parload had asked him, and Parload, who was a lean consumptive chemist's assistant with opinions, was the nearest thing to a friend he possessed.

The hall smelt of wet mackintoshes. A speaker from Manchester told them for an hour that the workers of England had no quarrel with the workers of Germany, and that the coming war, if it came, would be a war of masters; and the audience, which had come out in the rain to be told it, applauded with the contented gloom of people hearing their own opinions confirmed. William clapped when the others clapped. He was thinking of Verrall's hands, which were white and slightly freckled, and which he had once seen adjust Nettie's collar at the corner of Trent Row.

Afterwards, in the street, Parload said, "You're not yourself."

"I am exactly myself. That's the trouble."

"It's the girl."

William did not answer. They walked under the lamps, past a public-house from which came a roar of singing, and a newsboy went by shouting something about the Kaiser. Parload coughed into his handkerchief and looked up. The cloud had thinned, and above the chimneys of the Potteries, red with their own furnaces, one could see the comet, a small blurred thing, greenish rather than white.

"Funny," said Parload. "I fancy it's grown since Tuesday."

"Everything grows," said William, "that one wants to be rid of."

It was from Nettie's father, finally, that he learned the thing. Old Mr. Gardiner stood behind the counter among his candles and firelighters and treacle, and would not meet his eyes, and said that the girl had gone to a place called Lessing Park, in the south, where Mr. Verrall's people had a house, or knew someone who had. He said it as a man says what he has been made to repeat to the police. Then, unexpectedly, he took off his apron and folded it, and William saw that the old man was weeping, quietly, and without any alteration of his features.

William walked to Hanley that afternoon and entered a pawnbroker's near the market. He had never in his life been inside a pawnbroker's. There was a revolver in the window, among concertinas and wedding rings, and he bought it for twenty-two shillings and a box of cartridges, and the man wrapped it in brown paper with the indifference of one selling a kettle.

He observed himself throughout with a detached astonishment. He did not feel like a murderer. He felt like a man carrying out an errand, tiresome but necessary, such as having a clock mended. The parcel sat in his coat. He thought: I shall be hanged. He thought it quite calmly, and then, with a small shock, he thought that he did not much mind, and that this was perhaps the one thing in the whole affair that Verrall could not have taken from him.

That night he packed a bag and told his mother he had been offered work in Birmingham.

The train south was crowded with soldiers' wives and commercial travellers, and every second man had a newspaper. Austria had sent a note. Russia had made a statement. A stout person in a bowler hat explained to the compartment that Germany would never dare, and a thin person opposite explained that Germany would dare anything, and between them William sat with his bag on his knees and his parcel in his pocket, as quiet as a stone.

At Rugby he changed. The platform was wet and nearly empty. It was past ten, and as he waited for the slow train to the south he glanced up between the iron pillars of the roof and saw the comet, and was startled.

It was no longer a smudge. It hung over the western sky, clear and pale, a hard bright head with a tail drawn out behind it like breath on a cold pane, and its colour was not white nor yellow but a thin, unwholesome green. A porter, passing with a barrow, looked up too and said, "Ugly beggar, ain't he?" and went on. That was all. No one else looked. The world, William thought, was a place where an enormous and fatal thing might hang in the sky for weeks and men would remark upon its looks and then go to their suppers.

He had been angry for six days. Now, standing in the damp with the light of that cold green star on his hands, he was surprised to find that he was merely tired, and that Nettie's face, which he had rehearsed hating, kept returning to him instead as it had been in the lane in June, with a bramble-leaf caught in her hair.

He reached Lessing Park a little after four in the morning, having walked the last six miles from a station whose name he never afterwards remembered. The road ran between hedges, and then between iron railings, and then there was a lodge with its gates standing open, which struck him as foolish, and a drive of beeches, and at the end of the drive a long pale house with one window lighted.

He stopped under the trees. His boots were wet through. The revolver was in his hand, and he did not recollect taking it out. The air had a strange quality, very still, and sweetish, as of something faintly burnt; and the light was wrong. It was neither moon nor dawn. The whole sky had taken on a delicate green, deepening toward the zenith, and the lawn before the house, the gravel, the stone urns, and his own hands all lay under it like objects at the bottom of a clear sea.

He thought: now. He began to walk toward the lighted window, and the gravel was loud under him. He rehearsed the words he would say, and found they had gone. He rehearsed the face of Verrall, and that too was gone. There remained only a drowsiness, coming up from his feet, soft and not at all unpleasant, and a curious lightness in the chest, as if he had been holding his breath for years and someone had quietly told him he might stop.

A bird began to sing in the beeches, out of season and in the dark, and he thought that was very strange. His knees were failing. He sat down on the edge of the drive, with the revolver loose in his lap, and looked up through the branches at the green heaven, and saw that it was not terrible. It was only very beautiful, and very near. Somewhere behind him a door opened in the house, and a voice, a woman's, called out a name that might have been his; and he tried to answer, and found that he was laughing.
