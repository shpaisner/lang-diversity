The letter lay on the kitchen table at Hope Street where Leadford had found it, propped against the tea caddy, and for the better part of an hour he did not touch it. It was a short letter. Nettie had never been a woman for long letters, and her father, who sat across from him in his shirt-sleeves with his boots off and his stockinged feet on the fender, had not asked to see it. He had read it himself already, that was plain, for the envelope was torn along the top with a thumb.

"Well," said old Mr. Stanley at last. "There it is."

Leadford picked it up. I am going away with Mr. Verrall. Don't follow me, William. You would not understand and it would only make things worse. I am sorry. N.

Outside, the pot-banks were smoking against a sky the colour of dishwater. A cart rattled down the cobbles. In the next house a child was being told, shrilly and repeatedly, to leave the cat alone. Everything was exactly as it had been at breakfast, and it seemed to Leadford an impertinence that it should be so.

He had earned eighteen shillings a week at Pratt's. He had saved, in four years, eleven pounds four shillings, which was in the Post Office, and which was to have furnished two rooms. He thought of the eleven pounds with a bitter, clear-headed contempt. Verrall had a motor-car and a house in the country, and had spoken to Nettie across the counter of the draper's where she worked, three or four times, in a pleasant, careless voice.

"I shall be getting to the works," Leadford said.

"It's Sunday, lad."

"So it is," said Leadford, and sat down again.

On the Monday he went to Pratt's and did his work, which consisted of copying invoices into a ledger in a hand that his employer described as commercial. His ruler was straight and his ink was black. At the next desk young Hodgkiss was reading the Staffordshire Sentinel behind a raised lid, and every few minutes he whispered items to the room at large.

"Says here the Germans have called up their reserves on the frontier. Reservists, mind."

"It's all bluff," said the elder clerk, without looking up.

"And the comet's brighter. Astronomers say it'll be in the sky by Thursday, naked eye, plain as the moon."

"It's all bluff, that as well."

Leadford dipped his pen. Nobody in the office had mentioned Nettie, but the silence about her had a quality of attention, like the silence in a chapel when a man is known to have drunk. He understood that the whole street knew. He understood that they were sorry for him, and that he would sooner have been knocked down.

At dinner-time he walked up to the top of the town, past the Wesleyan chapel and the board school, to where the houses ended and the slag-heaps began. The afternoon was clear, with the thin, washed clarity that follows rain in the Potteries, and in the east, above the ridge, there was a faint smudge that was not cloud. It might have been a thumbprint on the glass of the sky. He regarded it without interest. A thing so far off could have no bearing on a man who had eleven pounds four shillings and nothing to spend it on.

By Thursday the comet had a tail. People stood in the doorways after tea with their arms folded and looked up, and the talk, which had been of wages and the price of coal, turned to the other thing, the thing in the newspapers that was larger than any comet. Austria had sent a note. Russia had answered it. The Kaiser had made a speech at some launching.

In the Rose and Crown, where Leadford went because it was no longer possible to sit in Hope Street, a man named Cotterill, a painter at the works and a Socialist, held forth to a half-circle of listeners.

"They'll not ask us. That's the beauty of it. Two or three gentlemen in a room in Berlin and two or three in London, and when they've done, you and me goes and gets shot. And the comet's the same. Nobody's asked. It'll come or it won't."

"It'll bring a change in the weather," said somebody.

"It'll bring the end of the world," said somebody else, with relish.

Leadford drank his beer, which tasted of nothing. He found that he was listening not to Cotterill but to the noise in his own head, a steady, tuneless noise, like a wheel turning on a dry axle. He thought of Verrall's hand on Nettie's arm. He thought it with great distinctness, as one thinks of a sum, and the thought arrived each time at the same answer.

"You're quiet, Will," said Cotterill.

"I've nothing to say."

"Well, that's a rare gift," said Cotterill, and the men laughed, and Leadford went out into the street where the comet hung, long and pale, over the chimneys.

The revolver he bought on the Saturday from a pawnbroker in Hanley, who asked no questions and seemed to think none were necessary. It was an old Webley, heavy and brown with grease, and came with six cartridges in a twist of newspaper. Leadford paid three pounds ten for it. He carried it home inside his coat, held against his ribs with his elbow, and all the way along Waterloo Road he felt that the people passing must see its shape. Nobody looked at him.

In his room he laid it on the counterpane and sat on the chair beside it. The window gave onto a yard, a wash-house, a wall. He did not feel excited, or even angry; he felt, rather, that he was being very businesslike, as though he had at last taken up a piece of work long neglected. He loaded it as the man had shown him. He unloaded it and loaded it again.

Mrs. Stanley's voice came up through the boards, speaking to the lodger below about a pound of bacon. The ordinary noise of the house was like a wall behind which he stood alone. He thought, once, quite coolly, that he would be hanged. The thought did not trouble him as it ought. It seemed to belong to somebody else, a steady, sensible man who did not exist.

He knew where they were. Her father, shamed into speech at last, had let it fall: Verrall's house, out beyond Lymm Hill, half a day by train and a walk of some miles. He would go on Monday. He wrote a note to Pratt's saying he was ill, folded it, and put it on the mantelpiece, where it remained a long time, a small white square.

The train was crowded with men carrying bundles and women carrying children, and every station had a knot of people at the end of the platform with a newspaper held between them. At Crewe a porter said that war had been declared, and another porter said it hadn't, and a stout man in a bowler said it was a matter of hours. Leadford sat in the corner with his hand in his coat. He noticed that the light in the carriage was strange. It was coming from above and behind, from the great pale smear that now crossed a third of the sky even at noon, and it laid on every face a faint, sickly lustre like the light under water.

He changed at a junction whose name he did not afterward remember. He walked the last seven miles, through lanes where the hedges were grey with dust, and met nobody but a carter who stared at the sky and would not be drawn into conversation. The air had a taste, a thin sharpness at the back of the throat, like the smell of a struck match. Birds were silent. A dog, tied to a gate, whined at him as he passed, and he saw that it was trembling.

He was not frightened. That surprised him, a little, as he climbed the last hill. He felt rather that he was being carried, as a leaf is carried down a gutter, toward something not of his own choosing, and that the comet and the Kaiser and Nettie and the old Webley were all a single weather moving over the same country.

At the crest he stopped. Below him, in a hollow of beech trees, lay a white house with a gravel drive.

He went down through the beeches quietly, keeping to the shadow, and came out at the edge of a lawn. The house stood before him with its long windows open and its blinds half drawn. On the terrace a table had been laid for tea, and at the table, with her back to him, sat a woman in a pale dress whom he knew by the set of her head before he had seen her face. She was bending to pour. Beside her, in a basket chair, a man in flannels was reading a newspaper, one leg crossed over the other.

Leadford stood among the trunks with the pistol in his hand. He had not noticed drawing it. The metal was warm from his body and damp from his palm.

It occurred to him that he had imagined this many times and that in none of the imaginings had the tea been there. The ordinary cups, the silver jug, the plate of thin bread and butter made the thing difficult in a way he could not have predicted. A wasp walked on the rim of the sugar basin. Verrall turned a page.

Then the light altered. It was not a cloud; it was as if some great hand had drawn a green gauze across the sun. The lawn took on a colour no lawn had ever worn, the white house grew faintly emerald, and the man in the basket chair let the newspaper fall. Nettie put down the jug and looked up. All around, the beech leaves had begun, very faintly, to stir, though there was no wind.

Leadford raised the revolver and held it level, his arm steady, his heart going slowly and hard. The air in his mouth was sweet. He had time to think, with great clearness, now, and to notice that his finger on the trigger had begun to feel unaccountably light, and that he was, for the first time in a week, not hating anybody at all.
