Willie Leadford learned of Nettie's going on a Tuesday, in the manner in which a man in Clayton learned most things of consequence: from his mother, over the tea-table, with the loaf half cut. Mrs. Leadford did not say it kindly, nor unkindly. She said it as she might have reported that the price of coal had risen. "Mrs. Stuart's been round. The girl's gone. Went on the Saturday, with that Verrall, and left a note on the dresser."

Willie put down his cup. He was twenty-two, a clerk at Rawdon's pottery at fourteen shillings a week, with a narrow, earnest face and the stoop of a young man who has read more than his wages entitle him to. For three years he had walked out with Nettie Stuart on Sunday evenings. He had saved eleven pounds, four shillings and some pence towards a home that was to be taken in Bleak Street, where the rent was five and sixpence. The figures rose before him now with a fantastic precision, like items in the ledger of a bankrupt firm.

"Gone where?" he said.

"London, they say. Or she says." Mrs. Leadford cut the loaf. "I never trusted that hair of hers, William. Nor his hands."

He rose, sat down again, and found that he could not swallow bread. The kitchen clock ticked on the mantel, beside the china dog he had won at a fair when he was eight.

The next evening the whole of Clayton seemed to be looking at the sky. Willie, coming up from the works through the smoke and the lamplighters, found knots of men at every corner with their faces tilted back and their pipes forgotten. Over the chimneys of the Nelson Pottery, in the thin green of the dusk, hung a small, pale smear that had not been there a month ago.

"That's it," said a man beside him, a presser with whom he had never exchanged more than a nod. "That's the comet. It's in the paper. They say it's coming straight for us."

"They always say something," said Willie.

"Aye. But look at it."

He looked, and felt only a dull resentment that the heavens should choose this week to be interesting. A star that would end the world was, to a young man who had already lost his, a piece of impertinence. He went on up the hill, past the chapel and the Co-operative stores, and the little smear went on hanging above the roofs, supremely indifferent to the Leadfords, the Stuarts, and Mr. Rawdon's bills of lading.

At the works on Thursday no one spoke of Nettie, which was how Willie knew that everyone knew. Old Hobson, the head clerk, gave him a letter to copy with a gentleness that was almost insulting. At dinner-time the talk was of Germany.

A newspaper lay open on the packing-case. The Kaiser had said something, or a minister had said something about the Kaiser; there were ships at Kiel, and a speech in the Reichstag, and a paragraph on the Balkans that no one fully understood. "It'll be war by Christmas," said the presser, who seemed to be the oracle of the district. "And what for? Because a lot of gentlemen can't agree about a bit of Africa."

"They won't be the ones to fight it," said somebody.

"No. We shall."

Willie ate his bread and dripping and thought of Verrall: of Verrall's soft hat and fair moustache, and the motor-car that had stood outside the Stuarts' door in June, to the great scandal of the street. He had been a gentleman's son, apparently, who amused himself in the Midlands for a season; one of those who could say "a bit of Africa" and mean it. War, comet, Nettie: all of it seemed to him the same large, careless gesture of people who had never counted their shillings.

On Saturday he went to see Mrs. Stuart. It was a mistake, and he knew it at the door. The house smelt of boiled cabbage and of grief that had resolved itself into temper. Mrs. Stuart sat with her hands in her apron.

"I'll not have her name said," she told him. "She's made her bed."

"Has she written?"

"A postcard." She did not offer it, and then, with a sudden collapse of her whole broad face, she took it from behind the clock. On the front was a view of Hampstead Heath. On the back, in Nettie's rounded schoolroom hand: Don't worry. I am quite happy. Tell Willie I am sorry. N.

Tell Willie I am sorry. He read it three times. It was perhaps the most unforgivable thing that could have been written, because it was so entirely sincere. It put him, with a stroke of the pen, among the people who are to be told things, like the milkman or the vicar.

"There's an address," said Mrs. Stuart, not looking at him. "Of a sort. Postmark's all I've got. Hampstead."

He gave the card back with a hand that was perfectly steady, and thanked her, and walked home beneath that pale thing in the sky, which seemed now larger and a little greener, as if it had been listening.

His father kept, in the bottom drawer of the chest on the landing, a revolver that had been brought from Canada by an uncle and never fired. Willie had known of it since boyhood. On Sunday night, while the household slept, he took it out, wrapped in a flannel shirt, and turned it over under the candle. It was heavier than he expected, and smelt of oil and old cloth. He found a box of cartridges, green with age, and did not know whether they would answer.

He was surprised at his calm. There was no fury in it. He felt rather as he did when balancing the books at the quarter: a thing was out, and must be made to come right. He counted his savings again, set aside the fare to London, and put the remainder in an envelope for his mother, with a note about the rent that was as dry and exact as any he had written for Rawdon's.

At the foot of the stairs he paused. A line of light lay under the kitchen door; his mother was awake and did not call out. He stood, and she did not call, and he understood that she had heard the drawer. Then the light went out, and he let himself into the street.

He reached the Heath on Wednesday evening, after two days of third-class carriages and strange pavements, so tired that the great city had become merely a noise at his elbow. A newsboy was crying something about an ultimatum. Nobody was buying. People stood in the road before the public-house with their faces turned upward, and the comet filled a third of the sky, no longer a smear but a vast, soft, greenish disc, so bright that the gas-lamps looked like little yellow lies.

He had found the house by inquiry and by luck: a villa behind laurels, with a light in the upper window and a motor-car under a tarpaulin in the drive. He stood at the gate with his hand in his pocket on the flannel-wrapped weight. He had rehearsed this moment a hundred times, in the train, and found now that he could remember none of the words.

The air had changed. There was no wind, but a strange, faint sweetness lay on it, like the smell of something he had known as a child and could not name, and the laurels were tinged with a colour he had never seen on leaves. A dog began to howl somewhere and then stopped, as though it had been persuaded. Up in the window a shadow crossed the blind, a woman's, with the head bent in the old familiar way.

"Nettie," he said aloud, and was ashamed to hear how small it sounded.

He drew out the revolver. His fingers felt curiously large and slow. All down the road, people were sitting down on the kerb, on the steps, on the grass of the Heath, quite gently, like folk at the end of a long Sunday; and a great green stillness came pouring over the roofs as he lifted his hand.
