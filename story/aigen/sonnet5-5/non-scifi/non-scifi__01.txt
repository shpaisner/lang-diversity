The news came to William Leadford on a Tuesday, which was the day the saggar-makers were paid, and so it reached him in a crowd. Young Rushton from the packing-shed told him at the gate of Brunt's pot-bank, with a face arranged to look sympathetic and not quite managing it. Nettie Stanton had gone. She had gone on the 4.10 from Knype, with a hat-box and a young man in a good overcoat, and the young man was Verrall.

Leadford said, "Ah," and went on through the gate, because there was nothing else to be done with his legs. He sat at his high desk among the invoices for lustre teasets bound for Hamburg, and he wrote figures in the proper columns until six. Afterwards he could not recall one of them.

His mother had the tea laid in the kitchen at Clayton Row. She was a thin woman with scoured hands, who had charred for other people's mothers all her life, and she knew at once. She did not speak. She cut the bread and poured the tea and pushed the jam towards him, and the clock on the dresser, bought on the instalment plan in 1889, went on as though nothing had occurred.

"Thou'lt eat," she said at last.

He ate a slice, mechanically, tasting nothing. Through the window he could see the bottle-kilns of the district standing in the dusk with their smoke going up straight and grey, and above them, quite plainly though the sun was barely down, a faint smudge of white that the newspapers called the Wanderer.

He had known Nettie Stanton for three years. He had walked with her on Sundays by the canal and had once, in the parlour of her father's house in Trafalgar Road, been permitted to hold her hand for the length of a hymn. Her father kept a small and failing draper's shop and a large and unfailing opinion of himself. It was the opinion, Leadford supposed, that had made the girl restless, for she had been brought up to expect more than the Potteries could offer a clerk at twenty-four shillings a week.

Verrall had offered more. He was the son of a man who owned a great deal of land and a share in three collieries, and he had a motor-car, which in Clayton was still a thing people came out of their doorways to look at. He had been seen at the Stantons' gate in it twice. Leadford had seen him once, and had noticed his hands, which were white and wholly idle.

"He'll not marry her," Leadford's mother said, from the scullery.

"I know that."

"Then what's tha going to do about it?"

He found that he had no answer, and that the lack of one frightened him more than the loss. It was like putting a foot down for a stair that is not there.

By the end of the week the town had settled the matter among itself, as towns do. Mr. Stanton shut the shop for a day and opened it again with his collar very stiff. Women stopped talking when Leadford passed the pump-end. He found that being pitied was a more tiring business than being ignored.

He read the paper that week with a thoroughness he had never given it. There was little in it about Nettie, naturally, and a good deal about Germany. The Kaiser had said something; a minister had replied; a cruiser had been seen where a cruiser ought not to have been. At the works men argued over their cans of tea, one saying that there would be a war by Michaelmas, another that the Germans were too sensible, and Old Gathercole, the kiln-man, saying that it was all one, since the comet would settle it before either.

"It's gotten bigger," said Rushton. "Look at it at night. It's the size of a plate."

"Tha mun look at it through a glass at the Institute," said somebody else. "They've a man there. He says it's no more than gas."

"Gas!" said Gathercole, with relish. "Aye. And we're to breathe it."

Leadford went to the Mechanics' Institute that Friday, not from curiosity but because it was warm and there was nowhere else. A pale schoolmaster, with a cold in his head, let the audience take turns at a small brass telescope on the roof. Leadford looked and saw a greenish disc with a ragged blur about it. It seemed to him neither threatening nor beautiful, simply indifferent, like the face of a stranger in a tram. He thought of the war, and the comet, and Nettie, and found them all equally far from anything he could alter.

He gave up his place to a boy, and went down the stairs.

The pawnbroker's in Hanbridge Road stayed open late on Saturdays. Leadford had never before been inside it, and he stood a while at the window among the wedding-rings and concertinas, looking at nothing, before he went in. The revolver lay in a tray among a quantity of pocket watches. It was an ugly, small, serviceable object with a worn walnut handle. The pawnbroker, a placid man in a skull-cap, asked no questions, and Leadford was grateful and ashamed in equal parts.

"Seven and six," the man said. "Cartridges, a shilling the dozen. You'll be wanting to keep it dry."

Leadford paid with the money he had been saving, for two years, towards a bicycle. He walked home with the parcel under his arm, and he noticed with an odd clearness that he was not angry. He had expected rage, the hot kind that the novelettes promised. What he felt instead was a kind of settled and rather dreary determination, as if he were a clock that had been wound.

His mother saw the parcel and said nothing. That was the worst of it.

On Monday he drew his week's wages, told Mr. Brunt's chief clerk that he was ill, and took the morning train to London, where Rushton had heard that Verrall kept rooms. The carriage was crowded and smelt of wet umbrellas. At Stafford a party of reservists got in, red-faced, singing, with their bundles on their knees, and a woman in the corner began to cry quietly into a handkerchief. At Rugby he saw soldiers on the platform and a cart stacked with forage. Nobody explained anything. Men simply looked at one another over their newspapers with the same expression, one of waiting.

Leadford sat with the revolver in his coat pocket and the weight of it against his thigh. He considered what he would say. He found he could not imagine Nettie's face at all; he could see only the hat she had worn, a brown straw with a blue ribbon, and he was irritated to discover that this was all he had kept of her.

Towards evening the light in the window changed. The fields beyond Bletchley had gone a strange, thin colour, neither dusk nor day, and the passengers one by one turned and looked up. Low in the west, where the sun had set red, a second glow was rising, pale and green and very steady, and it laid a cold sheen along the telegraph wires.

"There it is," said one of the reservists, in a small voice. "There's your comet."

Nobody answered him. The train ran on through the greenish light, and Leadford, who had come out to kill a man and who still meant to, felt for the first time that evening the small, absurd, immovable fact of his own life in the vast shadow of something else.
