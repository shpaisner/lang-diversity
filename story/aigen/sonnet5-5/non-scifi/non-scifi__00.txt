The letter lay on the kitchen table at Lime Street, propped against the teapot, where his mother had set it so that he should see it before he sat down. William Leadford saw it, and for some moments did nothing at all. He took off his cap and hung it on its nail. He noticed that the clock wanted winding. Then he opened the envelope with a knife, because his fingers would not manage it, and read four lines in Nettie's round, careful hand, the hand she had learned at the Board School and had always been proud of. She was sorry. She could not go on as they were. Mr. Verrall had been very kind, and she was going away with him, and he was not to look for her.

"Well?" said his mother from the scullery door.

"She's gone," said Leadford.

Mrs. Leadford dried her hands on her apron, slowly, finger by finger. She had expected it for a month. "I'd not say I was sorry, William," she said at last, "not to tell you a lie."

He ate his tea because it was there. Outside, the bottle-ovens of Clayton stood against a smeared yellow sky, and the evening smoke lay along the canal as it had lain every evening of his twenty-two years. He had four-and-twenty shillings a week from Bagshaw's office, and it had seemed, until this moment, a sum with a future in it.

By the end of that week everyone at Bagshaw's knew, and no one mentioned it, which was worse. Old Gedge, the head clerk, took to clearing his throat whenever Leadford passed his desk, as though the matter were a phlegm that sympathy might shift.

It was Gedge, however, who at dinner-time unfolded the Staffordshire Sentinel and said, to the room generally, that the astronomers were now agreed about the comet.

"What comet?" said the junior.

"The one you've been looking at all month, with your mouth open."

It had been visible for some weeks, a faint smudge low in the evening, growing. The papers wrote of it between their columns on Morocco and the German naval estimates. Gedge held that it would pass harmlessly; he held equally that Germany meant mischief; and he delivered both opinions in the same tone of gloomy satisfaction. Leadford, eating bread and cheese over a ledger, heard neither. He was thinking of Verrall's hands, which were white and large and had never held anything heavier than a racket, and of Nettie's hand laid in one of them.

"There'll be war by Christmas," said Gedge.

"There'll be something," said Leadford, and the room, startled by the sound of his voice, went on with its dinner.

On Saturday he walked out to Trafford Park, where the Verralls' people had their great house, and stood in the lane below the wall. He had no plan. The rhododendrons were over; the drive was raked; a gardener's boy was whistling somewhere out of sight. A groom, leading a bay mare, looked him up and down.

"Mr. Verrall's abroad," the groom said, before he could ask. "Been abroad a fortnight. Is it about a bill?"

"No," said Leadford. "It's not about a bill."

He went home by the long road through Swinton, past the new red villas of the men who had done well out of earthenware, and past the old cottages of those who had not. In a doorway a woman was rocking a child and talking across the street about the comet, and whether it would be hot. Above the chimneys, though it was not yet dark, he fancied he could see it, a pale and ragged thing, with a faint greenish tinge he had not noticed before.

It did not impress him. Nothing in the sky could be larger than what he carried. He walked with his hands in his pockets and his jaw set, and discovered with a kind of weary surprise that he was composing, sentence by sentence, what he would say.

He gave notice on the Monday. Gedge, who had a respect for regularity, asked if he were ill.

"I'm going to London," said Leadford.

"There's nothing in London."

"There's Verrall's club. A man there will know where he is."

He sold his books, his father's watch, and a barometer that had never been right, and with the money bought, from a pawnbroker in Hanley who asked no questions, a small revolver and a box of cartridges. He told himself he wanted it for protection. He knew perfectly well that this was an untruth, and the knowledge, instead of alarming him, gave him an odd cold steadiness, as of a man who has at last been given a task suited to his size.

His mother watched him pack from the bedroom door. "You'll do something wicked," she said.

"I shall do what's right."

"It's never the same thing, William, when you say it like that."

She put into his bag two shirts she had mended, a screw of tea, and her Bible, which he took out again when she had gone downstairs. At the station the evening paper had the comet on one side and the Kaiser on the other, and the porters were arguing about which was nearer.

London swallowed him without noticing. For four days he went between a club in Pall Mall, where a footman lied to him civilly, and a lodging in Camden Town that smelled of boiled cabbage and gas. On the fifth a clerk in a shipping office, bribed with half a sovereign, remembered that Mr. Verrall's letters were forwarded to a villa near Hindhead.

He went down by a slow train. The country was dry and still, a warm hush such as comes before thunder, though the sky was cloudless. At every station knots of people stood on the platform looking up, and the newsboys cried that the Powers had been given until Thursday. He was dimly aware that something was happening to the world, as one is aware of a neighbour's quarrel through a wall. It had no connection with him.

At Haslemere he asked the way of a carter, who answered with his eyes on the heavens. "Green, ain't it?" the man said. "Never seen the like. My missus says it's the Judgment."

Leadford looked. The comet had grown to a great pale fan across the south-west, and its edge was of a hard, unwholesome green. A little of the same colour seemed to have entered the evening air itself, so that the hedges and the white road took on the tint of old glass. He felt for the revolver in his coat, and found it warm.

The villa stood at the end of a sandy lane among pines, with a veranda and a lawn and a wicker chair upon it. Someone had left a book face-down on the chair. Leadford stood at the gate for what might have been a minute. He had imagined this moment a hundred times and always in rain, and it was strange to find it so quiet, with the light turning gently to green over the roof and a blackbird, deceived by the hour, beginning to sing.

Then the door opened, and Nettie came out.

She was thinner than he remembered, and had put up her hair in a way that made her look older. She did not see him. She stood on the veranda with one hand shading her eyes and gazed at the sky, and he saw that she was afraid, and that she was alone, and that she was exactly the girl who had once stood with him on the canal bridge at Clayton and said that she would never want more than that.

He had the revolver out. He did not recall drawing it. It hung from his hand like a stone he had picked up and could not think where to throw.

"Nettie," he said.

She turned. Her face changed, but she did not run. "Willie," she said. "Oh, Willie, what have you come for?"

He could not answer. The air between them had thickened, sweet and faintly pungent, and the blackbird had stopped. Somewhere beyond the pines a man cried out, and then another, not in pain but in wonder. The green deepened until the lawn, the wicker chair, and her white face were all drowned in it, and Leadford, with the weapon still in his hand and every word he had rehearsed gone out of him, felt his knees give way, gently, like a man sitting down at the end of a long day.
