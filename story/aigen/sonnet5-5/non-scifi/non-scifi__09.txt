The Potteries had never looked more disagreeable to William Leadford than on the evening Nettie Stewart's letter reached him. Smoke from the bottle-kilns lay low over the town, the cobbles of Brunswick Street shone with a thin rain, and in the window of the newsagent's the evening paper carried the same bill it had carried all week: THE COMET: NEW MEASUREMENTS. He was twenty-two, a clerk at Boulton's pot-bank at eighteen shillings a week, and he had saved, by dint of tea without sugar and a vow against tobacco, the sum of four pounds eleven shillings. He had meant it for a ring and a first month's rent.

He read the letter standing, in his mother's kitchen, with his wet cap still on. It was brief and ill-spelled, and its brevity was the worst of it. She was sorry. She had gone with Mr. Verrall, whose father owned the big house at Clayhurst, and who had a motor-car. She hoped William would not think too hardly of her. She had always been fond of him.

"Fond," said William aloud.

His mother, at the range, did not turn round. She had seen it coming for a month, as one sees weather. "Eat your tea," she said, and he ate it, every mouthful, tasting nothing, while the clock on the dresser, which gained four minutes a day, ticked on towards an hour that did not exist.

Afterwards he went out and walked the length of the Hanbridge road without once looking up. Above the chimneys, behind the cloud, the thing that the newspapers talked of was growing a little larger every night, and he cared nothing for it.

It was Nettie's sister Clara, a thin and unhappy woman who did out-work for a hat-trimmer, who told him the rest. She told it in her doorway, with the child on her hip and one eye on the street. Nettie had been seen at the Midland Hotel in Manchester with a gentleman's luggage. There had been dresses. She had been, said Clara, so taken with it all.

"She's not been ruined," said Clara, with the dreadful fairness of the poor. "She went with her eyes open. She was tired, William. She was tired of having nothing."

"And I had nothing," said William.

"You had nothing yet," said Clara; and then, because she was not unkind, "I'm sorry. Go home."

He did not go home. He went instead to the Free Library, where he had spent so many Saturday evenings improving himself, and sat before a volume of Ruskin without turning the page. He had believed in improvement. He had believed that a man who learned his French verbs and his book-keeping and kept his collar clean would in due time be given what he asked. Now the whole structure seemed a thing of paper, such as children build, and a young man with a motor-car had breathed on it.

At closing time the attendant touched his shoulder, and William rose with the stiff courtesy of the very angry, and went out into the rain.

By the end of the month the comet had grown to the size of a half-crown, and the town had begun, in its heavy way, to be proud of it. Men argued over it at the Red Lion. The curate preached on it. Meanwhile, in the same newspapers, in columns that the comet did not entirely displace, Germany was said to be building ships, and Russia to be moving something somewhere, and a minister had made a speech.

William sat in the corner of the taproom with a half-pint he did not want and listened to Old Gregg, a retired saggar-maker, who held the view that the Kaiser would settle the whole business before the comet had the chance.

"There'll be war by Michaelmas," said Gregg. "You mark me. And it'll be us as does the fighting, and them with the money as does the profit."

"It's all one," said a younger man. "The comet'll have us before the Germans do."

There was laughter. William did not laugh. It seemed to him, in his bitter clarity, that these men argued about the world as if it were something that happened to other people, and he thought, with a sort of cold relish: let it come, all of it. Let the Germans come and the comet come, and the Verralls of England with their motor-cars find out what they were made of. The thought gave him no comfort, but it gave him a direction, which in his state was the next best thing.

He finished his half-pint, which he had never done before, and went out.

The pawnbroker's in Hanbridge was kept by a man named Isaacs, who looked at William's four pounds eleven shillings, and then at William, and said nothing about either. The revolver was a small, ugly thing with a worn butt and a smell of old oil. It cost thirty shillings, with cartridges.

"Not for a person, I hope," said Isaacs, wrapping it in brown paper.

"For rats," said William.

"Ah," said Isaacs. "There's a deal of rats about this year."

He took the train south on a Thursday, third class, with the parcel in his overcoat pocket and his fare to Clayhurst counted out beforehand. The carriage was full of commercial men, who read their papers and said that the Foreign Office was being very quiet, which they took to be ominous. A woman opposite fed a child with bread and dripping. Beyond the window the black country thinned into hedges, and the hedges into the wet green fields of the south, which he had never seen, and which looked to him insolent in their prosperity.

It occurred to him somewhere beyond Rugby that he did not know what he meant to do. He had imagined the scene a hundred times: the door, the white face of Verrall, Nettie's cry. In none of these imaginings had he got beyond the cry. He turned the parcel over in his pocket, and the train went on through the afternoon, and the light began to take a curious tint.

It was past nine when he reached the lane beneath the Verrall house, and by then the sky had altered. No one had told him it would. The comet, enormous now, hung over the western trees, and the whole heaven round it had gone a pale and unwholesome green, like the glaze of a cheap vase. The hedges were green; his hands were green. A dog barked once somewhere and was silent. He found that he was walking quickly, almost running, with a curious lightness in his chest, and that the revolver, which he had taken out, hung in his hand as a thing hangs that one has forgotten to put down.

At the gate of the house a man in a gardener's apron sat on the ground with his back to the pillar, his head upon his chest, breathing slowly and deeply. William stepped over his legs. He thought: He is drunk, and then, quite distinctly, No.

The air had a taste. It was not unpleasant. It was faintly sweet and cold, and it seemed to go into him and rinse something out. He walked up the drive between rhododendrons, hearing his own boots on the gravel as though from a great way off, and reached the lit windows of the house, and saw through the glass a lady in evening dress lying along a sofa in an attitude of perfect peace, and behind her, upon the carpet, a young man, with his arm flung over his face.

William's knees went from under him. He sat down upon the step, quite gently, and the gun slid out of his fingers; and the last thing he thought, looking at the great green light upon the lawn, was that he had never in his life been so tired, nor so near to being, for no reason whatever, happy.

He woke with the sun on his face.

For some while he did not move. He lay upon the stone step in his damp overcoat with a crick in his neck, and the first thing he was conscious of was that the air was extraordinarily clean. It had no smell of coal in it, nor of horses, nor of anything he knew. The sky was a pure, high blue, such as he had seen perhaps three times in Staffordshire, and the lawn before him, drenched with dew, lay bright to the very edge of the trees. A blackbird was singing. It sang as if it had waited a long time for the opportunity.

He sat up, and saw the revolver in the gravel two yards off, and looked at it with a vague and tolerant surprise, as at some tool belonging to another man in another trade. He did not pick it up. He could not, for the moment, recall quite why he had brought it. Something had gone out of him in the night: the hard knot beneath the breastbone, the habit of rehearsing grievances like a lesson. He searched for it, as one feels with the tongue for a drawn tooth, and found only a space, and in the space a kind of clear and rather melancholy gentleness.

Behind him a window was thrown up. He turned. It was the young man, Verrall, white and tousled, in a crumpled dress-shirt, leaning out into the morning with his mouth a little open. He saw William; William saw him. For a moment neither spoke.

"I say," said Verrall at last, in a voice with no defiance in it at all. "Have you noticed? Have you noticed everything?"

"Yes," said William Leadford, and, to his own astonishment, he smiled. "I think I have."
