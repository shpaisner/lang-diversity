The news came to William Leadford on a Saturday, between the second and third firings of the glost oven, when the whole yard of Bratton's pot-bank smelled of hot clay and wet straw. It came by way of a girl named Ada Pinnock, who carried saggars with the loose, indifferent strength of one who has never been looked at twice. She delivered it as she might have delivered a cracked plate.

"Your Nettie's gone off. Her mother's been telling everybody. She went Thursday, with that Verrall."

Leadford went on lifting. He was nineteen, narrow in the chest, with the pale, intent face of a youth who reads at night by a candle-end. He set the saggar down on the barrow with great exactness, as though precision might still count for something, and said, "Ah," which in Staffordshire commits a man to nothing. Then he went into the shed and stood for some minutes before a stack of unfired bowls, looking at them without seeing them.

What he felt was not at first grief. It was a kind of immense embarrassment, as if the whole of Clayton had looked up from its tea and observed him. He had walked out with Nettie for fourteen months. He had saved eleven shillings toward a ring that was never bought. He remembered, with sudden and insulting clearness, her way of tucking her chin into her collar when she laughed.

At six he drew his money, two pounds one and fourpence for the fortnight, and walked home through streets whose every doorstep seemed to hold a woman who knew.

His mother did not mention it. This was her tenderness, and it was worse than speech. She set before him a plate of bacon and potatoes, and the clock ticked on the mantel between the brass candlesticks, and the kettle sang. Mrs. Leadford was a small woman whose hands had been scalded and cracked by thirty years of other people's washing. She believed in God, in thrift, and in not making a fuss.

It was Mr. Pinnock from next door who made the fuss, coming in without knocking, as he always did, with the evening paper folded to a column.

"They say it'll be as big as the moon by Christmas," he announced. "This comet. Gives off poison, some of 'em reckon. Cyanogen."

"I'm sure I don't know what that is," said Mrs. Leadford.

"Nor me. But it's in the paper." He sat heavily. "And the Germans have ordered more of them big ships. It'll come to a war, you mark. Kaiser and the French, and us dragged in by the scruff. Wasteful, that's what it is."

Leadford ate his bacon. He thought of Verrall, whom he had seen exactly twice: a tall young man in a grey suit that fitted him like a decision, standing on the steps of the big house at Clayton Hall, smoking, and regarding the potbanks as another man might regard a view. He thought that such a man would not know the price of bacon. The reflection gave him no comfort, only a dull, widening sense of the difference between the two sorts of people in the world, a difference which until that week he had accepted as one accepts weather.

On Sunday evening he walked up to Parload's.

Parload lived in two rooms over a chandler's shop in Swinton Street, and he was the nearest thing Leadford had to a friend: a long, stooping, unhealthy young man, clerk by day at the Gas Office, who by night attached himself to the heavens with the grim fidelity of a lover. On the roof, among the chimney-pots, he kept a small brass telescope lashed to a clothes-prop.

"You've heard, then," said Parload, without turning from the eyepiece. "I'm sorry for it. Come and look."

Leadford looked. The comet, in the glass, was a smudge, a pale and indecisive thing with a faint hook of light behind it, lying low among the stars of the north-west. He had expected terror, and found it resembled a thumb-mark on a window.

"It's nothing," he said.

"It's eighty million miles off," said Parload, "and it's coming at us. There's a green line in the spectrum. Never seen it before in any comet. Something new." His voice had a peculiar tremor. "They say at Greenwich it can't touch us. They say that of everything they don't understand."

"What happens if it does?"

"Nobody knows. That's what I like about it." Parload laughed shortly. "It'll be a change, anyway. Nettie's done a change for herself. You might do one."

Leadford said nothing. Below them, in the street, a Salvation Army band was playing "Rock of Ages" slightly flat, and the sound rose among the chimneys like smoke.

On Wednesday he took an afternoon's absence, which cost him a shilling, and went by tram to Hanbridge, to a pawnbroker's in Lawton Street with three brass balls and a window of concertinas.

The revolver lay among watches and wedding rings, a short black Webley with a chipped butt. The man behind the counter, who wore a skull-cap and had the lugubrious courtesy of his trade, named seventeen shillings. Leadford had thirty. He bargained, from a deep habit of poverty, down to fifteen, and was ashamed afterward of having haggled over the price of a murder.

"Cartridges?" said the man. "Six for sixpence. You'll not be wanting it for a burglar, I take it? Nobody's burgled in Clayton."

"For rats," said Leadford.

"Ah. Rats," said the pawnbroker, with the air of one who had heard of many rats.

He carried the thing home inside his jacket against his ribs. All the way back on the tram he felt that the weight must show; that the conductor, the two women with their baskets, the old man reading the Signal, knew precisely what he was. Yet no one looked at him. This was the discovery of the afternoon, and it surprised him more than the pistol. A man might carry the whole of his ruin about in a coat, and the world would only ask him for his fare.

That night the paper said that two German cruisers had been seen off the Norfolk coast, and that the Foreign Secretary would speak.

He learned from Nettie's mother, who had wept at him on the doorstep in a wet and noisy fashion that moved him not at all, that the pair were in lodgings at Birmingham, in a street off the Hagley Road, and that Verrall had a way of speaking of "arrangements." On Friday he gave in his notice, left his mother seven pounds on the mantelpiece beneath the clock, and took the train.

The third-class carriage was full of men talking war. A commercial traveller in hosiery held that the Germans would not dare. A mournful person in a bowler said they would dare anything. A boy of perhaps seventeen, in a cheap new uniform, sat among his relations with a face of such radiant stupidity that Leadford looked away. At Stafford, soldiers got in, singing, and one of them was sick quietly in his cap.

Through the window, as the afternoon darkened, the comet came out. It was no longer a smudge. It hung above the black spoil-heaps and furnaces of the Black Country, large and pale and curiously near, with a tail that rose behind it like a ghostly feather, and the sky about it had taken a faint greenish tinge, like the light at the bottom of a pond. The hosier stopped talking. Everyone, for a moment, stood at the glass. Leadford, whose hand lay on the revolver beneath his coat, felt a strange sensation, as though the whole business of Nettie and Verrall, the ring unbought, the eleven shillings, had become a very small affair, in a very small room, which someone was about to close.

He did not go to Birmingham that night.

At Wolverhampton the train stood for an hour for no declared reason, and he got out and walked, because he could not sit. He walked out of the station and along a road of terraced houses, and then a road of fewer houses, and then a lane between hedges whose names he did not know. He had no plan. Beneath the sky the whole country lay silent, as if it too had stopped to look. A dog barked far away and ceased.

The comet filled a quarter of the heavens. The light it cast was queer and sourceless, not white nor yet silver, and the hedges and the puddles in the ruts had a dim, bottled green. He took out the revolver and examined it, and was amazed by its stupidity, a small black object, an ugly tool for an ugly intention. "I'll do it," he said aloud, and his voice sounded thin, a clerk's voice, on the vast and listening road.

Then he noticed that the wind had dropped and that the air had begun to smell, faintly, of something sweet and strange, like apples, like wet iron. A pale vapour was creeping along the ditch, lying low in the grass, rising in slow soft coils about his boots. The stars had gone out behind a luminous green veil. Far off, from the direction of the town, came the sound of a hooter, then a second, then silence.

He tried to lift the revolver, and found that his arm was heavy. A curious peace, as of going down into warm water, began to rise in him from the feet. He thought of his mother, folding her hands; of Parload on the roof, who would be delighted; of Nettie, tucking her chin into her collar. He thought, quite distinctly and without bitterness, "How small it all was." The green deepened to a blaze that was almost music.

The revolver slid from his fingers into the grass. He sat down, gently, at the roadside, as a tired man sits, and the light closed over him.
