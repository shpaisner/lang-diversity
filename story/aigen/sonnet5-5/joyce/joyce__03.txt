She was gone. The word lay on the oilcloth beside the teapot and the heel of the loaf, a small black word in a small round hand, and William Leadford looked at it as a man looks at a stranger's dog that has come in out of the rain and will not go out again. Gone with Verrall. Gone, it said, and was sorry, and hoped he would not take it hard. Hard. The kitchen clock ticked its tin tick over the range. His mother's boots were drying on the fender and from the yard came the clank of the pump, and beyond the pump the wall, and beyond the wall the bottle-ovens of Clayton squatting in their own smoke like fat priests at a black mass, and beyond them the marl-holes, the slagheaps, the canal sliding greasy and slow between its sooty banks.

Nettie. Nettie of the lane and the may-hedge on Sunday, her hand in his, her hand in the crook of his arm, the warm smell of her hair in the thin sun. We'll be all right, Willie, when you get on. On. On to what? Eighteen shillings and the clay under the nails, the saggar-maker's bench, the foreman's thumb. And Verrall with his gloves and his drawling, his motor and his lovely careless money.

He folded the paper in four, in eight, held it, and laid it on the coals. It browned, curled, bloomed a blue flame for a moment, and was a black leaf. Gone. Soot in the chimney stirred and fell.

—Willie? his mother called from above. Is that thee?

—It's me, he said.

It was not. Not altogether. Someone else stood in his boots.

He went out without his cap. The sky over the ovens was the colour of dishwater, and low in it, where the dusk was thickest, something pale hung and watched him.

Evening in Hanbridge Road, and the lamps lit one by one, each with its small cough of yellow. Men going home, men going to drink, a girl with a jug, a cart of chinaclay rumbling to the wharf. He walked among them like a man walking under water. At the corner a newsboy bawled, and the bawling came to him in pieces, Kaiser, Servia, mobilisation, and further pieces, the Comet, latest, Astronomer Royal, the two shouts running together till he could not tell which star threatened and which army, which end was to come with trumpets and which with the tick of a telegraph.

—Wars, said a man at the pump with a pipe in his face. Wars and comets. Same thing. Same bloody thing, mate. Somebody up there's lost his temper.

—It'll miss us, said another.

—It'll not miss the Germans.

They laughed. Leadford did not. He looked up. There above the chimney-pots it hung, a faint smear, a thumbprint of ash on the dark glass of heaven, and a green tinge about it like the green of a bruise a week old. Nettie under it, now, somewhere. Under it with him. Did she see it, lying in the sweet linen of his lie, and did she shiver, and did he draw her to him, saying, Don't be afraid, little one? He knew the very voice. He had heard it drawl across the Whitsun field and had hated it before he had a reason.

The pump handle clanked. Water gushed on the stones and ran in the lamplight, red, gold, black.

The Crown and Anchor, and the long room thick with shag and Staffordshire, and the world's end being argued in the dialect of the Potteries over mugs of mild. He drank one and tasted nothing. A man told him the Russians would stand by the Serbs, the French by the Russians, and England, God help her, would stand by whoever paid for the standing. A man said the tails of comets were poison, he had read it, cyanogen, a gas, a gas that would go through the world like a thief through a house. Another said priests had said so, in Hanley, in plain pulpit words. Another said it was only the newspapers selling themselves.

—What dost think, Willie? Thou'rt a reading chap.

—I think, said Leadford, and the word stayed in his mouth, stale, a pennyworth of nothing.

I think I'd like to see them both dead.

He did not say it. He said, —I think I'll have another, and he had another. The faces round him swam, red, kind, stupid, doomed perhaps, and he was sorry for them, and he was sorry for himself, and under the sorrow something cold and bright and level had begun to move like a fish under ice. He remembered the pawnbroker's in Bethesda Street. The window. The blue gleam among the watches, the wedding-rings, the little cradle of a concertina. Five and six, a revolver had been, with a card tied to it. Good order.

He put down his empty mug.

Midnight, and the pawnbroker's shut, but a knock will wake a man whose bread is the desperate; and in the lit crack of a door a face like a boiled egg regarded him, and said nothing of the hour. Money passed. Something heavy was wrapped in brown paper and passed back. Cold through the paper, cold as a frog.

—Cartridges?

—Six. In the chamber, he said. Thou'll not be wanting more, I'd say.

Hadn't thought it showed. Did it show? He walked the whole length of the town with the parcel under his coat, hot now, a live thing, his own heartbeat in it, and he said to the lamps, Verrall, Verrall, Verrall, to the rhythm of his boots. Each lamp took the name and let it fall. The ovens were cooling, ticking and sighing in the dark like beasts after labour; once in the burnt air a smell came, sweet, strange, not of coal. Imagination. And overhead the pale thing grew larger daily, as if it leaned down to listen.

Where were they? His mother's cousin in the Euston Road had said once, idly, as a thing said once, that Verrall's people had a house in Kent, up from Sevenoaks, where the lawns were like green billiard cloth. Kent. He would go to Kent.

He went by the morning train, third class, wedged between a commercial traveller and a hamper of hens, and the fields of the Midlands flew by in their wet green, Warwickshire, Northampton, the long grey straggle of the suburbs, all the sleeping chimneys of England. Papers rustled on every knee. Ultimatum. Spectrum of the Comet: Remarkable Green Band. Nobody looked at him. He held the parcel on his lap as a mother holds a child.

Rain in London, and a wet cab-horse steaming, the Thames the colour of lead under a bridge, and the clatter of the Kentish train in its cutting. He thought of her mouth. He thought of the little mole beneath her ear. And through the thought, like a draught under a door, came the knowledge that he could not bring himself to think of her dead. Only of him. Only of the other one. And yet she would be there, would stand between, would cry out, and the blue thing in his fist would not know the difference between a cry and a name.

The light was going when he left the road. A lane, a gate, a beech-hedge; and the sky above the Kentish hills was no longer dusk but a strange wide luminous green, as though the day had been dipped in some enormous bottle. Birds had stopped. Not one. A silence lay on the fields like a hand.

He climbed the wall. The grass was cold, and he lay in it, breathing, with the revolver and the thudding. Far off a bell began, a church bell, hesitating, then a second; then, thin and lovely, a long cry rose from the village, and ceased.

He lifted his head. The comet filled half heaven, and all of it was green, green of absinthe, green of young wheat, green of the sea on a day he had never seen, and out of it, softly, silently, as snow comes in a dream, something was descending. It lay on the hedge and thinned along the hollows. It crept across the lawn toward the lit windows of the house. He smelt it: faint, sweetish, clean, a smell like rain on iron.

Nettie, he said. His hand tightened. Then he laughed, and coughed, and the laughter went out of him like a candle, and the stars, the green stars, wheeled over the beeches, and the grass came up to meet his cheek, cool and kind and enormous.

And he thought, quite clearly, So that is what it was, and did not know what he meant.

Darkness, then, not as of night but of water, a deep warm drowning without fear, and in it something moving like a slow tide, washing the grit out of him, the soot, the shillings, the small hot hatreds hoarded year on year in the cellar of the heart. He saw the potbanks go, one by one, as lamps go out, and his mother's kitchen with its patient clock, and Nettie's note blooming blue and turning leaf. He saw the Kaiser's moustache and the Tsar's white tunic, the cannon, the long grey trains of men, fold softly down like paper theatres. He did not mind. Out of the green, out of the gone, a voice that was no voice said Wake, and it was his own.

He woke. Dawn, and the dew heavy on him, and a lark, a lark, going up out of the hedge as if flung. The sky was washed and enormous and a pure pale gold, and the air entered him sweet as water to a thirsting man. He lay and looked at it, and wept, easily, without shame, for no cause he could name.

The revolver lay in the grass beside his hand. He regarded it, a small dull ugly tool, as one regards a spanner found in a garden, and could not for his life recall what it was for.
