Willie Leadford woke to the knocker-up's pole rattling the glass and the pot-banks already coughing over Burslem, and knew before he knew anything else that she was gone. Gone. A small word, a word like a plate dropped in the scullery, and all its pieces still ringing. He lay under the greyed blanket and heard the kilns breathe, bottle-ovens bellying up their smoke into a sky the colour of old dishwater, and the clogs on the cobbles, clop and clatter, clop, the whole street going to its clay as it went yesterday and would go tomorrow. Nettie. Nettie Stanton with the grey eyes and the brown wool coat and the way she said Willie as if it were a thing she was lending him. Verrall had a motor. Verrall had a voice you could have poured into a glass. Verrall, Verrall, the name tolling like a gate on a Sunday.

He dressed. The collar stud skittered under the bed and he went after it on his knees, a grown man on his knees in Staffordshire, praying to a stud. The note lay on the washstand where Mrs Pargeter had put it with her thumbprint in the corner. I am sorry. It is better. Do not look for me. Better for whom? He read it with his lips. Better, better. The letters swam and would not set, and outside the clogs went clop, and somewhere a whistle blew for six, and he went down to the day like an ox to the yoke.

—Seen it last night? said Dakin at the bench, not looking up from his saggar. —The comet. Big as a thumbnail now, they say.

—Let it come, said Leadford.

—Aye, and take Staffordshire with it.

—It'd be a mercy.

Dakin laughed, a wet laugh with clay in it. The Signal went round at dinner, passed hand to greasy hand, and the men bent over it like communicants. A star with a tail, said the paper, that the learned gentlemen had measured and found wanting nothing but to arrive. And under it, on the same page, the same black type, as if God and Kaiser were competing for one column: Germany. The Fleet. Morocco again. Reservists. Dreadnoughts, Dreadnoughts, a word like a drum rolled down a stair. Somebody said there'd be a bust-up by Michaelmas. Somebody said the Frenchies would never stand. A small man in a cap said Let them all go at it, what was it to him, and Leadford thought, what was anything to anyone, and chewed his bread and cheese that tasted of the tin it was carried in.

Europe drifting, drifting like a boat with no hand at the oar, and the green star climbing over it all, patient as a creditor. And he, Willie Leadford, twenty-two, ill-shod, ill-read, ill-loved, with a brain full of bees.

She had stood by the canal on the Sunday, remember, remember, the water black and thick as stout, and said You do not see me, Willie, you see something you have made. And he had said what? Nothing. A cough. Something about the rent.

Verrall in the lane with the motor ticking like a hot clock, goggles pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyes, gloves, gauntlets, the smell of petrol and brilliantine and money. Miss Stanton. Might I? Might I. Oh, the lilt of it, the Oxford drawl smeared like butter over a knife. And Nettie's face turned up to it as a plant turns, not even knowing it turned. A leaf. A heliotrope. Hers the fault? Whose? His own, with his greasy cuffs and his tracts and his Socialism read out of a penny pamphlet in a room that smelt of cabbage. Mother of God, he had told her about the exploitation of the potter, the lead in the glaze, the Rights of Man, he had talked to her of the Rights of Man while Verrall talked to her of Paris.

Paris. Lights. A hotel with a bell. Her hair unpinned.

He struck the wall with the flat of his hand and the plaster came away in a small grey sigh, and Mrs Pargeter called up the stairs, Mr Leadford? Is there aught amiss?

Night, and the window open on the tiles and chimneys, and there it hung, over Hanley, over the Wedgwood works, a smear of pale green fire with its brush dragged behind it. Cold. Indifferent. It did not care for the dead or the jilted. It did not care for Willie Leadford. It cared for its orbit, its long ellipse, its appointment.

He had bought the revolver that afternoon from a pawnbroker in Hanley, a fat man with a fob who weighed it in his palm as one weighs a loaf. Five shillings. Say no more. Keeps off the tramps. Five shillings and six rounds in a twist of paper. It lay now on the quilt, small and oily and absurdly sufficient, like a toy God had dropped.

Shoot him. Shoot her? No. Him. Then himself, perhaps, a tidy full stop, a full stop in the middle of the great unpunctuated sentence of the Potteries. He saw the newspaper: Tragedy at. Where? Where were they? Dakin had it from a carter that Verrall's people had a house down in the Weald, Kent, Surrey, some soft green southern place where the hedges were trimmed by hands that did not know the blacking. Thither.

Thither, he said aloud, in the dark, and the word was fine and stupid and he was ashamed and did not stop saying it.

The train bore him south through the night like a long iron sentence, and he sat with his hand in his pocket on the cold butt of the thing. Rugby. Willesden. Lamps. At every station, soldiers, the first of them, boys with kitbags and the tinny cheer of a band, and women at the barriers with handkerchiefs, and a sergeant roaring Gangway, gangway for His Majesty's what? What? Newsboys ran along the platform crying, Ultimatum! Ultimatum! and the word went down the carriages as a spark goes down a fuse. A fat man opposite said it was the Germans' doing, always the Germans, and a thin man said it was the bankers, and a clergyman said the hand of Providence, and above the roofs through the smutted glass the comet rode with its green-white plume, huge now, larger than the moon almost, and nobody looked up but Leadford.

Look at them. Clerks, drapers, cutlers' sons, going to be shot in a field for a quarrel about a map. And himself going to shoot a man for a quarrel about a girl. Where was the difference? Both were maps. Both were girls. The train shrieked, the dark flew, and in his pocket the five-shilling truth lay warm now with the warmth of his hand.

He walked the last miles. Dawn came wrong, came green, a pale beryl wash climbing the east where no sun had yet risen, and the hedgerows stood in it like things drowned and shining. No birds. Or birds, but all silent, perched in rows along the telegraph wire with their small heads turned up. A cart stood in the road with the horse asleep upon its feet. A cottage door open, a woman on the step with her apron to her mouth, staring.

—It's come, she said to no one, to him. —It's come down.

He did not answer. He went on, his boots clopping, clop, clop, on the white road, and the air changed, grew sweet, grew thin and sharp and strange in the nose, like the smell after thunder, like apples, like the first morning of the world, and his head swam, and the hedges wavered, and the sky above him opened slowly into a vast pellucid green, a green with no bottom to it, a green like the inside of a wave. The revolver was in his hand. When had he drawn it? He looked at it. It seemed a childish thing, a bit of black tin, a sad little cough of metal. His breath came deep. Deeper than any he had drawn in Burslem. Nettie, he thought, and the name was not a wound now but only a name, a soft name, a leaf on water. Verrall. Verrall too, a name. And the war, the Kaiser, the small man in the cap, all of them, names, names, dissolving like salt in the great green tide.

He laughed. He could not help it. He laughed, and the laugh went up like a lark, and the knees went from under him, gently, gently, and the white road came up to meet him, soft as a pillow, and the world, yes, said the sky, yes, I will, Yes.
