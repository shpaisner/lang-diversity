Smoke first, and the slow sour breath of the bottle ovens, rising over Clayton and Hanley and Burslem in the dusk like the prayers of a people who had long ceased to believe in the answer. William Leadford walked home from the potbank with the grey clay still dried in the creases of his knuckles. Clay he was and to clay returning, so said the Reverend Mr. Whatshisname on a Sunday, and Sunday was a long way off and Nettie farther. Nettie. The name went in him like a thumb into soft slip, leaving its print. He looked up over the roofs. There it hung, low in the east, the new thing, a smear of pale fire above the chimneypots, not a star, not a cloud, a visitor with no card. The comet, said the men. It will strike us, said Old Pomfret, or miss us, or turn us all to ash, and spat, and went in to his tea. Leadford stood. The cobbles shone with the day's rain. Somewhere a dog, somewhere a concertina, somewhere a woman calling a child in by its whole name, Albert Edward, Albert Edward, come you in. And he thought of her hair in the lane at Bursley, the hawthorn white behind it, her laugh like a plate cracking cleanly in two.

In the Crown they had the paper spread among the glasses. Germany, said one, and Austria, and the Serbs, and the Czar, a great jostle of names like cattle in a pen, and every name a gun. The Kaiser's moustaches stood up from the page. Fine thing, said Pomfret, a bit of a war, thin the lot of us, and who'll miss us? Leadford drank. The beer was brown and bitter, the colour of the canal. He heard it all and did not hear it. Europe was drifting, they said, drifting like a barge with no man at the tiller, down toward some weir. Let it. What was Europe to a man with eighteen shillings and a mother coughing in a back kitchen, with a lamp that smoked and a Bible that did not? What was Servia? He thought of Verrall. Verrall, with his good coat, his soft white hands that had never held a thing heavier than a glove, Verrall of the great house and the grey motor, Verrall smiling at her over the gate. A gentleman. The word sat in his mouth like a pebble he could neither swallow nor spit. Drink up, Will, said someone. You look as if you'd seen the comet in your pint. And he laughed, a short laugh, a bark, and the laugh was ugly in the room.

The letter lay on the dresser against the teapot. His mother had propped it there with the care that is itself a blow. He knew the hand, the round loops, the y trailing like a tail. Dear Will, you will hate me and I cannot help it. He read it standing. He read it again, and the words went soft and ran together like wet glaze: gone away, London, Mr. Verrall has been kind, a different life, a life, Will, I want a life. A life. And what had he offered? A cottage of two rooms. A man's grey patience. Clay under the nails at the wedding. Behind him his mother said nothing, which was her way of saying it all, and the kettle ticked on the hob, and out the window the comet burned over the slag heaps, greener now, he fancied, a cold bright eye. She has gone up in the world, he thought, up like smoke. Thou shalt not covet. He covetted. He covetted her laugh, her hair, the white road of her throat, and the thought that another's mouth now knew them turned in him slowly, a key in a rusted lock. Gone, gone. The word went round the kitchen. The clock took it up. Gone, said the clock, and Gone, and Gone.

On the Tuesday he went to the pawnbroker's in Hanley and came out with the revolver wrapped in a copy of the Staffordshire Sentinel. Cold it lay against his ribs, a small iron conscience. Six chambers. He counted them in the privy behind the house, turning the cylinder, click, click, and each click a syllable: Net-tie. Ver-rall. Thus is a man made, he thought, out of nothing, out of a ledger and a wet Sunday. He kissed his mother's forehead and she smelled of camphor and coal. Where are you off, Will? Work, he said, and the lie too was a small iron thing. The train drew him south through the dark midlands, through Stafford, Rugby, towns he knew only as names on the sides of crates, and the carriage window gave him back his own face laid over the fields, haggard, unshaven, a stranger's. Across the glass the comet walked beside him, keeping pace. Everyone in the carriage spoke of it. A woman said it was a judgement. A commercial traveller said it was cyanogen, he'd read it, a gas, a green gas, tails and tails of it, and we'd all be poisoned in our beds by Thursday. Leadford said nothing. Let it come, he thought. Let it take the lot of us, but let me first. The wheels beat out their iron rosary all the way to Euston.

London swallowed him, and spat him out toward the Essex marshes where the letter's second address, got by a lie from a cousin, sent him. Evening again. The sky had changed. It was not night but a green dusk, an unearthly lucid green, like light under the sea, and the people in the lanes stood at their gates with their faces turned up, pale as fish. Somewhere beyond the Channel, men were being told to march, and the news came down the wires with a faint hum. War, war, they said, the word carried in the breeze like thistledown. He did not care. He found the house, a villa with a laurel hedge, a lamp in the window gold against the green. He crouched in the ditch. The grass was wet. His hand found the revolver and the revolver found his hand. And through the window he saw her, Nettie, bending, laughing, and a man's shadow crossing the curtain. His heart thudded, thudded, a clay-pit pump. Now, he thought. Now, Will Leadford. Do it now. But the air was strange. It sweetened. A faint smell of almonds, of sleep, of something like the first morning of the world, crept along the hedge and into his lungs, and the green deepened, deepened, and the stars swam. His fingers loosened. He heard a bird begin in the dark, absurdly, one bird. The sky was a great green silence. He thought, Mother, and then he thought nothing, and the ditch received him like a mother, like clay.

Light. A thin cold gold of light on his eyelids, and a smell of wet grass such as he had not smelled since boyhood, since the fields at Trentham. He lay. His mouth tasted clean. He lay and wondered at it, and slowly, as a man lifts a heavy lid, he raised himself on his elbow and looked out upon the morning. It was the same morning and not the same. The sky was the blue of a thrush's egg, rinsed, immense, and the hedge shone, every leaf distinct, every dewdrop carrying its little sun. Birds. A hundred birds. The comet was gone, or was everywhere, dissolved in the sweetness of the air. He put his hand to his side. The revolver lay in the grass, small, ugly, absurd, a child's rusty toy, and he looked at it as one looks at a word in a forgotten language. Why? Why had he wanted? The hatred had gone out of him in the night, drained like water from a pot, and what remained was a great clear pity, wide as the sky, pity for the poor hot fool in the ditch, and for Verrall, and for Nettie, all of them clay, all of them breathing. He rose. His limbs were light. Down the lane a figure was coming slowly, in the gold, a man bareheaded, and the man's face was open as a child's. Leadford went to meet him with empty hands. Yes, said something in him, yes, and the world, washed and astonished, said it back.
