Smoke over Staffordshire, the bottle-kilns standing in their rows like the stumps of a burnt-out choir, and under them William Leadford walking, boots slapping the wet cinder of Trafalgar Road. Slack and slag and the sulphur breath of the potbanks. Clay in his nails. Clay in his hair. Clay of his clay, dust to dust, and the note in his pocket going soft with handling, folded and unfolded since the morning's first post. Dear Willie, by the time you read this. By the time. Time had a time and she had gone, Nettie, Nettie Stuart of the brown hair and the gate in Sneyd Lane, gone up the line to London with a man whose father owned a motorcar. Verrall. A name like a door shutting softly in a good house.

He had kissed her under the hawthorn, once, and the hawthorn had said nothing. He had saved four shillings and sevenpence toward the ring. Four and seven. The figures ticked in him like a clock in an empty room.

A girl passed with a basket of saggars, laughing at some joke not his. The kilns breathed. Overhead the sky was the colour of dishwater, and behind the dishwater, unseen, the thing that Parload said was coming.

Coming, coming. All things come. Even she had come to him once, up the lane in her Sunday blue, and now was a smudge on a platform, a veil, a hand on a carriage door held by another hand. He spat in the gutter and watched it go.

Parload's attic smelt of paraffin and boiled cabbage and the cold iron of the little telescope, which poked from the skylight like a thin black finger accusing the heavens.

—Look, said Parload. Look, Leadford, man. You're not looking.

He looked. A smear, a pale blur, a fish-eye of light with a tail dragged behind it over the dark. It meant nothing. It meant Nettie's hair on a pillow not his.

—Cyanogen, said Parload, his spectacles flashing. The band's in the green. A poison, d'ye see, a gas, and we're to go through the tail of it. Some say it'll scour the air clean. Some say we'll all be choked in our beds like rats. The Astronomer Royal says nothing at all, which is wise of him.

Cyanogen. A word like a cough. Leadford turned it over: cyan, blue, gen, begetting. Begetter of blue. Verrall begat Nettie? No. The green begat nothing. The green was a rumour in the sky and the sky was far.

—Will it fall on us?

—It'll pass, Parload said, and rubbed his hands. Pass, pass, a great sweet rubbing of the air. Isn't it grand? And the whole street below busy with its pence and its pots, and not one of them to lift an eye.

Leadford lifted his, and saw nothing, saw the blur, saw her turn in the doorway of a hired room and let down her hair.

Newsboys on the corner of Hope Street, shrieking like gulls over offal. Crisis! Germany! Ultimatum! The words flapped in the wet wind. Men in mufflers crowded round the placards, argued over the Balkans, the Emperor's moustache, the fleet at Kiel, the Frenchman who would or wouldn't. A big man in a fustian jacket said he'd go, by God he'd go tomorrow, shoulder to shoulder, and give the Dutchman what for. Dutchman, Deutscher, all one to him.

Europe leaning, Europe drifting like a drunk against a wall, the great powers in their braided coats mumbling treaties into their beards, and over them all the green thing slowly swelling. Lord, what a week. Comet and kaiser. Wars and wonders and a girl gone off with her bundle.

—Will there be war, Billy? said a voice, a lad's, one of the Boulton brothers.

—There's war enough, said Leadford.

He thought: let them march. Let the whole mob of them march to the sea in their thousands with their bayonets winking, and let the sea take them. What was a continent to him? He had a country the size of a hawthorn, a room, a lane, and it was ransacked. Let the shells fall. Let the Potteries burn blue as a plate.

A gull screamed. A newsboy screamed. He bought a paper, and could not read it, for the print swam, every line a long dark river with her name floating in it.

The pawnbroker's was a dim cave of other men's misfortunes: watches, flutes, wedding rings strung on a nail like a rosary of the lost. Behind the glass a revolver lay on a bed of green baize, patient as a sleeper.

—Webley, said the man, spectacled, soft-fingered, smelling of camphor. Good as new. Better. A gentleman's piece, left by a gentleman.

—How much?

—Thirty shillings.

—I've eleven.

Eleven. The mathematics of misery. Then the watch, his father's watch, silver, ticking in his hand with its small dutiful heart. He laid it on the counter. It lay there ticking for the last time, tick, tick, a pulse running down. The broker weighed it, bit the case, sighed his professional sigh.

—Done, then. And cartridges. You're not, I hope, a young man in trouble?

—Rats, said Leadford. There's rats in the shed.

—Ah, rats, said the broker. There's many a rat.

The weight of the thing in his coat pulled his shoulder down, a hard cold fact against the ribs. Out in the street the day was dying, going green at the edges, a queer sour afterglow he did not remember having seen before, a light like the inside of a bottle. People looked up. A dog sat down in the road and howled.

Night, and the road to the south rising under him, hedge and ditch and stile, the black fields smelling of rain and cattle. He had walked since dusk, with the revolver in his pocket knocking his thigh at every step, a small iron heartbeat: Verrall, Verrall, Verrall. Somewhere beyond the hill a house, a lamp, a window. He knew where, for Parload's cousin had told him, with a wink.

How would it be? The gravel crunching. The curtain. Her face turning, white, her mouth making his name, and the click, and the roar, and then what? The rope. The black cap. Fine. And Nettie on the floor in her blue, in her Sunday blue, with the hair spread out. No. No. He stopped, and the thought broke on him like water through a roof.

He went on.

At a gate he leaned, panting, and looked up. The sky had opened. There it hung, the comet, no longer a smear, but a huge pale feather laid across the whole south, its tail trailing down behind the elms, and the stars going out one by one before it like candles in a wind. The air was very still. No bird. No wind. The cattle in the field stood motionless with their heads all turned the one way, as at a funeral.

The world was waiting, the way a woman waits for a word.

And the green came.

It came not as a flame but as a fineness in the air, a thinning, a delicate drowning of the dark in some pale tide that rose from the hedgerows, from the ditch, from the puddled road, a green like the underside of leaves, like the sea seen through the bottom of a glass. He smelt it, a sharp sweetness, apples and iron and rain. His hand went to the revolver and found it strange, a toy, a lump of cold stupid metal. His knees were going. The gate slid under his palm. Nettie, he said, or thought he said, and the name came out of him like breath out of a dying man, light, and green, and forgiven.

Far off a church clock began to strike and did not finish. Far off the whole sleeping island of men and women lay down in the green, in kitchens, in barracks, in the long beds of the sick, in the Cabinet rooms where the papers lay unsigned, and one by one let go. The kilns cooled. The flags hung slack. Somewhere a German sentry sat down on the frontier grass, put his rifle across his knees, and shut his eyes.

Leadford sank into the wet grass of the verge. The cold stars wheeled and were gone. Down, down, through the clay and the coal and the old red marl, through all the heaped-up yesterdays, the hawthorn and the lane, the four and sevenpence, the little ticking watch, down into a sleep as wide as the sky, and something in the sleep said, soft as a mother's mouth against his hair: rise.
