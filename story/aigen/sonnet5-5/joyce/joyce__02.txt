The bottle-ovens of Clayhanger stood in the dusk like the stumps of a giant's rotted teeth, and Willie Leadford, walking home from Swinnerton's with clay under his nails and the day's ha'pence in his pocket, saw them and hated them. Smoke. Smoke on the canal, smoke on the slag, smoke on the face of the boy who sold the Signal at the corner, crying the news. Comet. Comet seen in Leo. Germans at it again in Morocco. Buy a paper, mister. He bought no paper. He had read already in a girl's hand, in four lines pencilled, that she was gone. Dear Willie, don't look for me, it is better so, N. Better so. Better for whom, Nettie Stuart, daughter of the gardener, of the lane, of the hawthorn? Better so, said the ovens. Better so, said the clogs on the cobbles, clack clack, a clerk's clogs, a clerk's lot, a dog's. And she with Verrall, Verrall of the motorcar and the cigarette-case, the gentleman's voice that said rather and awfully and took what the poor man had only looked at.

He climbed the hill by Hanley and looked up. There it hung above the chimneys, a blur of pale fire, a thumbprint of God on the dark glass. Millions of miles, the paper said. Tails and gases. Cyanogen, said the paper, and Willie said it too, cyanogen, tasting the word like a sourness, a word for the mouth of an ordinary man to choke on.

Where had they walked? By Trentham, in May, the may out, her hand a small warm animal in his. Her hair under the hat the colour of a good brown glaze before the firing, and what is a glaze but a promise, a lie the kiln tells the clay? Willie, she said. Willie, you do go on so. Go on. Go on about the Rights of Man and the price of bread and the Duke's park walled up against the poor, and she listening, half listening, her eyes on a bird, on a cloud, on the road where one day a horn would sound, parp, and a young man in goggles would lift his cap.

Ah, but he had not seen it. Blind as the potter's wheel going round and round. Round and round the same grievance, the same lane, the same face. Nettie. Netty the net that caught. Nettie, nettle, the sting in the hedge-bottom, dock leaf to cool it, none.

He sat on his bed in Bursley Row with the gas unlit. Below, his stepmother's voice rose and fell in the scullery like water in a cracked jug. The comet at the window now, nearer, or he thought so. Nearer. Everything nearer. The rent, the grief, the war.

At the Four Alls the talk was of the war. Smoke thick as pease-soup, and old Fenwick banging his pot. Let 'em come, he said, Kaiser Bill and his Uhlans, we'll give 'em Staffordshire. Give 'em the sack, said another, give 'em the pot-bank, let 'em turn saucers for fourteen shillings. Laughter, brown, beery. The dreadnoughts, said a man with a red neckerchief, the dreadnoughts is only the bosses' playthings, you'll be in the trench and they'll be in the Grand. Who'll fight for the Duke? Who'll fight for Verrall?

Willie drank and said nothing. Verrall. The name went round in him like a coin in a hat. Europe a powder-room and every nation with a lit pipe. Austria, Serbia, Russia, the Rhine, names that were like the names of the dead. And what was a continent's quarrel to him, whose own small continent had been taken from him in a hedgerow? Yet it was the same, he thought, the same hunger. Somebody wanting. Somebody taking. A man here, a nation there, and the comet over all, indifferent, a great green eye on the stupid ants.

Time, gentlemen, said the potman. Out into the lamplit wet, Willie went, and in his pocket, under the ha'pence and the pipe, his hand closed on the cold knuckle of a thing he had bought on Saturday from a pawnbroker in Longton. Six chambers. Cheap at the price.

The train south. The third-class carriage smelling of oranges and wet wool, a woman asleep with her mouth open, a soldier's boy with his brass buttons. Rugby. Bletchley. The fields went by in the dark, black, ploughed, patient, and above them the comet had grown to a great pale feather, and the sky behind it was strange, a sea-colour, a bottle-green. Willie pressed his forehead to the glass. He would find the house. He had the address from Nettie's sister, got by lies and a sixpence. He would stand at the gate. He would say, Verrall. Only that. Verrall. And then.

What then? The word then lay open before him like a pit. He saw the shot, the white shirt-front blooming red, and Nettie's mouth, and he could not see what the mouth said. Forgive. Murderer. Willie. He turned the revolver over in the dark of his pocket, a cold fish. I am not a bad man, he said to the glass. I am a man who was wronged. And the glass said nothing, but gave him back his face, a thin Staffordshire face, ill-shaved, the eyes too bright, with the green light laid across it like a hand.

He found the cottage near dawn, or what should have been dawn, but the light came wrong, a weird viridian light from no sun, leaking up through the hedges and the elms. Birds had stopped. A gate. A path with box on either side. A window, a lamp. And at the window, her face, turned toward him as if she had known, white, the hair down, Nettie, and behind her the shape of the man.

He had the revolver out. Verrall, he cried, and his voice was a strange cracked bird. Verrall, come out. The door opened. The man stood there in his shirt, haggard, no longer the swell, only a young man afraid, and the air between them thickened and swam. Willie raised his arm. And the sky, all the sky, was green. A green like the inside of a wave, like absinthe, like the eye of a cat, and the hedge shivered and the elms bowed and a sweet strange smell came over the garden, not death, no, not death, something like apples, like the first cut hay, like a window opened in a house that had been shut for years.

He fired. He thought he fired. The sound went soft in the air, a cough in a feather bed. He saw Verrall's mouth open on a yawn. He saw Nettie sink with her hand along the sill, slow as a swan folding. His knees went. The path came up like a pillow, kind, kind. Yes, he thought, and there was the comet, whole, the great green mother of it opening above him like a flower, and he thought, as the world went out, Oh, let it.

Then the waking.

Not as one wakes in Bursley Row to the knocker-up's pole and the klaxon of the works, the heavy head, the grey dread coming in like damp. No. He woke as a child wakes, entire, and it was morning, a true morning, the first, with the dew on the box hedge and a blackbird in the elm pouring out its small gold. The air was washed. It had a taste like cold water drunk from the hand. He lay on the path with the gravel printed in his cheek and the revolver in the grass beside him, ugly, ridiculous, a tool for some other, lesser man, and he looked at it as one looks at a toy found in the garden of one's youth.

He sat up. The sky was blue, and so blue it hurt, and in it no comet, no feather, only a high thin lingering of green fading like a breath on a mirror. And his heart, that clenched fist of a heart he had carried up from Clayhanger, lay open in him, quiet, unhurried, wise.

Verrall was sitting on the doorstep with his head in his hands. Nettie was beside him. They were weeping, he saw, and not for themselves alone. And Willie rose, and found that he was weeping too, and that it was good, it was like rain after a long drought. He crossed the gravel, empty-handed, and he said, softly, Nettie. Verrall. And they looked up, and there was no hatred in any face.

Over the hedge the whole shining country lay awake, and far off, a bell began, and then another, and Europe, he knew, would not go to war today, nor ever, yes, he said, yes, yes, and the morning said yes.
