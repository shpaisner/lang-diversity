Gone. The word was a bottle oven, round and hollow and smoking, and William Leadford walked about the inside of it. Gone gone gone, said his boots on the setts of Burslem, past the Town Hall's sooty pillars, past the pawnshop with its three golden balls like the bad eggs of a bankrupt hen. Clerk, twenty-two, a pound a week less the shilling for the club. In his pocket the note, folded to a hard little square, and he took it out under the lamp at Swan Square and read it again as one presses a bruise. Dear Will, don't hate me. I could not bear the Potteries any longer and I am going with Mr Verrall. N.

Mr. Verrall. Mister. The mister of gaiters and motorcars and the white clean hand on the gate at Checkshill. He whom the slip and the marl and the saggar-makers' bones had never touched. Clay to clay, said the clergyman on Sundays, but not that clay, not his, not the kind that dried to a fine white glaze and sat in drawing rooms bearing roses.

Nettie. Nettie Stewart with the brown hair that smelt of rain on a slate roof. Nettie at the tap in Sneyd Street with her sleeves rolled. Nettie, Nettie, netted, caught, flown. The furnaces breathed their red upon the low clouds and the clouds breathed it back. A dog nosed a cabbage stalk in the gutter. He had not eaten. He did not think he would ever eat again, and yet his stomach spoke, low and base, a clerk's stomach, mocking the tragic heart that housed it.

Parload's attic smelt of paraffin and cold tea and the damp of old star charts. A telescope poked its brass snout through the skylight like a creature sniffing the night.

— Look, said Parload. He was a thin man with a cough and a conviction. — Look, Leadford, and stop your moping.

He looked. A smear, a pale thumbprint on the black glass of heaven, and from it a faint feathered tail like a breath on a window.

— That's it, Parload said. — That's your comet. Coming for us at I don't know how many thousand miles a second. And it's brought a new line in the spectrum. Cyanogen, I think. And something else. Something that isn't anything I know.

— I don't care for your comet.

— No, said Parload, patient. — You care for the girl. But she's a small thing, Will, and that's a big thing, and when a big thing comes it makes the small things look foolish.

Foolish, he thought. Fool. The Fool of the tarot, walking off a cliff with his bundle on his stick, and the little white dog yapping. He saw Nettie in a railway carriage, her gloved hand in Verrall's, the telegraph poles flicking past like the pages of a book that said nothing.

— Does it hit us? he asked.

— Nobody knows. It brushes us perhaps. The tail. Gases.

— Gases, he said. And the word tasted green and cool, like the idea of sleep.

At the corner of Waterloo Road the newsboy bawled the Sentinel, and the bawl broke up in the wet like a rotten plate. Germany. Ultimatum. Fleets. Morocco. Servia. Mobilisation. Men stood under the lamp in their mufflers reading by one another's shoulders.

— Be war afore Christmas, said a man.

— Be bloody good thing, said another. — Clear the air.

— Whose air? Yourn? Muckle's? Hark at him.

Leadford stood among them and was not of them. Europe was drifting, so said the leader-writer, like a barge cast loose upon a darkening river: Kaiser and Czar and the old man of Vienna in his stiff gold collar, the French with their sullen red trousers, and we ourselves with our dreadnoughts and our Daily Mail, all sliding to the weir. And the men under the lamp wanted it, for their wages were thin and their wives were thinner, and here was something large to be angry at that was not the master or the pot-bank.

He thought: let them. Let the whole bloody continent go up like a kiln with the damper out, and Verrall in it, and she. Let the guns talk their big stupid language over the Rhine. He would be in no regiment but his own. His war was small and exact, one-roomed, a war of the hand, and it had a name in it, and the name had two syllables, and the stress fell upon the first.

In the lane behind the Bycars Road, long ago, that is to say the May before, among the hawthorn that smelt of old women's sweat and of honey. She had said, I would like to see things, Will. Not do. See. And he, ass, stupid ass of a bread-and-butter lover, had said, there's the Exhibition at Hanley.

Hanley. Her laugh then, the little ripple on its back. And he had laughed too, to be companionable.

And the Verrall, when he came, came with the sun upon him as upon a god in a bad picture, his motor throbbing at the end of Trubshaw Street like a great black beetle, and every child in the road at its side. He had spoken to Nettie once, only once, in Leadford's presence, and had lifted his hat, an act of such trivial and bottomless insolence that William had gone home and sat in the dark. Lifted his hat to her; to him, not even that.

Now the night wind shook the gas flame. Now the clock of St. John's struck eleven in its flat Methodist voice, and every stroke dropped like a coin into the black purse of the town. He had a sovereign and eleven shillings saved against a wedding, a wedding, God in his ceiling. Cheap at the price, the dealer in Hanley would say. Cheap. Cheap.

The revolver lay in his hand like a small cold animal. A Webley, secondhand, five-and-twenty shillings, with a brown stain on the grip that might have been rust or another man's grief. The dealer had wrapped it in the Sentinel. Mobilisation. He carried the war to Euston in his breast pocket, against the heart that wished it dead.

The train went south through the night, and the carriage held a commercial traveller snoring in a bowler, a woman with a hamper, and himself. Rugby: lamps, milk cans, a porter's long cry. Wolverton. Tring. Past Watford the clouds tore, and the sky stood open, and there it was without a telescope, the thing itself, pale green and pointing, a wound in the dark with its light seeping out.

The woman with the hamper woke and looked at it.

— Don't like it, she said. — It's not natural.

— It's a comet, ma'am.

— I know what it is. It's looking at us.

He turned to the glass and saw his own face laid over the sky, a thin Staffordshire face, unshaven, a little mad, with the green light passing through it as through a bottle. Somewhere in a hotel in Surrey or a villa in Kent, Nettie lay asleep with her hair undone. He would not think of her hair. The wheels said Verrall, Verrall, Verrall, and then, as the train slowed through the suburbs, they said nothing at all.

Dawn did not come right. It came sickly, a long way off, the colour of a lemon left in a drawer, and over it the comet hung unfaded and larger, its tail laid across the zenith like a bridge of glass.

He walked from the station along a road of villas whose names mocked him from the gateposts. Rosedene. The Laurels. Mon Repos. A milk cart stood in the road with the horse asleep upon its feet. He walked past it. A policeman leaned on a pillar-box, helmet bowed, the very picture of vigilance if one did not look. He did not look. He had the address from a letter folded in Nettie's mother's Bible, written in the slanting hand of a man who had never carried coal. The Hollies. A name.

The air changed. He felt it before he could say it: a thinning, a sweetness, as of apples in a loft. His step lengthened. The sparrows had stopped. The pistol knocked against his ribs, and he drew it and held it before him, absurd, a boy's toy, in a street of closed curtains where all the world had ceased to breathe its habitual small lies.

— Verrall, he said aloud, to the green and silent morning. — Verrall.

And his voice went from him light as a bubble. The hedges stood strangely clear, each leaf bright-edged, each thorn a separate wonder. Somewhere a long way off, over the Channel perhaps, the drift of Europe slowed, hung, waited. He lifted his face to the comet. His knees were loosening, kindly, like a knot in a rope. The gate of The Hollies stood before him, and beyond it a path, and beyond it a door, and he thought, quite clearly and without any anger, I am so tired, and the green came down like water over a stone, and over the sleeping policeman, and over the road, and over the wanting in him, and the revolver slid from his open hand and rang once upon the gravel and was still.
