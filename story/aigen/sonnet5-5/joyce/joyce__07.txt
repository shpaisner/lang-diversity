He came down out of the bank at six with the clay still in the creases of his knuckles, and the sky above Swinbourne's chimneys the colour of an old tooth. Leadford. William. Willie to nobody now. The hooter went and went and the men poured out through the gate like grey slip from a spout, clogs on cobbles, talk of beer and the Port Vale and the Kaiser, and he among them but not of them, a stone in the stream. Six days a week the saggar and the kiln, the glory-hole of fire, and a man's lungs furred white as a biscuit. And for what. For a room in Bursley Street with a cracked ewer and a candle-end and a girl's face in his head.

Nettie. Her name went through him like the cold through a wet boot.

Look at the sky, Will. Some wag by the gate pointed. Look, there, that's her coming, the comet. They say she'll fetch us all up in a heap by Christmas. Laughter. Leadford looked and saw nothing, a smear, a pinprick in the dusk, a pale thumbmark on the glass of heaven. Let it come. Let it break the potbanks and the parson and the pawnshop three-balled over the way. What was it to him, a star that burned a hundred million miles off, when the one burning thing in his own small world had been taken from him?

He walked home by the canal, past the black water with its skin of oil that took the lamplight and bent it. Nettie, Nettie. Her hand once on his sleeve under the lime trees at Trentham, her breath, the smell of her hair like bread. Had he dreamed it? Had he been dreaming all his twenty-two years?

The letter had been under the door when he came in, a white tongue in the dark. He knew the hand before he lit the candle. He lit the candle all the same, slow, slow, as a man delays at the lip of a cold bath.

Dear Willie, by the time you read this I will be gone. Don't look for me. Don't think hard of me. Mr. Verrall has been very kind.

Kind. He read it again. Kind. The word sat on the page like a fat black beetle. Verrall of the Hall, Verrall of the grey motorcar that went through the Potteries with a snort and a stink and left the children coughing in the road, Verrall with his white spats and his easy laugh, his father's money warm in his pocket like a live thing. Mr. Verrall has been very kind.

He sat on the iron bed and the springs cried under him. Outside, a dog; a late tram grinding at the corner of Hanbridge; a woman's voice calling a child in off the street, Alfie, Alfie, come in this minute. Somewhere under the floor the landlady's clock, tick, tick, the small teeth of time.

Had she stood there in the Stewarts' kitchen, tying her bonnet, one hand at her throat? Had she looked back? He saw her at the door, saw the motor throbbing at the kerb, the lamp-eyes lit, the leather glove held out to her, the white cuff. And she going, she going, the green hedges lifting and sliding past, the whole county unreeling behind her like a ribbon cut.

He did not weep. He put the letter in his mouth and bit it, tasting paper and ink and salt.

In the Cock and Bottle on Saturday they were all at it, the newspaper going round like a hot potato, thumbed and greased. Germany this and Germany that. Dreadnoughts. Morocco. The Frenchies mobilising at some place with a name like a sneeze. Old Gaffer Boulter with his clay pipe in his fist banged the table and said let them come, let the Hun come and we'll give him what for, and a lad in the corner said it's the bosses want it, the bosses and their gunmakers, it's never the likes of us. Shush, said the potman, we'll have none of that here.

Leadford drank. The bitter was brown and warm and tasted of pennies. Beyond the smoke and the sawdust and the flushed faces he heard it all as if through a wall: Europe, a great shuddering beast with its nostrils flared, the armies rising in their millions like gnats at evening, the long lines of railway trucks and the horses, and under it all the slow tick of a clock that nobody could stop. And he thought, with a cold amusement: they will shoot one another over a word, over a line on a map, and I am here with my one small private war.

The comet again, said somebody. It's in the Echo. Green, they say. Green as a bad egg. Professor says it's got a gas in it, a new gas, cyanogen.

Cyanogen, said Boulter. What's that when it's at home?

Poison, said the lad. Prussic. We'll all go off like candles.

Good riddance, said Leadford aloud, and the room laughed, not knowing.

On the Tuesday he drew his wages, and on the Wednesday he went to Birmingham and came back with a thing in his coat that weighed more than anything he had ever carried. A pawnbroker's revolver, oiled and blue, with a smell of old Sundays. He laid it on the counterpane and looked at it as a man looks at a letter he has not yet dared to open.

Verrall. He tried the name in his mouth: the two soft ls, the little r that rolled like a pebble, the snobbery sleeking every vowel. Verrall. A name to be taken out of the world like a tooth.

They were at a house, he had learned, a furnished villa in the south, by a sea that he had never seen, and the train would take him there by morning. He packed a clean collar and a heel of bread. He wrote nothing. Who was there to write to? His mother in the churchyard at Burslem under her little slate; his brother gone to Canada and the silence.

At the station the night was thick with expectation. Porters on the platform stood with their heads thrown back, and the people at the windows, and the guard with his lamp lowered, all looking up. The sky had a flaw in it, a bright and bilious smear, slowly widening over the roof of the goods shed. And the engine breathed and breathed, and he thought: I will do it, I will, I will, and the thought rolled in him with the wheels, yes, yes, until even the word was only noise.

By night the heavens had turned. Green, a green no painter owned, a green of bottle glass and of cold sea, of the dead and the unborn, poured down over the long roofs of the south, and every slate and puddle and pale upturned face drank it in. Leadford stood on the gravel before the villa with the revolver heavy in his pocket and his heart a hammer. Lamps were lit behind the blinds. A piano. A woman's laughter, thin and bright, which he knew, which he would have known in Hell.

He took the thing out. It shone green.

And the air thickened, sweetened, grew soft as breath against his lips; a strange mild odour, as of crushed leaves after rain, rose about his knees. The laughter in the house stopped. A dog somewhere whimpered once and ceased. He heard the whole country go still, the way a held note goes, the long cold hush of wheels and furnaces and tongues. Nettie, he said, or thought he said. His knees were water. The pistol slid out of his fingers and fell, silent, into the grass. Verrall, he said. And the green rose over him like a tide, and the stars went out one by one, gently, and the ground leaned up to take him in its arms.

He woke with the dew on his face.

Birdsong. A sound as though the world had been newly rinsed. He lay a while, looking up into a sky of such a pure and scalding blue that he thought: this is the first morning, there has never been another. The grass smelt of itself. His hand, flung out, touched something cold, the blue barrel, and he drew it into his palm and looked at it, curiously, as a man looks at a toy that once belonged to a child he was.

What had he meant to do with it?

He sat up. His head was clear as a washed cup. Every thought stood in its place, bright and separate, no longer crowding like the men at the gate. He saw the villa with its blinds half drawn and thought, quite simply: there are people sleeping in there. Verrall. Nettie. He turned the names over, and there was no knife in them. Only a vast astonished pity, a tenderness that rose in him like water in a well, for her, for the white-spatted fool, for the potters and the kaisers and Boulter with his pipe, for his own bitter hunched self upon the iron bed with the letter in his teeth. How small it had been. How terrible and small.

Along the road a man was coming, bareheaded, laughing quietly to himself, and the light struck his face and showed it wet. He lifted a hand to Leadford, and Leadford, rising, lifted his. Neither spoke. There was nothing, now, that needed to be said. Beyond the hedge the whole bright country was waking, field on field, to a day that no one had yet been taught to spoil; and the revolver lay in the grass behind him, rusting already in the dew, a thing from another age, while he walked down into the morning with his empty hands.
