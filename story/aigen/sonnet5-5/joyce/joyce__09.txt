Clay, clay and the smoke of clay. Under the slagbank the bottle-ovens stood in their hundreds like the stopped throats of a drowned choir, and the night came down on Staffordshire as it always came, sulphur-yellow at the rim and sooty at the root. Willie Leadford stood with his hands in the pockets of a coat that was not warm. Cold. Cold as a saggar-maker's bucket. Above the chimney of Mawdsley's the thing hung in the east, a smear, a thumbprint of pale fire on the black slate of the sky. The comet. They said it grew by an inch a night. The Signal said so, and the Signal knew nothing, but it said so.

Nettie, he thought. Nettie Nettie Nettie. The name went round like a wheel on the potter's foot, spinning the wet lump of his heart into some shape he had not meant, a vase, a pisspot, a thing. Gone. Gone with Verrall in his motorcar that stank of petrol and money, the lamps blazing, the dust rising behind them upon the Trentham road and settling, settling, upon the hedges where he had kissed her.

Ten shillings in the week. A clerk's stool. A mother's cough in the back kitchen. And what had he to offer a girl with eyes like that? Rain, he said aloud. Rain and a stool. The slag shifted under his boot and rolled, clinking, down into the dark, the broken crockery of a thousand dead dinners, willow-pattern, the little blue bridge and the two blue lovers fleeing over it forever, forever pursued.

Pursued, aye. Let him look to it.

The comet said nothing. It was green at the edge, he thought. Or was it the gas?

He went down.

Four years before, and the same lane in May. Hawthorn, the smell of it like something said behind a hand. Nettie Stanton with her hat in her fingers, her hair loosened by the wind, saying, Willie, you do go on so. And he had gone on. Of Socialism, of Mr Blatchford and the Clarion, of the day when no man should be a stool to another man's hunger, and she had looked at the hawthorn and not at him, and he had not seen it, the looking away, he had not seen.

Fool. Fool in a collar.

Her father's house, the aspidistra in the window, the antimacassar, the harmonium on which her sister played Abide with me on Sundays in a voice like a kettle set to soften. Respectability, pale as lard. And into this, like a blown fuse, Verrall: young Verrall of the Park, the glove, the gaiter, the little moustache, who had leaned on the gate with a cigarette and said something clever, God knew what, and Nettie had laughed. Laughed. A bell of silver cast in the foundry of the rich.

Ah, but she was hungry, he knew. Hungry for the lit rooms, for the world beyond the kilns, for a life that was not placing the saucer upon the cup upon the shelf. He knew it. Knowing was no use. Knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone.

He saw her now in the motorcar going, going, her veil blown back, and Verrall's hand upon the wheel, and the hand upon the wheel became another hand, and he shut his eyes in the dark lane of his skull and the hand stayed there, white, ringed, easy.

The Red Lion, Saturday, the fug of shag and wet wool. A fire. A spittoon. Sawdust that had drunk deeper than any man.

— It's the Germans, said Boulter the placer, thumping the paper. It's the Kaiser and his bloody fleet. Mark me.

— Mark thee arse, said Dukes.

— Morocco. Bosnia. Serbia, wherever the hell that is. Every damn week a new place to fight about. Mark me, lads, there'll be a day.

— Let 'em fight, said an old man by the settle, who had not spoken since Michaelmas. Let 'em. We'll make the plates they eat off.

Laughter, a rattle of coughing. Willie sat with his stout going flat before him. Dreadnoughts, said the paper. Conscription. The Triple Entente, the Triple Alliance, triple, triple, a word like a stammer. Somewhere across the grey water men in spiked helmets drilled in the squares of towns whose names he could not say, and in Whitehall old men in frock coats moved coloured pins, and the pins were men, and the men were Boulter and Dukes and himself, himself, a pin with a clerk's stool.

— And that, said Boulter, jerking his thumb at the window and the smear beyond it, that's the end o' the lot. Comet. Cyanogen, they say. Poison gas in the tail. We'll all be dead by Whitsun.

— Not Whitsun, said Dukes. Dead of drink first.

Leadford rose. The door swung; the cold took him. There it hung, bigger. A blister on heaven. Let it come, he said, and did not know if he said it aloud.

The letter lay on the oilcloth beside the teapot, and his mother watched him from her chair as he read it, her hands folded on the shawl like two dead birds.

Dear Willie, I am not going to say I am sorry because you would not believe it and perhaps I am not. I could not breathe here. You will say I have done wrong and perhaps I have. Do not follow us. Do not think badly of

He did not finish. The paper shook in his hand, shook as the whole kitchen shook, the dresser, the willow-pattern plates, the clock with its tin heart ticking out the minutes of his shame.

— What does she say, Willie?

— Nothing, Mother.

— She was always a flighty one.

— Nothing. Go to bed.

On the stairs the dark was thick with the smell of old Sundays. In his room, beneath the shirts, in the tin box that had held his father's medals and his father's burial club book, lay the other thing, got of Dukes's cousin for four-and-six: a revolver, black, small, with a cylinder like the stopped chamber of a rosary. He turned it to the candle. It looked back at him, one round eye. Do not think badly of. Of whom? Of whom was he not to think badly?

The candle guttered. In the window the comet was laid across the glass, and the glass turned it green.

He put the revolver in the pocket where his heart had been.

The morning train to Euston. Third class, a smell of oranges and carbolic, a woman opposite with a basket and a child that stared. Fields went by in the grey, and canals the colour of old pewter, and the pale leaning spires of villages that had never heard of Willie Leadford and never would. Wheels. Rails. Verrall, Verrall, Verrall, said the train, Nettie Nettie Nettie, said the train, and under that, lower, the thing that was not a word, only the clack of iron on iron, iron on iron.

They had taken a house, a man at the station had said, near Lord Verrall's, or Verrall's father's, or the Verrall who was not the Verrall, he had lost the thread. Villas. Gravel. A lawn on which a girl from the pottery might stand in a white dress and be somebody's wife. Not wife. He turned the word and it would not lie flat.

Newspapers rustled down the carriage. Ultimatum, said one headline, and below it, smaller, the astronomers: Contact with the Earth's atmosphere expected within the week. The child still stared. A man in a bowler spoke of gold, of Consols, of the Bourse, as of a sick relation. And the light in the carriage was strange, a quality of the light, as of a room seen through a bottle: a thin green bleed along the edge of the windows, along the white cuffs, along the child's upturned cheek.

— Mammy, said the child, why is the sun sick?

— Hush.

Willie Leadford looked at his own hands. They were the colour of drowned men's hands. He thought of Nettie on the lawn. He thought of the little round eye. And the train carried him south and the sky opened its great pale mouth above the fleeing hedges, the vowel of it, the long O of the end of things.

Dusk, and the gate. A name in black iron letters, The Larches, and the larches themselves like a row of dark women with their arms raised. Gravel, as he had known. A lit window, yellow, domestic, a lamp behind a curtain, and moving across the lamp a shadow, a woman's, the tilt of the head he knew as he knew the grain of his own pillow.

He stood. The air had changed. It was not cold now but thick and strange and still, and the whole sky had gone the green of a bruise beginning to heal, of the glaze in the pot when the fire has first kissed it, before the true colour comes. No wind. No bird. Far off in the north, beyond London, beyond Staffordshire, beyond the pins and the Kaiser and the Signal, something huge drew in its breath.

The revolver was heavy against his ribs. He took it out. The metal was green too. He held it and it was a small cold absurd thing, a toy for boys at fairs, and he thought: I shall go up the path. I shall knock. He will open, with his cigarette. I shall say her name.

And then what, Willie? And then what?

The shadow crossed the curtain again and paused, as if listening. Perhaps she felt him there. Perhaps she, too, looked up, and saw the sky grown strange, and was afraid, and wished for a hand, any hand, the old hand, the poor hand with ink upon the fingers.

His own hand opened. Or closed. He did not know which. The green was in his mouth now, sweet, faintly, like almonds, like the hawthorn, like the end of a long argument. His knees were soft. The gravel swam. The lamp in the window bloomed, and widened, and he heard, or thought he heard, all the bottle-ovens of Staffordshire sigh out together their last black breath, and the sigh went up into the green, and the green came down.

Nettie, he said. And the gate was open, and he was walking, and the night, the night, the night was singing.
