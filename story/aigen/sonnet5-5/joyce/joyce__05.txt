Dust and the smell of the Potteries, clay and coal and the sour breath of the gasworks, came in under the door of the room in Cobridge where William Leadford sat with Nettie's letter flat upon his knee. Sat. Read. Read again, as a man will press a tooth to learn the ache of it. Dear Willie, by the time you read this I shall be gone, and you must not think too hard of me. Gone. A small word, a word like a coin dropped in the slot of a machine. Gone with Verrall, Verrall of the long hands and the long motorcar, Verrall of the Hall that stood above the smoke where the air was a different air.

He rose. The candle bowed. Through the window the bottle-kilns stood in their ranks like the stumps of some burnt and giant forest, and above them, between two chimneys, hung the thing the papers had been shouting of for a week. The comet. A smudge, a thumbprint of pale light on the black glass of the sky, with a tail like the tail of a thought he could not finish.

Thinking of you, Nettie. Thinking of nothing else, God help me. Thinking and the thumbprint looking down, indifferent, cold, come out of the dark beyond the dark to see a potter's clerk with a letter in his fist.

He crushed it. He smoothed it again.

She had walked with him once along the Trent and Mersey, the canal lying flat and brown as strong tea, the barges nosing through with their painted roses and their patient horses. She had worn the grey hat. A wind off the slag heaps had lifted a ribbon of it and she had laughed, head back, the white of her throat. He had said nothing then. He was a man who said nothing, who kept the whole great clumsy cargo of it below hatches, and let the days go by like barges, slow, slow, towed by the dull beast of wages.

Twenty-two shillings a week. Twenty-two and the dream of a cottage, a bit of garden, a girl at the gate.

And Verrall had said a word and the gate was open.

What word? What word does a gentleman have, that a poor man has not? He turned it over: come. Only that, maybe. Come, with the engine throbbing at the kerb, and the leather seats, and the veil, and the roads running away south like silver rivers to places where no one's father coughed in the night. Her father coughed. Her father had the clay in his chest, the potter's rot, and the girl had looked at him, at Leadford, at the cottage, at the garden, and had measured them, and found them small.

Small. He said it aloud in the empty room. Small.

The Pig and Whistle was loud with it, the war. Tobacco hung in blue shelves. A man in a cap struck the table with his palm and the glasses leapt.

— The Kaiser'll have us, I tell you. Have us by Michaelmas. Ships, they're building, ships by the dozen.

— Let him come, said another. Let him come and be damned.

— And what about the star?

— Comet, said Parload, small Parload with the observatory eyes, who had come in rubbing his hands though the night was mild. Not a star. A comet. I have seen it in the glass, Leadford, the spectrum of it, the bands. Cyanogen, they say. Poison. The earth will pass through the tail of it.

— Poison, says the man in the cap. Wonderful. Germans in front and poison behind.

Leadford drank. The beer was warm and tasted of pennies. Cyanogen. A pretty word, a word for a lady in a novel. He saw the long tail sweeping across Europe, over the Rhine, over the barracks and the barges, over the Balkan hills where some small quarrel smouldered and some old emperor signed a paper, and he saw the nations lean toward one another in the dark like drunken men who have not yet decided to strike. And Nettie in a hotel somewhere, in the lamplight, with her hair down.

— You're white, Willie, said Parload.

— It's the light.

— There's no light here.

The pawnbroker's was shuttered, but Leadford knew the man, and the man knew the price of silence, and by midnight Leadford walked home with a small cold weight dragging at the pocket of his coat. A revolver. Six chambers. Six little rooms for six little sleeps. He had not said what he wanted it for. The pawnbroker had not asked, rolling his wet eyes toward the ceiling, toward the lodger asleep above, toward the weather.

— For burglars, Leadford had said. And the man: Aye. They're thick this year.

Thick this year. Everything thick. The streets of Hanley thick with fog and thick with the thing behind the fog, the vast silence that was not silence but the held breath of a world. He passed a church. He passed a chapel. He passed a hoarding where a hundred bills for Pears' Soap curled in the damp. Good morning. Have you used. He passed a woman asleep in a doorway with a child inside her shawl, and he looked at the child's face, and the face was no one's, a face of tallow, and he thought: Even this will be swept, even this, the tail will take it, and he walked faster, hand in his pocket upon the cold small certainty.

I will go to him. I will stand before him. I will say her name.

At dawn he took the road to the Hall, climbing out of the smoke. Hedge and gate and hedge. The sun a wafer of dirty brass. Larks, if there were larks, he did not hear them. He heard instead his boots, the iron of them, saying Nettie, Nettie, Nettie on the stones, until the word lost its meaning and became only a sound, a sound a boot might make.

He had read in the Signal that the Verralls were at home. That the young master had returned from the Continent. That a party was expected. Continent: another word. A great slow fish of a word, swimming in a sea of maps.

On the crest he turned and looked back, and the Five Towns lay below him in their bowl of smoke like a stew gone cold, and out of it the kilns rose, and the chimneys, and the little red streets where he had lived his whole life without once asking what it was for. Mother, father, Sunday school, the ledger. Who had ordered it? Who sat above and ruled the lines?

He thought: I have been a good boy. I have been so good. And look.

By evening the sky had changed.

He lay in a dry ditch at the edge of the Verrall park, the great house gleaming beyond the beeches with its windows lit, a faint music wandering out across the lawn, a waltz perhaps, some thin sweet nothing played by hired men. The revolver lay beside him in the grass. He had not yet touched it. He watched the light.

For the light was wrong. It came green. Not the green of leaves but a green behind green, a green with no earthly name, leaking down the sky from the throat of the comet, which now filled a quarter of the heavens and no longer looked like a thumbprint but like an eye, a vast and patient eye with a lid of fire. The beeches turned to bronze and then to something else. The windows of the Hall paled. The music faltered, and he heard, far off, a dog begin to howl, and then another, and then a silence in which his own heart struck like a hammer upon a hollow cask.

He thought of Nettie, and for a moment he could not see her face. He thought of Verrall, and could not see his. He thought of the Kaiser, the ships, the guns, all the little men with their little maps, and they stood before the green like clerks before a judge, and he almost pitied them.

The air thickened. It tasted strange upon his tongue, sweet, and sharp, and clean, like water drawn from a well no one had known was there. His fingers loosened. The grass bent. And William Leadford, who had said nothing all his life, opened his mouth to say something, and breathed.
