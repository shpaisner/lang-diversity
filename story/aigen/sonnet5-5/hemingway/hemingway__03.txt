The letter was on the table when he came in from the works. It was not long. Nettie wrote that she was sorry and that he would understand someday. He did not understand it then. He read it twice standing up and then he sat down with his cap still on.

Outside the window the chimneys of the Potteries were smoking. The kilns had been fired that morning and the smoke lay low over the town and went brown where the sun touched it. He had walked home through it. He had thought about Nettie, and about her father's house, and about the Sunday he had taken her up on the hill where you could see the whole yellow valley.

Verrall had a motorcar. That was the thing he kept coming back to. Verrall had a motorcar and a good coat and hands that had never held anything heavy.

He took off his cap. He folded the letter and put it in his pocket. He was not hungry, but he cut bread and ate it standing at the window, and when it was dark he went out and looked at the sky.

The comet was there. It had been there for a week, small and pale above the roofs. Now it seemed larger. He looked at it a long time. It did not mean anything to him. It was only a thing that was in the sky.

In the public house on Hope Street they talked about the comet and they talked about the Germans. The two were mixed together so that you could not tell which frightened the men more.

"It's the end of the world," a man named Gedge said. "It says so in the paper."

"It says the Germans are mobilizing, too," said another.

"Let them come," said Gedge. "Let the comet come and all."

Leadford drank his beer and said nothing. The paper lay open on the bar. There were pictures of ships and a picture of the Kaiser with his mustache turned up. In the corner there was a drawing of the comet with a long tail like a lady's veil.

He had not slept. He had lain on the bed in his clothes and listened to the trains in the yard. At four in the morning he had decided something, though he could not have said what. It was in him like a stone in a boot.

"You're quiet, Willie," Gedge said.

"Yes."

"It's the girl."

"It's nothing."

"She went off with the gentleman," Gedge said. "Everyone knows. There's no shame in it."

Leadford looked at him. Gedge stopped talking and drank. The room was warm and smelled of wet wool and tobacco. A man by the fire was singing low and badly. Through the window the comet hung over the chimneys, and the war was in the paper, and nobody in the room could do anything about either of them.

He finished his beer and went out.

He bought the revolver in a shop off the Hanley road. The man did not ask him anything. It was a small black revolver with a worn handle, and the man wrapped it in brown paper like a loaf of bread.

"Six shots," the man said. "Mind how you carry it."

"I will."

He paid with money he had saved for a ring. That was a fact and he did not think about it. He put the package inside his coat and walked to the station.

The train to London was full of men going to see about the war. They sat with their newspapers and their hats on their knees. A woman across from him held a child and looked out the window at the dull fields. Once the child pointed at the sky and said something, and the woman pulled its hand down.

Leadford sat with the package against his ribs. He knew where Verrall's people had a place in the south. Nettie's sister had told him, crying, in the street. He had the name written on the back of an envelope.

He watched England go by. It was gray and green and the hedges were wet. There were cattle standing in the fields with their backs to the wind. He thought that he would find the house and knock. He did not think past the knock.

Toward evening the light went strange. The woman with the child looked up, and then everyone looked up.

The house stood back from the road behind a wall. It was a small house, white, with a porch and a garden that someone had tended. He stood at the gate. His boots were wet and his legs ached from the long walk from the station.

There was a lamp in the window. He saw a shape move across it. It was a woman, and he knew the way she carried her shoulders.

He stood there a while. He put his hand inside his coat and took hold of the revolver. It was cold and it did not feel like anything that belonged to him.

Then the door opened and Verrall came out on the porch. He was in his shirt sleeves and he was smoking. He looked young and tired. He looked toward the sky and he did not see Leadford at the gate.

"Hell of a night," Verrall said, to no one.

Leadford said nothing. His heart was going hard. He thought, now. He thought it quite clearly. He would walk up the path and it would be done.

But Verrall turned his head then and saw him. The cigarette stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Who's that?"

"Leadford."

"My God," Verrall said. He did not move. "You've come a long way."

"I have."

"Nettie's inside."

"I know it."

They looked at each other across the dark garden. Behind Verrall the lamp burned yellow. Above them the sky had begun to turn a color that Leadford had never seen. It was green, and it was coming down.

The green came over the garden like water. It was not a cloud. It was in the air itself, and it made the leaves look wet and the white house look like glass. Leadford saw Verrall's face go strange in it. He saw Verrall put out a hand to the porch post.

"Do you see that?" Verrall said.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

He took the revolver out then. He held it down along his leg. It seemed a small and foolish thing to be holding. The air tasted sharp and sweet at the back of his throat, like a fruit he had never eaten. He breathed it in because there was nothing else to breathe.

Down the road a dog began to bark and then it stopped. Everything stopped. There was no wind in the hedge and no sound from the village. He thought of the men in the public house and of Gedge saying let it come.

"Nettie," Verrall said, and his voice was thick. He turned toward the door. He took one step and sat down on the boards of the porch like a man who has run too far.

Leadford's knees would not hold. He was not afraid. That surprised him. He knelt in the wet grass by the gate and the revolver slipped out of his fingers and he did not reach for it.

The green was in his eyes and in his head. He thought, very slowly, that it was beautiful. He lay down on the grass. The last thing he saw was the lamp in the window, small and yellow, going soft at the edges. Then it went out, and he slept.

He woke with the sun on his face.

He lay a while without moving. The grass was wet under him and it smelled of earth and of morning. A bird was singing somewhere in the hedge. It was the first thing he heard and it was a clean sound, and he listened to all of it.

He sat up. The sky was blue. It was the bluest sky he had ever seen. The green was gone, and the comet was gone, and the air was light and cold and tasted of nothing at all, which was good.

The revolver lay in the grass a yard from his hand. He looked at it. It was an ugly thing. He could not remember why he had carried it so far. He remembered, but the reason had gone thin and far off, like something that had happened to another man. He picked it up and opened it and shook out the six cartridges into the grass. Then he put the revolver down again.

On the porch Verrall was sitting up with his back against the post. He had his hands open on his knees and he was looking at them. He looked at Leadford and Leadford looked at him. There was nothing in his face to hate.

"I thought you'd shoot me," Verrall said.

"I thought so too."

"I'm glad you didn't."

"So am I," Leadford said, and it was true.

The door opened behind Verrall. Nettie stood in it with her hair down and her eyes wide. She looked at the two of them and at the sky. She put her hand to her mouth, and then she was crying, but it was not an unhappy crying.

Leadford got up. His legs were stiff. He stood in the garden and the sun was warm on his shoulders and the bird went on singing. For the first time in a long while he was not tired.

"Come and have breakfast," Nettie said.

He went up the path.
