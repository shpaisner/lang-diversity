The comet had been in the sky for three weeks, and Leadford had stopped looking at it. He walked home from the pot-bank in the dark with the smoke of the kilns lying low over the Potteries. The street was wet. The lamps were lit in the windows, and in the end house a woman was singing something about a sailor.

He let himself in. His mother was dead and the kitchen was cold. He did not light the fire. He sat at the table in his coat and looked at the letter that had been there since Tuesday. It was short. Nettie had not been a woman for long letters. She wrote that she was sorry and that she was going with Verrall. She wrote that he would understand someday. He did not understand now.

He folded it and put it in his pocket. Then he took it out and read it again.

Verrall had a good coat. Verrall had hands that had never held anything heavier than a cane. He had come to Staffordshire in the spring to look at his father's holdings. He had looked at Nettie too, and Nettie had looked back.

Leadford got up and went to the window. The comet hung over the chimneys, small and green-white. It did not seem to care about any of them. He drew the blind and sat down again in the dark.

At the Crown the men talked about the comet and the Germans. They said it was one or the other, and maybe both.

"It'll hit us in October," said a man named Gammon. He was a glazer with a red neck. "Or it'll miss us. The paper says it'll miss."

"The paper says the Germans will miss us too," said another. "Then they'll come in over the water."

They laughed. Leadford drank his beer. It was thin and tasted of the tap. He had not told them about Nettie, but they knew. A town like that knew everything by the second day.

"Leadford," Gammon said. "You've gone quiet."

"I've nothing to say."

"The comet's got nothing to do with you. Nor the Kaiser."

"No," Leadford said.

A boy came in with an evening paper and the men crowded around it. There had been another incident on the frontier. Ministers were in conference. Reserves were being called somewhere. Leadford read the headline over a shoulder and felt nothing. A war was a big thing and far away. What had happened to him was small and close, and it sat in his chest like a stone.

He finished the beer and went out. The street was cold and clean after the smoke of the room. He stood a while on the step. Everyone in the world, he thought, was waiting for something to fall on them. He was only waiting for one man.

On Saturday he went to Hanley and bought a revolver from a man in a back room who asked no questions. It was an old Webley, heavy and dark. The man wrapped it in brown paper and took four shillings more than it was worth. Leadford paid. He put six cartridges in his waistcoat pocket and carried the parcel under his arm like a loaf.

He did not feel like a murderer. He felt like a man who had a job to do. That was better. When a man had a job he did not have to think about the other things, like her hair, or the way she said his name, or the summer on the canal path.

On the tram home he sat by the window. A soldier sat across from him, a young one, with a red face and new boots. The soldier looked at the parcel and then away. Outside, the sky was clear and the comet was there already, though it was only afternoon, a pale smear above the roofs.

At home he unwrapped the pistol on the kitchen table and cleaned it with an oily rag. He had never fired a pistol. He broke it open and closed it. He loaded it and unloaded it. He did this four times. Then he loaded it again and set it down.

He knew where they had gone. Nettie's sister had told him, crying, at the door. The Verralls had a house in the south, in Kent, and the son had taken her there. It was a long way. He would need money for the train.

The train left Stoke at six in the morning. He had a third-class ticket, a loaf, a piece of cheese, and the revolver in his coat. The carriage smelled of wet wool. Men sat close together and did not speak.

At Rugby there were soldiers on the platform. They stood with their kit about them and an officer walked up and down with a paper in his hand. A woman cried at the end of the platform. Nobody on the train looked at her.

"It's coming," said a fat man across from Leadford. "I've had it from a man who knows."

"The war?"

"Both."

The fat man opened a newspaper and held it up between them.

Leadford looked out the window. The country went by, wet and brown and very orderly. There were hedges and sheep and a church. It looked like a country that did not know. A boy on a bridge waved at the train and Leadford did not wave back.

By noon the sky had begun to change. It was not much. A tinge, a thinness in the light. The green of the comet had spread, and it lay along the horizon like something spilled. The fat man put down his paper and stared. Then he picked it up again.

At London he changed stations and waited three hours in the buffet. He ate the cheese. He drank a cup of tea so strong it was almost black. He kept his hand on the pistol through the coat.

He reached the village at dusk. It was small and had one street and a pub with a sign that said the Plough. He asked a man at the gate for the Verrall house. The man looked at him for a long time.

"Up the hill," he said. "You can't miss it. Big place. Lights."

"Are they at home?"

"Everyone's at home tonight," the man said. "No one's going anywhere."

Leadford went up the hill. The road was chalk and it shone under the strange sky. The air was thick and still. There was no wind, and there were no birds. He could hear his own boots and his own breath. Above him the comet had grown enormous. It was no longer a smear. It was a green thing, a bright cold eye, and the whole heaven was stained with it.

He stopped once and looked back at the village. Lights were burning in every window. Down in the street people stood in the open, with their faces turned up. Nobody was talking.

He thought about Nettie. He tried to think of her as she had been on the canal path, in the yellow dress, laughing. But the picture would not stay. It kept turning into the picture of the letter. He walked on. His hand was on the pistol. It was warm now from his body, and it felt like a part of him.

At the top of the hill the gates of the house stood open. There was a drive with elms along it. At the end of the drive a window was lit, yellow in the green dark.

He went up the drive slowly. The gravel was loud. He told himself he would go to the window first and look. He would see them. He would know what to do.

The air had begun to smell strange. It was sweet and sharp, and it stung a little at the back of the nose. He coughed. A yellow-green haze was lying low among the elms, and it moved without wind. He thought it was mist. Then he saw that the sky itself was coming down.

He reached the window and looked in. It was a library. A lamp was lit. Verrall sat in a leather chair with a glass in his hand, and Nettie sat on the floor by his knee with her head against his leg. They were not talking. Her face was turned toward the window, white and wide-eyed, and she saw him. She did not scream. She only looked.

He took the pistol out. His arm came up. It was very steady. He thought, now. He thought it quite clearly.

Then the haze reached him. It was in his throat and his eyes, and the world went green and bright. He heard himself laugh. It was not a bitter laugh. It was something he had not heard from himself in years, helpless and clean. The pistol was heavy and he did not know why he held it. He looked at it as a man looks at a tool he has forgotten the use of.

His knees let go. He sat down on the gravel. The elms went round slowly overhead. He could hear Verrall's voice from the room and it was not angry. It was only a voice. He thought that he had been very tired for a long time.

The pistol slid from his hand. The last thing he saw was the sky, wide and green and kind. Then he slept.
