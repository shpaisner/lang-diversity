The letter was on the table when he came home from the works. It was not long. Nettie wrote that she was sorry and that he would see it was better this way. She wrote that she had gone with Verrall. She did not write where.

Leadford read it standing up. His hands were gray with clay and left marks on the paper. He put it down and washed at the pump in the yard, the water cold and the soap hard. His mother watched him from the door.

"Well," she said.

"She's gone."

"I know she has. Her mother was here."

He dried his face on the sacking towel. Beyond the roofs the kilns smoked and the sky over Staffordshire was the color of a dirty plate. He had known Nettie since they were children. He had thought it was settled. He had not had money to settle it properly, and now he understood that was what the matter was.

"Eat something," his mother said.

"I'm not hungry."

"Eat anyway."

He ate. The bread was good and he could not taste it.

The comet was in the evening sky now. You could see it before the lamps were lit, a smear of pale light with a tail like breath on glass. In the Crown the men looked at it through the window and then looked at their beer.

"It'll hit us," said Gedge, who dug for the clay company.

"It'll not hit anything."

"The papers say the stars are all gone out of line."

"The papers say a lot."

Leadford sat at the end of the bench. He had one pint and it lasted a long time. Someone had a newspaper and read from it. The Germans were moving. The French were moving. There was a place in the Balkans with a name nobody could say. The men said there would be a war and that it would be good for trade. Then they said it would be bad for trade. Then nobody said anything.

"You're quiet, Willie," Gedge said.

"I've nothing to say."

"It's the girl."

"It's not anything."

Gedge nodded. He was a big man and he did not push. Outside the comet hung over the chimneys, and it did not seem to care about the war or the girl or any of them. Leadford looked at it a long time. He found he hated it. It was easier to hate than the other thing.

On Sunday he walked to the Verralls' country place. It was six miles. The house had a lodge and a gravel drive and rhododendrons that looked as if they had never been rained on. He did not go in. He stood at the gate and a man in a gaiters came out and asked his business.

"I want to know where Edward Verrall is."

"Mr. Verrall's abroad."

"With a girl."

"Mr. Verrall's abroad," the man said again. "I'd go home if I were you."

"I'm going."

He walked back along the canal. The water was black and still and a barge lay tied at the lock with no one on it. He thought of Nettie in a train, in a hat he had never seen. He thought of her laughing. He stopped thinking of it because it did him no good.

At the lock a boy was fishing. The boy said the comet would end the world on Thursday. Leadford asked him how he knew.

"Everyone knows."

"Then you'd better catch something before then."

The boy laughed. Leadford did not. He went on walking, and the light went out of the day.

He bought the revolver in Hanley from a man who asked no questions. It was an old Webley, heavy and dull, and the man threw in six cartridges wrapped in a bit of newspaper. Leadford paid with what he had put by for the ring.

He cleaned it that night at the kitchen table after his mother had gone up. He did it carefully. He had never owned a gun and he did not know much about them, but the man had shown him how it broke open. He loaded it and closed it and sat with it in front of him.

It was very quiet. The clock ticked. Somewhere a dog barked at the sky.

He did not think he was a bad man. He thought that a man who had worked since he was twelve and been honest and been left for a gentleman's son might be allowed one thing. He did not say what the one thing was. He put the revolver in his coat and put the coat on the hook and went upstairs.

He did not sleep. At four he got up and looked out. The comet had grown. It was a hard green-white now and the whole street lay under it as under a moon, every slate and chimney pot very clear. He stood at the window until the light changed.

In the morning he took the train south.

The house was in Kent, on a hill above a wood. A man at the station in Maidstone had told him the way, and told him too that the gentleman and the young lady had taken the place for the month. Leadford walked out in the afternoon. The country was strange to him, soft and green, with hedges and little orchards, and not a kiln anywhere.

The air was queer. He noticed it on the road. There was no wind, and the birds had stopped. A cart stood in a lane with the horse hanging its head and the carter sitting on the shaft not speaking. A woman at a gate looked up at the sky and crossed herself, though she did not look like a woman who did that.

"Is it tonight?" she said.

"I don't know."

"They say the tail touches us tonight."

"Then it does."

He went on. The revolver pulled at his pocket. He kept his hand on it. The sun went down red behind the wood and the sky above it was a color he had never seen, a thin clear green like the water in a bottle. He thought, I will do it and then it will be done. He did not think past that.

At the top of the hill he saw the house. A light was lit in a downstairs window. He stopped in the road.

He went in through the garden. The grass was wet and he could feel it through his boots. The green light was in everything now. His hands were green, and the white wall of the house, and the leaves. He took the revolver out and held it down along his leg.

Then there was a smell, sharp and sweet, like nothing he knew. He breathed it and his chest felt strange. He thought of the men in the Crown and Gedge saying it would hit us. He took another breath. His knees did not seem to belong to him.

"Not yet," he said aloud. "Not yet."

He went on toward the window. He saw the lamp and a table and a man's shoulder and the back of a woman's head, the hair he knew. He raised the revolver. His arm was heavy. It was very heavy. The barrel wavered and he watched it as if it were someone else's.

The green came up around him like water filling a pit. It was soft and cold and then it was not cold. He heard himself laugh. He did not know why he laughed. He tried to find the hate and it was there, small and hard, like a stone in his shoe, and he reached for it and could not close his fingers.

The revolver fell into the grass.

He sat down beside it. The sky over the wood was all one shining color and the stars were back in it, thousands of them, very near. He thought of his mother and of the bread. He thought of Nettie as she had been at fourteen, in the lane behind the pot-bank, with a blue ribbon. He had not remembered the ribbon in years.

"Well," he said.

Nothing answered. Everything was very still and very clear. He lay back on the wet grass and looked up, and he was not afraid, and that was the strangest thing, that he was not afraid at all.
