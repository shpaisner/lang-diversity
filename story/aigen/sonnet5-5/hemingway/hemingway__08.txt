The letter was on the table when he came in from the works. It was a short letter. Nettie had never written long ones.

I am going away with Edward Verrall. Do not look for me. I am sorry, Willie. You were always good to me but I could not stay.

He read it standing up. The kitchen smelled of the clay that was in his clothes and of the cold tea his mother had left. He put the letter back on the table and smoothed it flat with the side of his hand. Then he went out into the yard and stood there.

The chimneys of the Potteries were smoking against the evening. The slag heaps were black and the sky over them was the color of dishwater. Above the bottle ovens, low in the west, was the comet. It had been small a month ago. Now it looked like a smear of chalk on a slate, and it had a tail.

He looked at it for a long time. He was twenty-two years old and had never been farther than Stafford.

"Damn you," he said. He did not know if he meant the comet or Verrall or Nettie. He went back inside and did not eat.

In the morning he did not go to the works.

At the Red Lion the men had the paper spread on the bar. They were not talking about Nettie. They did not know about Nettie yet.

"It will be the Germans," a man named Haddon said. "Mark me."

"It will be the French."

"It will be everybody."

Leadford took his beer to the corner. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He listened to them argue about ships and treaties and the Kaiser, and about what the comet meant, and whether it would hit. A man said it was only gas. Another said gas was enough.

"I don't care if it hits," Leadford said. He had not meant to say it aloud.

They looked at him. Haddon looked at him longest. Then he looked back at the paper, because he knew. Everyone knew in the end.

Leadford finished the beer. He thought of Verrall, who was a gentleman's son with soft white hands and a way of talking that made a poor man feel like a dog. Verrall had come up to the Potteries to see how the other half lived. He had seen it all right. He had seen Nettie.

Outside it was getting dark. The comet was bigger than the night before. There was a green edge to it now.

Leadford walked to the pawnshop on Hope Street. He had four pounds saved. He bought a revolver for three of them and six cartridges for the rest. The pawnbroker did not ask him anything.

He took the train south. The third-class carriage was full of men who talked about the war and women who talked about the comet. A child ate bread and dripping and looked at him. He kept his hand in his coat pocket on the revolver and looked out the window.

The country went by. It was green and he did not see it. He saw the letter. You were always good to me. That was the worst of it. A man could stand being hated. He could not stand being thanked.

At Euston he asked a policeman how to get to the station for Kent. The policeman told him. London was loud and gray and nobody looked at anybody. At the bookstall the placards said Crisis and Ultimatum. A newsboy shouted something about the Balkans.

He knew where they had gone. Verrall's father had a house in the country, and Nettie had once let it slip, laughing, that Edward had said it had a garden with a wall.

He got to the village at dusk and walked out of it. The road was white and empty. The hedges smelled of dust. He found the wall at the end of a lane and a gate in the wall, and beyond the gate a lit window.

He did not go in. He stood in the lane and watched. After a while a woman crossed the window. She did not stop. She did not do anything. She only crossed the light, and he knew her walk.

He took the revolver out and held it down against his leg.

The sky had changed while he was watching the window.

He noticed it when the hedges began to look wrong. The light was thin and strange. He looked up and the comet was across half the heavens, huge, with a green fire spreading out of it like water into cloth. The whole country was green. His hands were green. The gate was green.

A dog began to howl somewhere, and then another.

He thought: Now. Do it now, before it is the end of the world. He put his hand on the gate. It was cold and wet with dew.

There was a noise on the road behind him. Marching. He turned and in the green light saw men in column, a long dark line with rifles. They were not marching well. They had been marching all day. An officer on a horse went past and did not look at him.

"Where are you going?" Leadford said to the last man.

"Dover," the man said. "They say it's started."

"What's started?"

"All of it."

The man went on. The boots went on. Leadford stood with the revolver and thought how small it was. It had seemed like a great thing in the pawnshop. Now it was a piece of iron in his hand, and the sky was burning green over England, and the men were marching toward a war that nobody wanted.

He smelled something sweet and sharp on the wind. It did not smell like anything he knew.

He breathed it in. He breathed it in again. His knees went soft and he sat down in the road.

He thought of Nettie when she was sixteen, at the edge of the canal with her shoes in her hand. He thought of his mother's kitchen. The thoughts were very clear, and they did not hurt.

The sky was the color of a new leaf. The soldiers had stopped on the road. Some of them were sitting. One lay on his back with his rifle across his chest and his mouth open. The horse stood with its head down. Nothing moved anywhere.

He lay back on the road. The dust was soft. The revolver slipped out of his fingers and he did not reach for it.

It's only gas, the man in the pub had said.

That's all right, he thought. That's all right, then.

He closed his eyes. The last thing he knew was the great green light going through his lids, and a feeling, which he could not explain, like a hand lifted off his chest after a very long time.

He woke with the sun on his face.

For a while he did not move. The road was under his back, and it was only a road. The air was clean. It tasted like water. A bird was singing in the hedge, and he listened to it as if he had never heard one.

He sat up. The soldiers were stirring along the road. They sat up and looked at one another and at their rifles. One of them laughed, a short surprised laugh. Another one took off his boots and put his feet in the grass. The officer had got down from his horse and was standing with his cap in his hand, looking at the sky. It was blue. The comet was gone.

The revolver lay in the dust by his knee. He picked it up. It was heavy and ugly. He opened it and shook the cartridges out into his palm, six small brass things, and looked at them. He could not remember why he had wanted them.

He remembered all of it. That was the strange thing. The letter, the pawnshop, the train, the window. It was all there. But the anger was gone, burned out of him in the night like a splinter drawn from a thumb. What was left was a clean sore place and, under it, something that felt like pity.

He put the cartridges in the ditch and threw the revolver after them.

The gate was open. He walked to it and looked in. There was a garden with a wall and a woman in the garden. She was standing among wet roses with her hands hanging. Beside her stood a man, bareheaded, thin-faced. They were not touching. They were looking at him.

Leadford stood there. The bird went on singing.

"Nettie," he said.

She did not run, and she did not look afraid. She looked at him as though they had all woken in the same room.

"Willie," she said. "Come in. Come in and sit down."

He went in.
