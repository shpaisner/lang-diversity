William Leadford came out of the works at six. He washed at the tap in the yard and the water was cold. The smoke from the bottle kilns lay low over the town and the cobbles were wet. He walked home by the canal. He did not look at the water.

The room was the same. The table was there and the two chairs, and the shelf with the cups. On the shelf was the letter. He had read it so many times that he no longer needed to read it. I am going away with Mr. Verrall. Do not look for me. You would not understand and it is no use. It was in her round hand. She had learned it at the board school, and it was neat.

He sat down on the chair and kept his boots on.

Verrall was the son of the big house at Checkshill. He had a motorcar and good gloves. Nettie had been a parlourmaid there for eleven months. Leadford had walked out to the gates on Sundays and she had come down the drive in her hat. They had talked about a house, and about two rooms and a garden. He had saved four pounds.

He did not cry. He had thought he might. He sat until the light went and then he lit the lamp and ate bread and cheese. The bread was stale. He ate all of it because there was nothing else to do.

Outside a dog barked, and then stopped.

In November the comet came into the papers. It was a small thing at first, a paragraph below the football. A smudge in the telescopes, and the astronomers said it would come close.

By December a man could see it. Leadford saw it from the yard of the works, when the others had gone. It was a pale thing low in the east, with a faint tail, like a thumbprint on a window.

"It'll fetch the end of the world," said old Hinchcliffe, who fired the kilns.

"It'll fetch nothing," Leadford said.

"There's a green in it. They've found a green line. A new gas."

"Let it."

He meant it. He found he did not care what the sky did. He worked and he ate and he slept badly. In the public house the men talked about the comet for a night or two and then they talked about Germany. The newspapers said the Germans were building ships. The newspapers said there would be a war in the spring, or in the summer. A man named Parload said the Germans were only waiting. Another man said the Russians were worse.

Leadford drank his beer. It was thin and a little sour. He listened to them and thought that all of them talked like men who wanted something to happen. He wanted something to happen too. He did not know what it was.

When he walked home the comet was higher. It was bigger. He stopped on the bridge and looked at it, and it did not look like anything.

In January he heard where they were. A girl from the Checkshill kitchen told his sister, and his sister told him in the street, with her face tight and sorry. They were in London. They had rooms in Chelsea. Verrall had quarreled with his father and there was no money, or not much.

"She's not married to him," his sister said.

"I know it."

"Willie, don't do anything."

"What would I do?"

He went back to the room and took down the tin box from under the bed. There was a revolver in it. He had bought it from a man who had it from a soldier, and he had never fired it. He had bought it in December without knowing why. He turned it over in his hands. It was heavy and cold and smelled of oil.

He put six cartridges in it. Then he took four out again and put them in his pocket. He did not know why he did that either.

That night he did not sleep. He lay with his eyes open and the comet's light came faintly through the blind. Toward morning he heard the first cart on the road. He got up and shaved with care. He put on his good collar. He knew that he was going to London, and that once he knew it, it was not a thing he could stop.

The train was full of men going south. There were reservists in the third-class carriages with their boxes tied with rope. The war had not come, but they were being called as if it had. At Rugby the platform was crowded and a sergeant shouted. A woman held a baby up to a window.

Leadford sat with his shoulder against the glass. The revolver was in his coat. Across from him a soldier with a red neck was telling a story about Aldershot. Nobody listened.

"You're not for the colours?" the soldier asked him.

"No."

"You will be."

"Maybe."

"There'll be plenty for everyone." The soldier laughed. He had good teeth. He offered a cigarette and Leadford took it and smoked it down to his fingers.

Outside the country went by gray and flat and wet. The fields were empty. The comet was not visible in the day, but he felt it there, behind the cloud, getting closer. He thought of Nettie in a room in Chelsea. He thought of her hair. He tried to think of Verrall's face and could only think of the gloves.

At Willesden the train stopped for an hour. Men leaned out of the windows and shouted at nothing. A newsboy ran along the platform with a placard. Leadford could not read it. It did not matter. He put his hand on the revolver and kept it there until the train moved.

It was dark when he came out of the station. The streets were thick with people. They were not going anywhere. They stood in the road and looked up, and he looked up too.

The comet had swollen. It filled a third of the sky. Its light was not white now but a pale and sickly green, and the faces of the people under it looked drowned. Nobody spoke. A horse stood still in the shafts of a cab, and the driver stood at its head with his hat in his hand.

Leadford started to walk. He went along a wide street and then a narrow one. He had the address on a paper but he did not look at it. A policeman leaned on a wall with his eyes closed and his hands loose. A woman sat on a step with her shawl over her head. He thought they were drunk. Then he saw a man lying in the gutter with his cheek against the stones, breathing slowly, and he saw that the man was not drunk.

The air tasted strange. It was sweet and thin, like the air after lightning. His head became light. He stopped and put his hand against a lamppost.

"Nettie," he said.

The green came down the street like water. It did not hurry. He saw it go over the cab and the horse and the driver. He thought, this is the end of it, and he was not afraid. He took the revolver out of his coat and held it, and it seemed a very small thing. Then his knees went and the stones came up soft against him, and the light went out.

He woke in the cold, and for a while he lay and did not move.

It was morning. The sky above him was a high clean blue, such as he had not seen over any town. The air was sweet. It went into him like cold water. His head was clear. He lay on the stones and looked up and everything that had been tight in him for months was gone, as a fist is gone when it opens.

He sat up. The revolver lay beside him. He looked at it for some time. It was an ugly, stupid thing. He did not pick it up.

People were rising in the street. They got up slowly, one by one, and looked at each other. The policeman stood and put his helmet straight. The cab driver was crying and did not seem to know it. Nobody spoke at first. Then a woman laughed, quietly, and a man said, "Well," and no one answered, because there was nothing to answer.

Leadford stood. He was hungry and his legs were stiff. He thought of Nettie and of Verrall, and he found that he thought of them gently, as one thinks of two people who have been very much deceived. He did not know what he would say to them. He thought that he would find them, because they would be waking too. He thought they would be frightened.

He left the revolver on the stones and walked up the street. The sun was coming over the roofs, and he walked toward it.
