The letter was on the kitchen table when I came home from the works. It was under the sugar basin so it would not blow away. I knew the handwriting. I did not need to read it, but I read it standing up with my cap still on.

It said she was sorry. It said she could not go on the way we had been going. It said Verrall would be good to her and that I should not look for her. She had signed it Nettie, as she always did, with the tail of the last letter going down under the line.

I folded it and put it in my pocket. Then I sat down on the kitchen chair and looked at the stove. It was out. My mother had gone to bed early and the house was very quiet. Outside the kilns of the Potteries burned along the valley and the smoke lay over everything the way it always did.

I had been eleven years at the works. I made eighteen shillings a week. Verrall had a motorcar and a father with land in the south. I had known all that. I had known it every day she walked out with me on the canal path and let me hold her hand and talk about the rooms we would have.

I went up and lay on the bed in my boots. I did not sleep.

That week the comet was in the sky every night. It hung over the slag heaps after dark, small and pale, with a smudge of tail. The men at the Red Lion talked about it and about the Germans. They said one was as certain as the other.

"It will hit us," said Tom Bayliss. "It will knock the world flat."

"It won't hit anything," said old Hackett. "It's the Germans you want to look at."

"The Germans are only the Germans."

"They have the ships."

I drank my beer and said nothing. They knew about Nettie. Everyone in Burslem knew. They were decent about it and did not look at me, and that was worse than if they had.

Bayliss pushed a paper across the bar. The front page was all Morocco and fleets and ultimatums. At the bottom there was a piece by a professor who said the comet was made of gas and would pass harmlessly.

"Harmlessly," said Hackett.

I went out and stood in the street. The comet was above the chimneys. It was not very bright. I looked at it a long time and felt nothing about it at all.

On Saturday I went to see her father. He was a small gray man who kept a shop and he did not ask me in. He stood in the door with his hand on the frame.

"She is gone to Kent," he said. "That is all I know."

"With him?"

"With him."

"Do you have the address?"

"I have not," he said. Then he looked at his boots. "I would not tell you if I had, William. You are a good lad. You would do something."

"I would not do anything."

"You would," he said. "Go home."

I went down to Hanley instead. There was a pawnshop on a side street with a tray of revolvers in the window. I had sixteen pounds in the Post Office, saved for the rooms. I took out twelve of it on Monday. The man wrapped the revolver in brown paper and did not ask me why. I put it in the inside pocket of my coat. It was heavier than I had thought it would be.

I did not tell myself what it was for. I had found that if you did not tell yourself, you could go on.

The train went south all day. At Rugby they put soldiers on. They were boys mostly, with kit bags, and they sang for a while and then stopped. At Euston the newsboys were shouting. I bought a paper and read that the Germans had mobilized or were about to. It was not clear which.

I crossed London on the underground and took another train into Kent. A man in my compartment, a clerk, watched the sky through the window.

"Look at that," he said.

The comet had grown. It was in the western sky, though the sun was not down yet. It had a green cast to it. The tail spread wide and thin like something spilled.

"They say the air will change," he said.

"Do they?"

"I don't believe it. But they say it."

"What do you believe?"

"I believe I'll go home to my wife," he said, and he laughed. It was not a good laugh. He got off at the next station and I watched him walk away fast along the platform without looking up.

I had the address by then. A girl at the Stuarts' cousin's had given it to me for a shilling. It was a cottage near a village called Lugwardine, then a lane, then a gate.

I walked the last four miles in the dusk. The lanes were high-hedged and smelled of dust and elder. No one passed me. A dog barked at a farm and stopped.

The cottage was white, with a pear tree against the wall. There was a light in the lower window. A motorcar stood in the lane, and the sight of it did something to me. I stood behind a hedge a long while.

Through the window I could see a table and a lamp. Then I saw Nettie. She had her hair down. She was laughing at something, with her head turned away, and a man's arm came into the lamplight and put a cup in front of her. I could not see his face. I did not need to.

I took out the revolver and unwrapped the paper and put the paper in my pocket. My hand was steady. That surprised me. I thought about the canal path and how she had said someday. I thought: now it is someday.

The sky had turned a strange color. The comet filled half of it. The light that came down was green and thin, like light through a bottle. The birds had stopped. There was no wind. The pear leaves hung without moving.

I stepped out from the hedge.

I smelled it before I knew what it was. It was sweet and sharp, like new rain on iron. It came across the field in a low green drift and lay along the lane. I breathed it in. I could not help it.

My knees went first. I thought: I am drunk. Then I thought: it is the gas. The professor was wrong. I held the revolver and tried to lift it toward the window, but my arm was heavy, and the window swam and went green and then went away.

I lay down in the lane. The dust was warm against my cheek. I thought I should be afraid. I was not. It was like the end of a long day at the kiln, when the work is finished and you sit down.

The last thing I saw was the sky, and it was very beautiful.

When I woke it was morning. A lark was going up somewhere. The air was clean and cold and sweet, so clean that I lay and breathed it for a while before I moved. The hedge was wet. The sky was pale blue with no green in it. The comet was gone.

The revolver lay in the grass beside my hand. I looked at it and could not think why I had brought it. It was only an ugly piece of iron. I sat up slowly.

I thought of Nettie, and of Verrall, and the knot that had been in my chest for two weeks was not there. I touched the place where it had been. I thought of the men in the Red Lion and the Germans and the soldiers on the train, and it all seemed far away and a little foolish, like a quarrel among children.

I got up. I left the revolver in the grass. Then I went to the gate of the cottage, and I did not know yet what I would say, but I knew that I would not be angry.
