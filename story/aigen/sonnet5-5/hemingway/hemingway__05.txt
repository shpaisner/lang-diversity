The comet came up in the east every night after the kilns had gone dark. It was small at first, a smear of light like a thumbprint on a window. By October it had a tail. The men at the works Leadford worked in looked at it on their way home and said nothing. Then they went into the public house and talked about Germany.

Leadford did not go into the public house. He had no money for it. He walked up Bursley Road with his hands in his pockets and the clay dust still in the creases of his knuckles. He was twenty-two. He had a room over a chandler's shop, a sister in service in Hanley, and Nettie.

He had Nettie the way a man has a coat he cannot afford to lose. He did not talk about it. In the evenings he stood outside her father's house, and she came out with her shawl over her head, and they walked past the canal where the water was the color of tea. She laughed at the things he said. He did not say many things.

"You're always so serious, Will," she said.

"I am serious."

"I know you are. It's why I like you." She looked up at the sky. "What's it for, do you think? The comet?"

"It's not for anything. It's a comet."

She laughed again. He liked it when she laughed. He did not know yet that a laugh could be given to a man and also to another man.

On a Thursday she was not at the door. Her father stood in the passage in his shirtsleeves. He was a thin man with a gray face, and he held a piece of paper folded small.

"She's gone," he said.

Leadford looked at him.

"She's gone with that Verrall. The Verrall from the big house at Clayton. A motor came. She took the brown bag." The old man held out the paper. "She left this. I can't read it. I don't want to."

Leadford took it. The handwriting was round and careful, the way she had learned in school. Dear Father. Don't be angry. I am going to be happy. I could not have been happy here. Tell Will I am sorry. N.

Tell Will I am sorry.

He read it twice. Then he folded it the way it had been folded and put it in his pocket. The old man was crying without any sound. Leadford did not know what to do with that, so he put his hand on the man's shoulder for a moment and took it away.

He went out into the street. It was dark and the comet was over the chimneys. It was larger than the night before. It had a greenish edge. He stood looking at it a long time, and he felt nothing at all, and he knew that the nothing would not last.

In the morning he did not go to the works. He went to a pawnshop in Burslem, where he sold his watch and his good boots, and he bought a revolver from the man behind the counter. The man did not ask him anything. It was an old Webley and heavy in the pocket. The man gave him six cartridges in a twist of paper.

"You want to be careful with it," the man said.

"I'll be careful."

"They always say that."

He took the train to London. The third-class carriage smelled of wet wool and tobacco. A man across from him had a newspaper and read parts of it aloud to nobody. Ultimatums. The Kaiser. Ships at Kiel. A man beside him said it would be over by Christmas, and the other said it would not, and a third said that it did not matter because the comet would finish them all before the Germans could.

"Gas," the third man said. "The papers say it's got gas in the tail. Cyanogen."

"What's that?"

"Poison."

"Then we'll all be dead and no need for a war."

They laughed. Leadford looked out the window. The fields went by gray and wet. He had his hand on the revolver through the cloth of his coat, and the metal was warm from his body. He thought of nothing but the street where Verrall had a house, which his sister had written once in a letter about the family, and the number of the door.

In London it was raining. Soldiers were in the streets, and crowds at the corners, and boys sold the late editions and shouted. Leadford walked a long way. He asked a policeman for the street. The policeman looked at his boots and told him, and he went on.

The house was shut. The windows had their blinds down. He rang and nobody came. A woman across the way, shaking a mat from a doorstep, said the family had gone to the country. The young gentleman was in France. Or Switzerland. She didn't know.

"And a girl with him?"

"There's always a girl with that sort," the woman said, and went in.

He sat on a step in the rain. His coat was wet through. He took out the revolver and looked at it and put it back. A newsboy ran past shouting that war was declared. The sound went down the street ahead of him like something thrown.

He enlisted two days later because there was nothing else, and because they gave a man bread and a place to sleep. A sergeant with a red face took his name. They gave him a rifle that was too long for him, and put him on a train, and then on a boat, and then he was in a field in Flanders where the mud was the color of the clay at home.

There were many men. They dug. It rained. At night the comet stood over the German lines, huge now, and the green of it lay on the wet faces of the men, so that they all looked drowned. Nobody talked about it. A corporal named Haines said it was like a bad egg, and a boy from Leeds began to cry quietly, and Haines told him to stop.

Leadford kept the revolver inside his shirt. He did not know why. He had not seen Verrall. He had not seen Nettie. He had a feeling that if he looked long enough at the line of the German trench he would see them there, which was foolish, and he knew it was foolish, and he looked anyway.

On the third night the guns stopped. It was very quiet. A wind came up from the east and smelled of something sweet, something like apples that had been kept too long. The green in the sky went white and then green again.

"What's that?" the boy from Leeds said.

Nobody answered. Haines stood up on the fire step with his rifle and then he sat down. He sat down slowly, as if he were very tired, and put his head against the earth wall.

Leadford felt it in his own chest. A lightness. He thought: This is it, then. He thought of Nettie's laugh. He thought of the canal. He tried to take the revolver out of his shirt and his hand would not close on it. The sky came down, green and gentle. Then there was nothing.

He woke and it was morning, and he was cold, and the sky was a color he had never seen. It was clean. It was blue and gold, and there was no smoke in it anywhere.

He lay for a long time and breathed. The air was good. It went down into him like water. He sat up. Haines was asleep beside him with his mouth open. The boy from Leeds was turning over and rubbing his eyes. Down the trench the men were stirring, one and then another, and none of them reached for a rifle.

Leadford stood up and looked over the parapet. In the field beyond, the Germans were standing too. They stood in the open, in their gray coats, with their hands empty. One of them was laughing. It was not a bad laugh.

He put his hand inside his shirt and found the revolver. He took it out and looked at it. It was an ugly thing. He could not remember clearly why he had wanted it. He remembered, but the wanting was gone, the way a fever goes, and left him weak and clear.

He set it down on the fire step.

He thought about Nettie and he waited for the old pain, and it came, but it was a different pain. It was smaller. He thought of her father at the door with the paper. He thought that she had been a girl who wanted to be happy, and that there was nothing wicked in it. He thought that Verrall was probably a man who had wanted the same.

"Well," Haines said, sitting up. He looked at his hands and then at Leadford. His face was open and bewildered, like a child's. "Well, I'll be damned."

"No," Leadford said. "I don't think so."

The sun came over the German line. He walked down the trench toward the men, and they made room for him, and somebody gave him bread.
