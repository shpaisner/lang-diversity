The letter was on the table when he came home. His mother had not opened it. She stood by the stove with her hands in her apron and watched him read it.

It was short. Nettie wrote that she was sorry. She wrote that she was going with Verrall and that it was better this way. She wrote that he would understand some day. He read it twice and put it down.

"Well?" his mother said.

"She's gone."

"I know she's gone. Her mother was here at noon."

He sat down. Outside it was raining on the slag heaps and the pot-banks and the long brick rows of the town. The kilns made the sky red at the bottom. It was always red there in the evening, and he had never minded it before.

"He has money," his mother said.

"I know he has money."

"You have a place at Rawlinson's. That is something."

"It's seven shillings less than nothing."

She put a plate in front of him. He did not eat. He looked at the plate and at the letter beside it. The handwriting was round and careful, the way she had written in school. He had seen it on a hundred notes. Meet me at the gate. Don't be cross. He was not cross now. He felt hollow, as if someone had taken something out of him and left the shape.

"Eat," his mother said.

He ate a little. The tea was strong and he drank all of it. Then he took his cap and went out into the rain.

Parload lived in a room above a chandler's shop with a telescope in the window. He was thin and pale and wore spectacles mended with wire. He worked all day at the gas-works office and all night at the sky.

"Come up," he said. "You look rotten."

Leadford climbed the stairs. The room smelled of paraffin and cold tobacco. There were charts on the walls and a long brass tube on a stand by the window.

"She went off with Verrall," Leadford said.

Parload said nothing for a while. He wiped his spectacles.

"I am sorry," he said. "I am truly sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Show me the thing."

"The comet?"

"Show me."

Parload bent to the eyepiece and turned a brass screw. The clouds had thinned and there were a few stars. He stood back and Leadford looked. At first there was only blackness. Then a small smear of light, pale and a little green, with a tail like a breath on glass.

"It's coming straight at us," Parload said. "Nobody wants to say so. The papers say it will pass wide. It will not pass wide."

"What happens?"

"I don't know. No one knows. There is a gas in the tail. Something new in the spectrum. A green line I can't name."

Leadford looked a long time. The light was small and very cold. He thought that it did not care about Nettie or Verrall or Staffordshire, and the thought was almost a comfort.

"Let it come," he said.

In the morning the men at Rawlinson's talked about the war. They talked while they worked and they talked at the dinner hour, standing in the yard with their bread and cheese.

"The Germans are mobilizing," said the foreman. He had the paper folded small in his fist. "It will be this month."

"It will be nothing," said an old potter named Hewitt. "They have said it every year."

"They have ships now. Look at the ships."

"I have looked at the ships."

Leadford ate his bread. It was dry and he chewed it a long time. He did not care about the Germans. He did not care about the ships. But he listened, because it was better than listening to himself.

"You'll go, Leadford," the foreman said. "You're young. They'll want you."

"They can have me."

"You're a hard one this morning."

"I'm not hard."

Hewitt looked at him. He was an old man and he had seen a good many young men go sour. "It's the girl," he said. "I heard."

Leadford folded the paper that had held his bread and put it in his pocket. "It's nothing," he said.

"It is never nothing," said Hewitt. "But it passes."

"Does it?"

"It did with me."

Leadford went back to the bench. The clay was wet and cold under his hands and he worked it until the whistle went.

He bought the revolver on Saturday from a man in Hanley who asked no questions. It was an old Webley with a worn grip. The man wrapped it in brown paper and took four shillings and a half, and that was the end of it.

He carried it home under his coat. He cleaned it that night at the kitchen table after his mother had gone to bed. He worked slowly. He had never owned a gun but he had watched a man clean one once. He oiled the cylinder and turned it and listened to the click.

He knew where Verrall's people lived. It was a big house on the ridge, with a gravel drive and a wall. He had walked past it with Nettie one Sunday and she had said, laughing, that she would like a house like that. He had said she would have one. He had said it like a fool.

Now he sat with the gun in front of him and the lamp turned low. He was not angry. That was what surprised him. The anger had burned out sometime in the week and left something dry and plain behind. He would go there. He would see them. After that he did not know.

He heard his mother turn over upstairs. The bed creaked. Then it was quiet.

He put the gun in his coat. He turned down the lamp and sat in the dark. Through the window the sky over the kilns was red, and above the red, very small, was the green smear. It was bigger than it had been.

He went on Monday, after work, on foot. The rain had stopped. The roads were thick with mud and the hedges dripped. He passed carts and a man with a barrow and two women who did not look at him.

Near Trentham he saw the soldiers. They were a small party, perhaps twenty, marching in column with their rifles slung. They looked tired. A young officer rode at the side and did not speak. The men did not speak either. Their boots made a sound on the road like a long tearing of cloth.

"Where are they going?" Leadford asked a boy at the gate of a farm.

"To the train. They say it's started."

"What's started?"

"The war. Over there. Somewhere."

The boy did not know more. Leadford went on. The air had a queer taste and the light was wrong. It was late afternoon but the sky had gone a thin yellow, and the shadows lay long and strange across the fields. A dog ran past him with its tail down. Birds sat along the wires and did not sing.

He walked faster. He felt the weight of the gun against his ribs.

At the top of the ridge he stopped. The whole country lay below him, the chimneys and the smoke and the canal shining like a knife. And the sky to the west had begun to turn green. Not the green of grass. A clean pale green, like the inside of a shell, spreading slowly upward from the horizon.

He stood and watched it. He thought of Parload bent over the brass tube. I don't know. No one knows.

The green came over the ridge while he was still standing there. It did not come like wind. It came like water rising in a glass, and the light went soft, and the hills were the color of old copper. He smelled something sharp and clean. He breathed it in before he thought not to.

He took out the revolver. He did not know why. He held it in his hand and looked at it, and it seemed a small and foolish thing, a toy of black iron. Down the road the soldiers had stopped. One of them sat down in the ditch. Another laughed, softly, and the laugh went on too long and then stopped.

"Verrall," Leadford said. He said it aloud. The name sounded thin.

His knees went loose. He tried to take a step and could not. The green was in his chest and in his head, and it was not unpleasant. It was like the first warm moment of a bath. He thought: so this is it. He thought of his mother by the stove. He thought of Nettie at the gate with her hair blown loose. He thought that he had loved her. That was all. It was a plain fact, like the road or the rain.

He sat down in the grass. The gun slid from his fingers and lay beside him. Above him the sky was green from edge to edge, and very quiet, and the stars were gone.

He lay back. The grass was cold and he did not mind it. Somewhere a bird began to sing, and then stopped, and then there was nothing. Then even the nothing went, gently, the way a lamp goes out when the oil is done.
