The kilns at Clayton burned all night and the smoke lay low over the town. Leadford came home from the works at six and washed in the yard. The water was cold and had soot in it. He dried his face and looked up. The comet was there above the chimneys, small and green-white, with a thin tail behind it like a scratch on glass.

"It's bigger," his mother said from the door.

"It's the same."

"It's bigger than last week."

He went in and ate. There was bread and a piece of cold bacon and tea. His mother watched him eat. She did not say Nettie's name. Nobody in the house had said it for nine days.

After he ate he went out again and walked down to the canal. He stood on the bridge. The water was black and did not move. He had known Nettie since they were children. They had walked out along this towpath in the spring, and she had held his arm and talked about a house with a garden. A small house. Two rooms up and two down. He had said yes, that could be done. He had saved eleven pounds.

Now she was gone with Verrall. Verrall had a motor car and a father with land. He had good gloves.

Leadford spat in the canal. The ring spread out and went flat. Above him the comet hung there and did not care.

The letter had come on a Tuesday. It was not a long letter. Nettie wrote that she was sorry, that she could not explain, that she was not made for the life he meant. She wrote that he was good. He read the word good three times and it meant nothing.

He went to her father's house. Old Stuart sat in the kitchen with his boots off.

"She's gone to London," Stuart said. "That's all I know."

"With him."

"With him."

"And you let her."

"She's twenty. What would I do?" The old man rubbed his feet. "You're a fool if you think I'm pleased."

Leadford stood in the doorway. The kitchen smelled of cabbage and wet wool. On the table lay a newspaper, and he could read the upside-down headline. It was about Germany again. It was about ships and a quarrel over some place he had never heard of. There was always a quarrel over some place. The paper said the Powers would not yield. The paper said the comet might be seen with the naked eye through the week.

"They'll have a war," Stuart said. "And then she'll be sorry."

"She won't be sorry."

"No," Stuart said. "Maybe not."

Leadford went out. It had begun to rain, a fine gray rain that smelled of the kilns. He walked home with his hands in his pockets and did not feel the rain.

They let him go from the works on Friday. The foreman said it was slack. It was not slack. It was that he had hit a man on Thursday, a man named Dawes who had made a joke about Nettie. He had hit him once, in the mouth, and Dawes had sat down in the clay.

He took his pay and went to the Red Lion. There was talk of the war. A man with a gray mustache said the Germans were ready and we were not. Another said the comet was a sign. A third said it was only gas, the paper said so, a lot of gas that would burn up in the air.

"Burn what up?" Leadford said.

"All of it, maybe."

"Good," he said.

He drank two whiskies. Then he went to a back street near the station where a man he knew sold things. He bought a revolver, a small black one, with six cartridges wrapped in a rag. It cost him three pounds. The man did not ask what for.

He carried it home inside his coat. It was heavier than he had thought it would be. In his room he sat on the bed and turned it over. He did not think about it. He thought only about the road to London, and about a face with a mustache and good gloves, and he put the revolver under his pillow and lay with his boots on.

He walked south. He did not take the train because he did not want to be seen on a train. He slept in a hayrick the first night and in a ditch the second. The comet grew. Now it was brighter than any star, and at night it threw a pale light across the fields. The tail had spread out like a fan.

On the third day he heard from a carter that the fleets had met. It was not certain. Nothing was certain. Men were drilling in the market towns, and in one village he saw boys marching with sticks. A woman at a farm door gave him bread and said her son had gone to the depot.

"Will it be bad?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"They say the sky's going to change."

"It always changes."

He ate the bread standing in the road. The air that evening was strange. It was very still. The birds had stopped. A dog lay flat in a yard with its nose on its paws. Leadford felt the weight of the revolver against his ribs and he walked faster. Over the hedge the sky had a green tint in the west, thin and clean, like the inside of a bottle.

He thought, I will be there tomorrow.

Then the light turned bright green, and the green was in his mouth, sweet and cold. He tried to get the revolver out. His hand would not do it. He sat down in the road, and the road came up soft and took him.

He woke in the grass. The sun was well up. He lay a while with his eyes open and did not move.

The air was different. He could not have said how. It was light and it was sweet and it went all the way down into him. His head was clear. He had no headache, and he had always had a headache, and now he understood that he had always had it. A lark was going up somewhere. He heard it and it was like hearing for the first time.

He sat up. The road was empty. A man lay across the hedge with his hat beside him, breathing. Beyond him a horse stood in a field and looked at Leadford calmly.

He put his hand in his coat. The revolver was there. He took it out and looked at it. It was an ugly thing. It was a dull small ugly thing made by men who were afraid. He did not understand how he had carried it.

The man in the hedge woke and sat up. He looked at Leadford. They looked at each other for a long time.

"It's all right," the man said.

"Yes."

"I don't know how I know."

"No," Leadford said. "I don't either."

The man smiled. Leadford smiled. He stood up, and his legs held him, and the whole country lay bright and washed under the sky.

He kept walking south, but he did not walk fast. All that day he passed people in the road and in the fields, and they spoke to him, and he spoke to them. A woman gave him milk. Two soldiers sat on a wall with their rifles on the ground between them. One of them was crying and did not seem ashamed. The other had his arm around him. They said the orders had come and nobody had obeyed them. They said it was over. Over was all they said.

He reached the place by evening. It was a cottage near a village in Kent, a low house with a pear tree. He had learned the address from Stuart, who had learned it from a postcard. He stopped at the gate. The revolver was in his pocket still. He had not thrown it away. He did not know why.

Nettie came out and stood in the doorway. She wore a gray dress. She was thinner. Behind her stood Verrall, bareheaded, with his hands hanging. Neither of them spoke.

Leadford took out the revolver. They watched him. He held it a moment in the light, and then he opened the gate, walked to the pear tree, and laid the gun in the grass at the roots.

"I came to kill you," he said to Verrall.

"I know," Verrall said.

"I can't remember why."

Nettie began to cry. Leadford went to her and took her hands. They were cold. He held them until they were warm. Nobody said anything for a long time. The sun went down red over the orchards, and the first stars came out, and the comet was gone, and the evening was very quiet.
