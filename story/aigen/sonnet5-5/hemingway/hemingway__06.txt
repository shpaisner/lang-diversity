The kilns had gone cold on Saturday and William Leadford walked home along the canal with his wages in his pocket. It was not much. It was enough for the rent and the coal and a little over. He had the little over in his hand and did not know what to do with it.

Nettie was gone. She had gone on the Tuesday train with Verrall, who had a good coat and clean hands and a father with a house on the hill. She had left a note on the table under the tea caddy. He had read it once. He had not needed to read it twice.

The Potteries lay under their smoke. The bottle kilns stood in rows like men waiting to be paid. Leadford stopped on the bridge and looked at the water, which was the color of weak tea and had a skin of oil on it.

Above the smoke, where the sky was clearing in the east, he could see the comet. It was small yet, and pale, like a thumbprint on a window. The papers said it was coming. The papers said many things.

He went on. His mother's house was dark. He did not light the lamp. He sat in the kitchen with his boots on and listened to the clock, and the clock did not say anything he wanted to hear.

Parload found him there on Sunday. Parload was thin and spectacled and worked at the gasworks, and in the evenings he went up on the roof of his mother's shed with a brass telescope he had built himself.

"You've not eaten," Parload said.

"No."

"Come up and look at it."

"I don't want to look at anything."

"It's a fine night. It's clear." Parload stood in the doorway with his telescope under his arm like a man carrying a gun. "It's got a green line in it. The spectroscope shows it. Cyanogen, they think."

"What's that to me?"

"I don't know," Parload said. "Only it's a thing. It's a big thing, and it's coming, and it's all there is to look at that's any good."

Leadford looked at him. Parload was a decent man and a fool. He was the only friend Leadford had.

"She went with Verrall," Leadford said.

"I know."

"Everyone knows."

"Yes."

They sat. Outside a dog barked and stopped. Parload did not say he was sorry, and Leadford was grateful for it.

"I'll look at your comet," Leadford said at last.

They climbed the shed. The comet hung over the chimneys with its tail spread behind it, and it did not care about either of them. Leadford looked a long time. Then he said it was very far away, and Parload said that it was not as far as it had been.

On Monday the talk in the Crown was of Germany. A man with a newspaper read aloud, and the others drank and listened with their caps pushed back. There was trouble over some port. There were ships. There were men in Berlin who said one thing and men in London who said another.

"They'll not fight," said the old potter by the fire. "They've too much to lose."

"They've nothing to lose," said the man with the paper. "That's why they'll fight."

Leadford drank his beer. It tasted of nothing. A young man near the door said he would go if they called him. He said it twice. Nobody answered him.

Afterward Leadford walked down to the pawnbroker's on Slater Street. He had passed it a hundred times. In the window were watches and rings and a concertina and, on a velvet cloth, two revolvers. He went in. The pawnbroker was a small man with a cough. He did not ask questions. He put the revolver on the counter and a box of cartridges beside it, and Leadford counted out the little over and then the rest of it.

"It's a good one," the pawnbroker said.

"Yes."

"You'll want to keep it dry."

"I'll keep it dry."

Outside it had begun to rain. The comet was gone behind the cloud, and he was glad. He put the revolver inside his coat against his ribs. It was heavy and cold. For the first time in six days he felt something that was almost like being well.

He took the morning train. It was crowded with men in dark clothes and the talk was all of the war, and he sat by the window and said nothing. The country went by wet and green. The fields did not know about anything.

At the junction he asked the way. A porter pointed up the hill. "Verrall's," the porter said. "You can't miss it. It's the big one with the trees."

He walked. The road went up between hedges and the rain came and went. Twice he took out the revolver and looked at it and put it back. A woman with a pram passed him and said good morning, and he said it back to her. His voice sounded strange.

He had thought about it in the night. He had thought he would go to the door and ask for her. He had thought he would see Verrall's face. After that he had not thought. There was a place where the thinking stopped, and beyond it there was only the sound of the shot, and he walked toward that place.

The light was wrong by afternoon. He noticed it first in the hedges. The leaves had a color in them that was not the color of leaves. He looked up. The cloud had broken and the sky was a pale and sickly green, as if something had been spilled into it. The comet was not a thumbprint now. It filled the east. The birds had stopped.

He stood in the road. A man came down the hill running, a gardener or a groom, with his mouth open, and he went past Leadford without seeing him. Far off, down in the valley, a church bell began and stopped.

"Well," Leadford said aloud.

He went on up. His eyes burned. The air had a taste in it, sharp and strange, like a struck match. He felt a heaviness come into his legs and thought, I will not stop. I will get there.

At the gate of the big house he put his hand inside his coat and held the revolver. The trees were very green, and the light was green, and the gravel path went up to the door. There was somebody lying on the lawn. A man, in a good coat. He lay on his side with one arm under his head, like a boy asleep.

Leadford walked toward him. He did not understand why his knees would not hold. He sat down on the grass, and then he lay down, and the green came over him softly like water. The last thing he saw was the sky. It was very quiet. He thought of Nettie, and he thought of the gun, and then he did not think of anything.

He woke in the morning and it was the first morning.

He knew it before he opened his eyes. The air was sweet. It was clean in his chest, like a drink of cold water after a long walk. He lay on the grass and the sun was on his face. There was no smoke in it. He sat up.

The lawn was wet and bright. The trees stood still. The man in the good coat was sitting up too, a little way off, and he was looking at his own hands as though he had never seen them. It was Verrall. His face was white and young and puzzled.

Leadford looked down. The revolver lay in the grass beside him. He looked at it for a long time. It was a dull ugly thing, a piece of iron somebody had made, and he could not remember what it was for.

"Leadford," Verrall said.

"Yes."

"I think I have been a fool."

"I think I have too," Leadford said.

They looked at each other. There was nothing in it that was like the hate he had carried up the road. It had gone out of him in the night as the fever goes. He felt weak and light and very hungry. He could see everything. He could see that Verrall was afraid and ashamed and was a man, and no more than that.

The door of the house stood open. Nettie came out on the step. She had her hair down. She stood in the sun with her hand on the post and she looked at the two of them on the grass, and she did not say anything. Her face was clear, and it was sad, and it was kind.

Leadford got up. His legs worked. He picked up the revolver by the barrel and walked to the edge of the lawn, where there was a pond with lilies on it, and he threw it far out. It made a small sound in the water and the rings went out.

"Is it over?" Nettie said. She meant more than one thing.

"I don't know," Leadford said. "I think it has just begun."

He stood in the sun and breathed. Somewhere down the hill a bell began to ring, and this time it did not stop. It rang clear over the valley. Then the birds started.
