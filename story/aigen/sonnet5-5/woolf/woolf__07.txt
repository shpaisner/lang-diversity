Leadford read the note standing, as though sitting down would have made it true. It lay on the oilcloth beside the teapot, in Nettie's round and careful hand, a hand that had been taught to make the loops of its letters generous and had no notion what to do with such a sentence. I am going away with Mr. Verrall. Do not look for me. Outside, the kilns of Staffordshire pressed their smoke against the dusk, and the bottle-shaped chimneys stood in their rows like the patient, stupid dead. He heard a cart go by. He heard the clock. It seemed to him monstrous that the clock should continue, that it should go on dividing the evening into its small equal pieces when the evening had so plainly been broken. He folded the paper once, and then once more, and put it in his waistcoat, against the ribs, where it lay all night like a second, colder heart.

Nettie, meanwhile, sat in a railway carriage that smelt of horsehair and rain, and watched the fields go backward. How quickly the world could be unfastened! She had thought it would be harder, that there would be some tearing sound. But there was only the rhythm of the wheels, and Verrall opposite, with his gloves, his good coat, his air of having been born already on the other side of everything. She loved him, she supposed. She loved the way he did not look at prices. And yet, at the window, a thin and doubtful thought rose in her, of William's hands, grey with clay, turning her wrist over once as if it were a thing too fine to hold. She put the thought away. Above the dark hedges, in the part of the sky not yet claimed by cloud, hung a faint smear of light, the comet, which the papers said was coming. "Is it near?" she asked. Verrall smiled, not looking. "Near enough to be interesting," he said, and she laughed, because he had laughed.

On the tram next morning Leadford sat wedged among men who smelt of work, and heard them talk. A paper passed from hand to hand. The Germans; the French; some quarrel over a country he could not have found upon a map, and ministers saying what ministers say. "It'll come," said a man with a ruined thumb, and said it with a kind of relish, as one speaks of weather that will at least be different. And the others nodded, and the tram swayed, and the grey streets went by with their wet slates and their chapels, their pawnbrokers, their small shut faces. Leadford did not care for Germany. He cared, with a fixed and narrowing intensity, for one room in a house he had never seen. Yet it seemed to him, in the swaying, that the two things were somehow the same: the great anger of nations and the private anger in his chest, both gathering, both drifting toward some edge, both pretending that nobody had decided. Through the tram's streaming window he glimpsed the comet, pale at noon, like a thumbprint on glass.

He bought the revolver from a man in a back room in Hanley, and the transaction was so small, so easily done, that he came out into the street feeling cheated of ceremony. It weighed in his pocket like a stone he had picked up on a beach. Then the long journey south, third class, with the thing against his thigh; the towns lighting their lamps one by one, indifferent; the nights growing curiously bright. For the comet had swelled. It now stood each evening above the roofs, a pale green wound in the sky, and people stopped in the roads to look, and said nothing, or said foolish things, and went indoors and did not sleep. Leadford did not look at it much. He looked at the address he had wrung from a cousin of Verrall's cook. He rehearsed what he would say, and each time the words dissolved, leaving only the dumb bright image of himself in a doorway, a figure from a ballad, and beneath it a slow, shameful suspicion that he was not wholly unhappy to be so tragic, that grief had made him, for the first time, important.

It happened as he climbed the lane. He would afterwards say that he did not see it happen but only found it had happened, as one finds that a sentence has ended. The air changed. A greenish, delicate light came over the hedges and the elms and the chalk of the road, and the sky, so long hostile, seemed to lean down and breathe. He smelt something, sweet and thin, unlike anything; he thought of apples, of the inside of a shell. Birds had stopped. A far gate swung once and was still. He put his hand to the revolver and felt his fingers loosen, not in surrender but in a strange forgetting, as the hand forgets a dream. The lane tilted gently. He thought, quite distinctly, How tired I am, and then, with astonishment, how tired everyone is, everyone, always, and the thought opened, wide as a field, and he lay down in it, and the green came over him like water over a stone.

He woke to dew. That was the first thing: the cold exact bead of it on his wrist, on the grass-blades, on the barrel of the revolver lying beside him, which looked now merely absurd, a black ugly toy someone had dropped. The sky was the palest and cleanest blue he had ever seen, scoured, as though the night had been taken away and washed. He sat up. His mind, he noticed, was extraordinarily quiet. The old furious engine in it had stopped, and in the silence he could hear everything: a blackbird, the tick of cooling earth, his own breath going in and out, a marvel. He thought of Nettie, and the thought did not wound. He saw her plainly, a girl of twenty, frightened and hungry for a little brightness, and he saw Verrall too, a young man as lost as himself in the vast clumsy muddle of wanting, and he felt toward them both a pity so large it was almost laughter. Nothing needed to be avenged. Nothing, he saw, had ever been separate. The men on the tram, the ministers, the armies, the kilns, the small shut faces of the streets: all of it had been a sleep, a stifled, quarrelsome sleep, and the green breath from the sky had simply lifted the lid. He stood, and the world, rinsed and shining, stood with him. Far down the lane a figure was walking toward him through the light, with her hands open, and he found, with no surprise at all, that he was walking too.
