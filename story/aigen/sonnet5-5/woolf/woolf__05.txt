The letter lay on the kitchen table where she had left it, propped against the teapot, and William Leadford, coming in from the works with the white dust of the potbank still in the creases of his knuckles, saw it before he had shut the door. He knew. Or rather, the knowing had been going on in him for weeks, like water under a floor, and now merely came up through the boards. He did not take off his cap. The clock ticked; the kettle, which she had filled and set to the back of the hob, murmured as though nothing had happened; and outside in the entry a child was bouncing a ball against the wall, one, two, three, with the terrible regularity of ordinary life.

Dear Willie, I cannot go on. That was all he read. Verrall's name was not in it, and did not need to be. He sat down. How strange that a man might sit in his own chair, in his own kitchen, in Staffordshire, among the smell of damp slate and boiled cabbage, and feel the whole structure of his days, so laboriously put up, brick by small brick, lean outward like a chimney in a gale. Nettie. He said it aloud, and the word seemed to belong already to someone else.

Afterward the evenings were the worst, when the smoke of the kilns lay flat across the town and turned the sky the colour of old pewter. He took to walking. He walked past the chapel and the Co-operative stores, past the lit windows of the public house where men stood at the bar with their pipes and their newspapers, and it was there, on the third night, that he heard them speaking of the comet.

It had been a speck, a rumour, a paragraph at the foot of a column; now it had a name in every mouth. "Seen it? Last night, plain as a lamp, over Hanley." The landlord's wife said it was a judgement. A young clerk, flushed and clever, said it was only gas, mere gas, tenuous as breath, and that the Earth would pass through its tail and no one the wiser. And at the other end of the bar, the same voices, with the same relish, passed from the comet to the Germans, to the Balkans, to the fleet, as if the heavens and the Foreign Office were two parts of one conversation. Leadford drank and heard none of it, and heard all of it. Somewhere beyond the hills the great engines were being wound, the telegraph wires were humming, and men in rooms he would never enter were pushing small coloured flags across maps. What was one girl in all that? And yet she was the whole of it; the rest was merely weather.

He remembered her at the canal bridge, years ago it seemed, though it was only two summers: her hat pushed back, a strand of hair across her mouth, laughing at something he had said and could not now recall. That was the cruelty of it, that the great moments left no words behind them, only the angle of light on a cheek. She had wanted, she told him once, to get out. He had thought she meant of the town, of the smoke. He had not understood that a person might mean out of a life, out of him.

And Verrall. He had seen him only once, from the pavement: a tall young man in a motor-car, leaning on the wheel with that peculiar slackness of those who have never had to hurry, the lamp-light sliding over the polished brass and the pale gloves. The car had gone by, and the gutter water had splashed his boots, and Nettie, who stood beside him then, had watched it go. He had noticed that. He had noticed it and said nothing, and silence, he now saw, had been his one contribution to the affair.

He bought the revolver on a Saturday from a man in Burslem who asked nothing, and carried it home wrapped in a copy of the Sentinel, which announced on its front page, in the large black type reserved for catastrophes, the nearness of the comet and the failure of some conference. The weapon was heavier than he had imagined. He laid it on the table, where the letter had lain, and looked at it as one looks at a stranger who has been given a bed for the night.

Then the train: third class, the carriage smelling of wet wool and oranges, the fields of the Midlands unrolling grey and green beyond the glass. A woman opposite knitted without pause. A boy pressed his nose to the window and said that the comet was coming, Mother, was it not, and the mother said hush. Leadford sat with his hand upon his pocket. He felt himself oddly divided, as if one William sat rigid in the corner seat, bent on his errand, while another drifted lightly above the roofs of the carriage and watched with a kind of pity. Past Rugby, past the long smudge of London gathering on the horizon, the afternoon thickened; and there was in the light a quality he could not name, a faint stain, as though someone had breathed upon the glass of the world.

The cottage was in Surrey, or he had been told so, a place of hedges and gates. He reached it at evening, on foot, having walked miles from the station along lanes that smelt of hawthorn and dust, and the sky, as he went, had begun to change. At first he took it for sunset. But the green was too clean for that, a green like the inside of a leaf held to the sun, spreading from the zenith in slow, silent tides, and the hedgerows, the faces of the few people standing in their doorways, the white road itself, took on a submarine calm. A cart-horse stood motionless in a field. A man at a gate said something to him, and he did not hear it. The comet had come; it lay along the heavens like a pale blade, and the air, drawn in, felt cool and strangely sweet, as if he had been breathing smoke all his life and had only now been offered something else.

He found the gate. He found the path. Light showed in a low window, and through it, as he stood among the wet lupins with the revolver cold and absurd in his fist, he saw a room: a lamp, a table, a woman's bent head. Nettie. She was sewing, he thought, or pretending to; and across from her sat Verrall, with a book open upon his knee that he was not reading, his face lifted toward the green, peaceful window as toward something he had long awaited.

Leadford raised the weapon. He meant to; he would swear afterward that he had meant to. But the sweetness was in his lungs now, rising behind his eyes, loosening the knot he had carried for weeks under his ribs. His arm grew heavy, then oddly far away. The hedge swam; the lamp-light widened into a single golden pool; and he thought, quite clearly, How tired they both look, and felt for them, for the first time, not hatred but a vast and helpless tenderness, as for children asleep. The revolver slid from his fingers into the lupins.

He sank down upon the garden path. Above him the green deepened, ceased to be colour and became a kind of knowledge, and the last thing he heard was the low, unhurried sound of the whole earth, from Staffordshire to the Rhine, letting its breath go.
