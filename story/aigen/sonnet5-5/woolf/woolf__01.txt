The letter lay on the mantelpiece where the clock should have been, propped against the tea-caddy, and for some moments William Leadford did not touch it. He stood in his boots and his wet coat, with the smell of the works still in his clothes, the clay and the coal and the sour breath of the kilns, and looked at the thing as one looks at a stranger who has come into the room and sat down. Outside, the bottle-ovens of Staffordshire stood in the dusk with their smoke going up and flattening against the low sky. A cart passed. A woman called a child in. The ordinary evening went on, quite unaware that it had been cancelled.

I am sorry, Willie. You would never have understood. That was all he read, or all he afterwards remembered, though there were more lines, crossed and recrossed. He understood the handwriting well enough. It was the round, hurried hand that had once written Thursday, by the canal bridge, and he had kept that scrap in his pocket-book for a year, and it was there now, against his ribs, absurdly warm.

He sat down in his wet coat. The fire had gone out, and he noticed that, and noticed too that the noticing was a way of not noticing something else, as a man may count the paving-stones while a surgeon is at work. Verrall, the letter said further down. Only the name. Verrall, who had gloves.

She had been so small, that was what came back, walking beside him in the lane behind the pot-banks in the evenings, her hand not quite in his arm but near it, as though waiting to be persuaded. She had talked of places. It was always places: Brighton, a house with a green door, a street where nobody's father had worked in clay. And he, who had only Burslem and the ledger at Rawlins's and eighteen shillings a week, had said, patiently, Some day, Nettie, and had heard the words go out into the air and wither like breath on glass.

Verrall had a motor-car. That was the simplest fact and the cruellest. He had stood once outside the chapel with his hat tilted and his easy, unhurried voice, and the car had shone behind him, and Nettie had looked at it, only looked, in the way one looks at a lit window from a dark road. Leadford had seen the look and had told himself that it meant nothing. Now every moment of the last months rose up and rearranged itself, each small silence showing its true face, so that he lived the whole summer again backwards, like a man reading a letter from the end.

At the corner of Hall Street the men were talking, as they had talked all that month, with the evening paper passed from hand to hand. Germany, one said, and another, the Austrians; and someone spat and said it would be over by Christmas, if it came at all. Leadford went by them. Their voices followed him like the noise of a distant river, a thing that has always been there and means nothing until the flood.

He looked up, because someone had pointed. Through a rent in the smoke, above the roofs, hung the comet. He had read of it and thought little of it, a smudge in the newspapers between the shipping news and the Balkans; but there it was now, a faint greenish stain with a tail drawn out behind it, as if the sky had been rubbed with a thumb. It was very quiet. It did not seem to belong to the crowded and quarrelling earth at all, nor to care that a clerk in Staffordshire had been left, nor that the great nations were leaning toward one another in the dark like drunken men. It merely hung there, enormous and patient, coming on.

They say it will touch us, said a boy.

Nothing touches us, said Leadford, and was astonished at his own bitterness.

He bought the revolver from a man in Hanley who asked no questions, in a back room that smelled of paraffin and old boots. It was a small, ugly, domestic object, and when he held it the thing felt less like a weapon than like a decision made by someone else long ago. He put it in his pocket, where it dragged the coat down on one side, and walked out into rain that had begun without his noticing.

All the way to the station he rehearsed it. The door would open; Verrall's face would change; he would say something, he had not yet found what, some sentence that would gather up the eighteen shillings and the canal bridge and the green door at Brighton and set them down at last in the light. But the words kept dissolving. Instead he saw only trivial things: the wet lamps swimming in the road, a dog nosing a kerb, a woman's shawl. How odd, he thought, that the mind in extremity should busy itself with shawls. And always, when the rain thinned, the green smear overhead, brighter now, like an eye slowly opening.

The train carried him south through a country that seemed to be holding its breath. At each station a few figures stood on the platforms looking upward, and the lamps burned with a queer unsteadiness, and in the carriage a man in the corner murmured to nobody that the animals had been restless; his dog had howled the whole night through. Leadford said nothing. He watched his own face in the black glass, pale and ordinary, a face one would never turn to look at, and behind it the fields sliding away, silvered faintly now by a light that did not come from the moon.

He thought of Nettie, and found that her face would not come. There was a hat, a movement of the wrist, the way she had of tilting her head to listen; but the whole of her escaped him, as a word escapes when one has said it too often. And this frightened him more than the rage had done. If he could not remember her, what was it that he proposed to avenge? Only a shape, a want, a long ache that had taken the name of love because it had no other.

The train slowed. Somewhere ahead the night was waiting, and the hour.

He came to the house a little after midnight, up a drive of pale gravel whose crunching seemed to him the loudest sound in England. The sky had changed. The comet no longer hung at the edge of things but filled a quarter of heaven, and its light lay over the lawns and the cedar and the long front of the house with an unearthly, sea-green clearness, in which every leaf stood separate and every window held a small cold flame. There were no shadows to speak of. It was like walking along the floor of some vast and tranquil water.

He stopped by the cedar. His hand was in his pocket, closed on the metal, and the metal was no longer cold. Somewhere within, behind one of those shining panes, Nettie was sleeping or lying awake; Verrall too; and the thought no longer burned him as it had, but passed through him strangely, as a draught passes through an open house. He was aware of his own breathing. He was aware of the silence, which was not the silence of an empty night but of an immense attention, as though the air itself had leaned closer to listen. A faint sweetness stirred in it, or he fancied so, a taste on the tongue that was neither smoke nor rain.

What am I doing here? he thought, quite simply, and could not answer; and yet his feet went on, over the grass now, toward the steps, while overhead the green light deepened, and deepened, and began, very softly, to descend.
