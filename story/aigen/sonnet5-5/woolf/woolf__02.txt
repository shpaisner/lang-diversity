He had stood at the window so long that the room behind him had gone dark without his noticing, as rooms do, the furniture withdrawing one piece at a time into the common dusk, until only the square of glass remained, and in it the kilns of Clayton breathing their orange breath against the sky. Nettie's note lay on the sill. He had read it so often that the words had worn smooth, like the step of a church, and meant nothing; I am sorry, Willie, I could not bear it, you must not follow; and what she could not bear, he thought, was presumably himself, the narrow stairs, the smell of clay and wet slate, the evenings of being poor together. Somewhere a door banged. Somewhere a woman called a child in from the street, and the child did not come. The bottle ovens went on smoking as they had smoked yesterday, as they would smoke when he was dead, and it seemed to him monstrous that a thing so large as his grief should make no difference to the chimneys.

Verrall. He tried the name over, and it was soft, well-tailored, a name that had never carried a bucket. He saw the hand on Nettie's elbow at the gate; the motor-car idling at the lane's end, brass catching the last light; Nettie's face turned up, laughing at something he had not heard. How easily, he thought, one is taken in by the ordinary afternoon! He had seen it all and understood nothing, as a man may watch a train go by and never guess that his whole life is aboard it.

She had been at her best on the hill above the town, that summer, the wind pulling her hair out of its pins. They had sat on the cropped turf with the whole smoking valley spread below them like something spilled, and she had said that one day she would go where the air was clean. He had taken it for a figure of speech. He had put his arm around her and thought: this is enough, this will always be enough. The sheep had moved along the slope with that slow tearing sound. A lark had gone up and up. And nothing, nothing in the whole bright afternoon had warned him that she was already, even then, leaning out of his arms toward some other life, as a plant leans on a sill.

In the morning the men at the works were talking of the comet. It had been in the papers for weeks, a small smudge on a photographic plate, and now it was a thing with a tail, a thing with a date; and the talk ran round the benches among the wet grey pots, rising and falling under the grinding of the wheels. Some said it would strike. Some said it would be a gas. Old Hinks said it was a judgment, and Mortimer, who read the Staffordshire Sentinel, said it was nothing of the kind, that the astronomers had calculated it to the minute, and that the real danger was not overhead but across the water, where the Germans were building ships. Leadford listened without hearing, his hands moving among the clay. Between the two fears, the one in the sky and the one over the sea, there seemed to him a curious likeness, as though the whole world were a house in which everyone sat listening for a step on the stair, yet went on laying the table and quarrelling over the salt. Ministers spoke. Armies were counted. And on the bench beside him a boy whistled, tunelessly, the same four notes, over and over, until Leadford could have struck him.

That evening he went into the pawnbroker's in Hanley, where the window was full of other people's lives: wedding rings, a flute, a child's christening mug, a row of watches all stopped at different hours. The man behind the counter did not look at him. He laid the revolver on the green baize as one lays down a loaf. It was heavier than Leadford had expected, and colder, and it seemed, in his palm, to be listening. He paid with money he had saved for a different purpose, for a ring, in fact, which was now, he supposed, somewhere on Nettie's hand or in a drawer. Outside the lamps were being lit one after another down the long street, each making its small yellow room in the dark, and above the roofs, faint but unmistakable, hung the thing itself, a pale and spreading stain, as if some great thumb had been pressed against the night.

He took the train on Thursday, third class, sitting opposite a woman with a basket of eggs who looked out at the fields and never once at him. The country slid by, wet and green and indifferent: hedges, a canal, a farmer standing at a gate with his hand over his eyes. At every station the newspapers were shouted, and the words ultimatum, mobilisation, the Comet ran together into a single word, a single dread. He thought: they will all be dead or changed by the end of the month, and what I do will not matter. And then, with an astonishing clarity, he thought: it matters to me. That was the whole of it; the grief, the revolver, the journey. It was the one small private fact that no comet could abolish, and he held it as one holds a coal, not because it was warm but because it was his. The woman got out at Rugby. Her eggs rode away on her arm, intact, absurd, precious. He watched them go.

It was evening again when he came up the lane toward the house where, he had learnt, they were staying: a villa among beeches, shuttered, with a lamp burning in one upper window. And the sky had changed. He stopped. It was not dark, though the sun had gone; a strange green light lay over everything, delicate as the inside of a shell, and it lay on the beeches and the gravel and the backs of his own hands, so that he seemed to be standing at the bottom of a clear sea. The comet had swollen into half the heavens. It no longer looked like a menace; it looked like a tide. And the silence! No bird, no wheel, no far-off shouting of newsboys; only a great suspended hush, as if the world had drawn a breath and forgotten to let it go.

He took out the revolver. It lay in his hand, quite small now, a mere black habit of the fingers. Through the lit window he could see a ceiling, a corner of a picture, a hand, perhaps hers, passing across the light. He raised the weapon and found that he was weeping, not from sorrow exactly, but from some enormous loosening, as if a cord he had worn about his chest for years had been quietly cut. The air had a taste. It was sweet, and thin, and it went down into him like a cool drink on a day of fever; and his anger, which he had carried all the way from Staffordshire, which he had fed and watched and warmed himself by, seemed to rise from him, to grow transparent, to be something he had once read about in a book. He tried to hold it. He said, aloud, "Nettie," and the word came out strange and without edge. The green deepened. The beeches swayed, though there was no wind. And Leadford, with the revolver sliding from his fingers into the soft grass, sat down slowly in the lane, like a child at the end of a very long day, and looked up, and let the sky come down.
