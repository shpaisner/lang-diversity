The rain had fallen on the Potteries since morning, on the bottle-kilns and the slag-heaps and the long wet roofs of the terraces, and it seemed to William Leadford, standing at the window with Nettie's note crushed in his hand, that it had been falling all his life. I am sorry, Will. I could not go on as we were. Do not look for me. That was all, and yet he read it again, as if the words, held nearer the failing light, might rearrange themselves into some other and more bearable sentence. Behind him the kettle began to sing. A cart ground down the street. Somewhere below, his landlady was scolding a child, and the voice rose and fell through the floor with an appalling ordinariness.

Verrall. He said the name aloud, and the room received it without comment. He saw him as he had seen him once, on the steps of the Rawlinsons' house: the good coat, the unhurried hand, the way of looking at a girl as though she were something lightly admired in a shop. And Nettie looking back. Had she ever looked at him so? He thought of the evenings on the canal path, her arm in his, her talk of the places she would see; and he understood, with a slow cold clarity, that she had only ever been talking, and he had only ever been the ground on which she stood to talk.

He sat on the bed. The note slipped to the floor. Outside the chimneys breathed their yellow breath into the dusk, and the dusk took it, and said nothing.

In the evenings the men gathered at the corner beneath the lamp, and the talk was of the comet. It was in the papers; it was in the pulpit; it was, said old Hickson, a thing of no consequence, a smudge, a trick of the astronomers who must be paid for something. Yet each man, going home, glanced up into the thinning cloud. And there, on clear nights, it hung: a small pale blur at first, and then, as the weeks went on, a thing with a faint green edge, like a thumbprint of cold fire on the dark.

Mingled with this, in the same breath, came the other talk: Germany, the Balkans, the ships, the Kaiser's moustaches and what the French would not endure. Leadford, passing, heard it as one hears the sea from a bedroom, a vast indifferent murmuring in which his own grief was a pebble. Men spoke of war with an odd relish, he thought, as if it were weather that might at last break the drought of their days. Boots in the street; flags in the newsagent's; a drum from the Territorials' hall on Thursday nights.

And what was a man's one small ruined love against that? He tried to hold the thought, to be reasonable, to be the kind of man who read the leader columns. But the thought slid from him. All that stayed was the picture of Nettie in a railway carriage, laughing, with her hat tilted, and the green light on her face.

He bought the revolver from a man in Hanley who asked no questions, and it lay in his pocket like a second, heavier heart. He did not decide to use it. That was the strange thing; he decided nothing. He merely found himself, the next morning, on the platform with a ticket, and the train drawing in, and his own hand on the carriage door as though it belonged to someone resolute.

All the way south the country unrolled behind the glass: fields, hedges, a church tower standing up out of its elms, a woman pegging out linen in a garden that was gone before he had fully seen it. How little any of it knew of him. A clergyman opposite slept with his mouth open. A child ate an orange. And Leadford sat rigid, rehearsing what he would say, and the words kept dissolving into the rhythm of the wheels, Verrall, Verrall, Verrall, until the name was only a sound, a clatter, a nothing.

He had learned from a cousin where they had gone, a cottage by the sea, rented for the season. He imagined the door, the lamp, the two faces turning. He imagined the noise it would make. Then, with a shiver, he imagined afterward, and could imagine nothing, a blank white wall.

At a junction the train waited. Along the platform the lamps were being lit, though it was not yet evening, for the light had taken on a curious quality, a greenish thinness, and the porters stood looking up.

By the time he reached the coast the sky had changed. It was not night, yet not any day he knew. Over the sea hung the comet, enormous now, no longer a smudge but a great tilted veil of luminous green, and beneath it the water lay quite still, the colour of a bottle held to the sun. People had come out on the road. They stood in doorways and on the shingle, their faces tinted like the faces of the drowned, and none of them spoke, and the gulls had stopped their crying.

Leadford walked on. His boots rang strangely on the road. He passed a man who laughed softly to himself, and a woman kneeling with her hands over her eyes, and a soldier on a bicycle who had let the bicycle fall. Far off, perhaps, a gun was sounding, or it was thunder, or it was only the pulse in his own ears. The thing in his pocket knocked against his thigh with each step.

He thought: it is the end, then. And he was astonished to find that the thought gave him no terror, only a kind of loosening, as when a hand that has clenched all night at last begins to open. The cottage stood ahead, a white shape against the green. A lamp burned in the window. He drew out the revolver and looked at it, small and black and foolish, and the air that came to him then had a taste he had never tasted, sweet and cool and faintly metallic, and he breathed it in.

Afterwards he could not say whether he had reached the door. He remembered the garden gate, the wet scent of stocks, and a sensation of the ground tilting very gently, as a boat tilts when someone steps aboard. He remembered thinking that he ought to be frightened, and that the idea was amusing. Then the green came down over everything like water closing, and the lamp, and the cottage, and the long grievance of his own name, all went out together.

There was no dream. There was only a vast and level darkness, in which something, not he, not anyone, seemed to be turning over, as a sleeper turns, and settling toward morning. Across the whole of the world, in the fields of France and the streets of Berlin, in the pit-heads and the pantries, in the barracks and the ballrooms, the same quiet hand was laid upon every forehead. Men who had loaded rifles lay beside them with open palms. The drum in the Territorials' hall hung silent. In the Potteries the kilns went cold, and old Hickson slept with his chin on his chest and his scepticism gently lifted from him like a hat.

And the thing in the sky, which had been so long feared, bent down and breathed upon the earth, and the breath was not death, but something stranger, a clearing, a rinsing, the lifting of a lid.

He woke to light. It lay along the grass in long gold bars, and it was morning, an ordinary morning, and yet it was not ordinary at all; for the air was so clean that every blade seemed to have been newly made, and the sky above was a pure and tender blue in which no green remained. A bird was singing. He lay and listened to it with a feeling he could not at first name, until he saw that it was simply happiness, undeserved, entire.

He sat up. The revolver lay in the dew beside him, and he looked at it for a long time with a kind of wondering pity, as one looks at the toy of a child long dead. How had he carried it? How had the hot small knot of himself, that had seemed to fill the whole world, ever been so tightly drawn? He saw Nettie then, not as the lost, tilted face in the railway carriage, but as a girl, only a girl, wanting what everyone wanted, to be larger than her life; and he saw Verrall too, vain and frightened, and not his enemy but a fellow creature stumbling toward the same light. The hatred had not been conquered. It had simply gone, like a fever, leaving the body weak and clear.

The cottage door opened. Someone stood there, blinking, one hand raised against the brightness, and Leadford rose and went toward them across the shining grass, with his hands empty, and the whole new world waking about him, one soft breath at a time.
