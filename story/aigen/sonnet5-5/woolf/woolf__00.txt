The smoke had lain over Clayton all that week, and through it, when the furnaces were banked and the lamps lit along the Hanley road, the comet showed as a small smudge, a thumbprint on the dark window of the sky. Leadford saw it from the yard of the pot-bank as he stood with his coat collar turned up, not looking at it exactly, but aware of it as one is aware of a clock in a room where someone has died. Nettie had gone. That was the fact, and it had the hardness of a fact; he turned it over and over, and it presented the same edge. Gone on Tuesday, by the afternoon train, with a bag. Her mother had said so in the doorway with her arms folded, a woman who had decided in advance what her face should do.

The kilns stood about him like enormous bottles, and the rows of chimneys went on, and the little lit windows of the terraces, in each of which someone was frying something, or quarrelling, or lifting a child. He felt the whole district press upon him, its sootiness, its habit. What was one man's grief to it? It had swallowed generations of grief and gone on turning clay into cups.

"Evening, Will," said someone, passing.

"Evening," he said, and the word came out of him like a coin from a machine.

Above, the thumbprint widened by some imperceptible fraction, and was not interested in him at all.

He remembered the lane, because one always remembers the lane. It had been June, and the hawthorn gone over, and Nettie before him with her hat in her hand, saying that the Verralls' gardens ran down to the very wall and that you could see the lawn through a crack in the bricks. She had pressed her eye to it. He had looked at the back of her neck, at the fine hairs curling there, and had thought: I shall never be able to give her a lawn.

That was the whole of it, perhaps. Not that she was false, for she had not been false, she had been eager, which is different; her mind had gone through the crack in the wall long before the rest of her. And then the young man himself, Verrall, in his motor, slowing at the gate, lifting his cap with that ease which cost him nothing because he had never been without it. How quickly one's own hands became visible! Leadford had looked at his, the ink on the forefinger, the cracked thumb-nail, as at tools belonging to another man, an inferior workman.

Now the lane was merely a lane, and he walked it at dusk with the mud taking his boots, and the hedges dripping, and every gap in them a wall with a crack in it. He put his eye to none. He knew what was there. A lawn, perfectly green, going on without him.

At the works they talked of Germany. The foreman held the newspaper at arm's length, and the men leaned in as though the print were warm. Ultimatums; a fleet; a Balkan telegram; the word mobilisation, which sounded to Leadford like something done to furniture. They said it would come before winter. They said it with a kind of relish, since it would be something at least, a change in the long flat grey of the weeks.

"And the comet," said a boy. "They say it'll strike us."

"They say," said the foreman, and spat, and the matter was dismissed.

But Leadford, tracing a pattern on the drawing-board, a border of leaves for a dinner service that someone in London would eat from, found his line wavering. Europe drifted. That was the sensation: not a march, not a decision, but a drifting, like barges loosed on a canal, nudging, grinding softly together in the dark, while the men who should have held the ropes stood on the bank and looked at their watches. And he too drifted. On Friday the manager called him in and said, not unkindly, that his work had fallen off, that there were reductions, that times were what they were. Leadford stood on the carpet. He heard everything, and felt it all happen to a figure some way off, a figure in a bad coat. Then the street, and the sky tinged oddly at its edges, a faint greenness, like water in which something has been rinsed.

His mother's kitchen smelt of blacklead and of bread. She sat with her hands in her lap, which was unlike her, and he saw that she knew, that all the street knew; there is no privacy in such places, only a decent pretence of it. She said, "You'll want your tea." He said he would not. The kettle sang. The clock beat out its small economical seconds, and he thought: I am twenty-three, and everything I have is in this room.

He went upstairs and sat on the bed. Rage came to him slowly, not as fire but as a sort of clearness, as when a fog lifts from a field and every fence-post is distinct. He saw Verrall's cap, lifted. He saw the lawn. He saw Nettie's neck. And it seemed to him that there was one action, simple and round as a plate, which would make all these things hold still.

On Saturday he bought the revolver in a shop in Hanley, from a man who wrapped it in brown paper as he might have wrapped a kipper, and asked nothing. The weight of it against his ribs was extraordinary. It was the first thing in weeks that had weight. In the train going south the carriage was full of men in dull uniforms, and women crying with their handkerchiefs balled, and above the chimney-pots of every town the comet burned larger, a pale green eye that had stopped pretending to be a star.

Beyond Birmingham the country opened, and he walked, having no money for the rest. The roads were strange. Carts went by loaded with bedding; a regiment lay in a field with its boots off, and a boy among them was playing a mouth-organ, one thin tune, over and over, which seemed to Leadford the saddest sound he had ever heard because it asked nothing. People stood at gates looking up. Nobody was working. There was a holiday feeling and also a feeling of the hour before a funeral, when the blinds are down and the cake is cut.

He slept under a hayrick and woke with the sky the colour of an old bruise, and the comet, enormous now, trailing across a third of the heavens like a veil drawn by some invisible hand. The grass was grey with dew and the dew was faintly green. He ate a crust. He felt very clearly that he had no future, and the knowledge, curiously, lightened him. A man with no future walks fast.

By evening he had come to the village below the Verralls' park. There was the wall. There was the long line of limes, and a window high up already lit, yellow, domestic, and behind it someone moving. He stood in the road with his hand inside his coat upon the cold butt. A dog barked, and stopped. Nothing else. The whole air had begun to hold itself, as a held breath, and the leaves did not stir.

Then the light changed. It did not come from the sky exactly, but from everywhere, from the stones of the road, the faces of the cottages, the backs of his own hands; a green so delicate that at first he thought it was the pressure of tears. The limes turned the colour of the inside of a wave. Far off, the soldiers had begun to shout, and then, one by one, the shouting ceased, as lamps go out down a long street.

He drew the revolver. He held it up, and it seemed a curious thing, a small black toy of oiled metal, belonging to a child of some previous century. He tried to fix his mind upon Verrall's cap, upon the lawn, but they would not come; they floated off like leaves on a stream, and what came instead was the lane in June, the hawthorn, and Nettie's neck, seen not with desire now but with a vast and level tenderness, as one might look upon someone sleeping in a train. He breathed. The air was sweet, strange, tasting of nothing he knew, and it went down into him and loosened something that had been knotted since childhood.

"Oh," he said aloud, "it was never—"

But the sentence would not finish, and he did not need it to. His knees were softening. He laid the revolver in the road, gently, as one lays down a tool at the end of the day. Above him the green deepened, the stars went out behind it, and the whole country, the barges, the kilns, the field of boots, the lit window, sank together into a silence that was not death but its exact opposite, the silence of a great thing about to be begun again. He lay down on the grass. Somewhere a bird, mistaking the hour, began to sing.
