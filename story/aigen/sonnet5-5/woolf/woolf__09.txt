The smoke lay over the Five Towns that evening as it lay every evening, a long brown bandage drawn across the sky, and William Leadford, coming up from the pot-bank with the clay still grey in the creases of his knuckles, thought that nothing in the street had altered, though everything had. The same women stood at the same doors. The same lamp-lighter, a stooped figure poking at the dusk, moved from post to post. And yet Nettie had gone. She had gone on Tuesday, with a bag, with her good hat, with Verrall, who had a motor-car and a father with money and a way of tilting his head, as if the whole of Staffordshire were an amusing remark made for his benefit. It was now Thursday. Between Tuesday and Thursday a gulf had opened, and William walked along its edge, looking down.

He remembered her at the canal, in the summer, laughing, her hair coming loose; he remembered the precise sound of that laugh, which seemed now to have belonged to someone else. He had been going to say something. All through that summer he had been going to say it, and each Sunday it had grown larger and more impossible, a stone he carried in his mouth, until at last somebody else had spoken, easily, in another language altogether, the language of motor-cars and London.

His mother had the kettle on. She did not look at him. That was her kindness, and he hated it.

Over the tea-table lay the newspaper, and in the newspaper, side by side, like two guests who had not been introduced, were the Comet and the Germans. The Comet was said to be growing; Professor So-and-so of Greenwich had measured it, and it had a tail, a thin pale feather of light that was visible now before dawn if one stood in the yard and looked east. The Germans, meanwhile, were building ships, and the Foreign Secretary had risen to say something that meant nothing in a great many words, and in Paris and in Berlin men in buttoned coats were bending over maps.

William read it all and none of it. The words slid off him as rain slides off slate. Yet he felt them gathering, somewhere beyond the kitchen, beyond the smoking chimneys: the great indifferent preparations of the world, the armies shaking themselves like dogs, the star that came on without hurry. What was he beside it? A man of twenty-two with a bad wage and a worse grief. And still the grief was the only thing that had edges. The rest was fog.

"They say it'll be the end of us," said his mother, to the teapot.

"They say a lot," said William.

But he looked at her bent grey head, at the cup trembling a little in her hand, and for a moment the whole of life seemed a thing precariously held, a cup carried across a room, and he was ashamed of the stone in his mouth.

On Saturday he walked out to the edge of the town, where the slag-heaps ended and the fields began, to find Parload. Parload worked in a lawyer's office by day and by night kept a small telescope on a table in his bedroom window, and he spoke of the Comet as other men spoke of a woman, with a sort of ruined tenderness. He was thin, freckled, shabbily luminous.

"Look," Parload said, and William put his eye to the brass and saw nothing, then a smear, then a kind of faint, quivering greenness hanging in the dark like breath upon glass.

"It's cold," said Parload. "Cyanogen, they think. Poison, if you like. But it's so far, Leadford. So far that one's own troubles—" He stopped, seeing William's face. "I heard about Nettie. I'm sorry."

"Everyone's sorry."

"Yes. It's the least we can be."

They stood together at the window. Somewhere below a dog barked, and a train drew its long lamplit sentence across the valley and was lost. William thought: she is under that same sky, somewhere, and she is not thinking of me; she is not thinking at all, perhaps, but only being happy, which is a kind of sleep. And the thought, which should have made him weep, made him instead very quiet and very hard, like a thing that had been put in the kiln.

He bought the revolver on Monday from a man in Hanley who asked no questions, and who seemed, curiously, to be thinking of something else. It lay in his pocket like a second, colder heart. He drew out his savings; he told his mother he had work in Birmingham; she said nothing, and her saying nothing filled the house to the rafters.

The train to London was crowded with people talking of the war. A commercial traveller read aloud from a paper; a woman pressed a child against her coat; a soldier slept with his mouth open, young, absurdly young. The fields went past, ploughed and wet, and above them, as the afternoon thinned, the sky took on a strange tint, not the colour of weather, but something finer, a sort of pallor stretched over the world. People bent to the windows. Nobody spoke.

William held the revolver through the cloth. He rehearsed what he would do. He would find them; she would turn; he would see in her face the whole sum of his loss, and then, then, there would be an end. The scene played itself again and again, always the same, a lantern-slide in a darkening room. And yet each time the faces were a little less distinct, as if the glass were clouding, and it came to him that he did not know what Verrall's face looked like when frightened; that he had never, in fact, truly looked at Nettie, only at what he wanted of her.

The train ran on into the dusk, and the dusk was green.

By the time he reached the lodging in Kensington whose address he had wrung from a cousin, the whole city had gone strange. The street-lamps burned with a sickly, doubled light; people stood in the road with their heads thrown back; a policeman had taken off his helmet and held it against his chest like a man at a funeral. And above the chimneys, vast now, immensely nearer than any star had a right to be, hung the Comet, trailing across half the heavens a veil of emerald fire.

He did not notice that he was walking quickly, nor that guns had begun, somewhere to the east, a muffled coughing along the horizon: the war, then, had come, on the very night when the sky itself seemed to lean down and look. He found the door. He climbed the stair. His hand was on the revolver and his heart was knocking, and from beyond the door he heard, quite clearly, a woman's voice, not laughing, but speaking low and troubled, a voice he knew as one knows the grain of a table.

He raised his arm. And the air in the stairwell, which had been thick and yellow with gas-light, began to alter. A freshness moved through it, a strange sweet lightness, as of a window opened in a long-shut room; he drew breath, and the breath went down into him like water into dry earth.

How long he lay on the stair he never knew. When he opened his eyes the light was pouring through the landing window, gold and entirely clear, and the revolver lay beside his hand, a small black ugly object, as foreign to him as a tool from another trade. He looked at it for a long time. He could not remember what it was for. Or rather he remembered, but the remembering had no teeth; the rage had gone out of him as the fog goes out of a valley, leaving every hedge and roof-line astonishingly distinct, and what remained was a clarity so tender that it was almost pain.

He thought of Nettie, and thought: she was frightened, too. He thought of Verrall, and found only a young man, vain and uncertain, who had wanted what everyone wanted, to be held against the dark. It seemed to him that he had never understood anything before this morning; that he had gone about the world with a cloth over his eyes, and the cloth had been lifted by some great, kind, impersonal hand.

He rose. Through the window the city lay washed and shining; the sparrows were shouting in the plane-trees; and in the street, men and women who had been, last night, on the edge of killing one another were standing about in the sun, laughing, weeping, touching each other's sleeves as though to make quite sure. The guns had stopped. Across the roofs the sky was a pure unclouded blue, and in it, faint and already fading, the last thread of green.

He put the revolver into the grate, gently, as one lays down a sleeping child. Then he went down the stairs, and out into the morning, where everything was beginning.
