William Leadford woke that morning, as he had woken for eleven mornings, to the sensation that something had been taken out of the room during the night. The bed was the same, and so was the jug on the washstand with its chipped lip. The window, where the grey light came in over the roofs of Clayton, still showed the bottle-kilns standing up out of the smoke like the stumps of something felled. But Nettie was gone, and the loss had spread itself through the furniture until even the chair seemed to be holding its breath.

He dressed. He went down. The woman of the house said something about the weather, and he said something back, and both of them understood that nothing had been said. Outside, the Potteries had begun their day of dust and clay and endless small repetitions: the clogs on the cobbles, the gates, the hooters that sounded across the valley one after another. He walked among them as a man walks under water. Above the chimneys, if he looked, and he tried not to look, hung the pale smudge they had been talking about for weeks, the comet, which in daylight was nothing, a rumour, a thumbprint on the glass of the sky.

He had loved her, he supposed, from the afternoon by the canal, when she had stooped to tie her boot and the light had come through the willows and lain on her neck. That was all it had been: a neck, a boot, the green water going slowly by with a straw turning on it. Yet he had carried the moment about with him for two years, and it had grown until it held the whole of his future, the little house, the table, the evenings. He had not asked himself whether she had seen the same house.

And then Verrall, with his motor-car and his easy, careless voice, a young man to whom doors seemed to open before he reached them. Leadford saw him again now, leaning on the gate of her father's yard, laughing, with one glove off. What had Nettie seen? Not a man, perhaps, but a window, a way out of Clayton, out of the clay. He could not even hate her for it, and that was the worst. He could only hate the glove, the car, the voice, and these he hated with a pure and bitter steadiness that frightened him, as if another person were living in his body and only now had put down a bag and taken off a coat.

At the works the men stood about the yard at the dinner hour with the newspapers passed from hand to hand, and the papers said Germany, and said Morocco, and said ultimatum, words as distant as the comet and as likely, it seemed, to arrive. Old Bayley read aloud, his finger following the line. Somebody said it would be over by Christmas. Somebody said it would be a good thing, it would clear the air, and a boy laughed, not knowing why.

Leadford listened and did not listen. It seemed to him that all Europe was moving in the same slow, sleepwalking drift as himself, that the great Powers were only so many Leadfords with their bags packed in the dark, hating a glove, hating a voice, and that something would be done presently which no one had precisely decided. The kilns breathed. The sun, a coin behind the smoke, went over. And in the evening, when the sky cleared a little above the hills, there was the comet itself, and it was no longer white. It had a greenish cast, faint, like the inside of a shell.

He bought the revolver in Hanley from a man who asked no questions, and who wrapped it in a sheet of the Staffordshire Sentinel, so that the war lay folded round the thing. He carried it home against his ribs. All the way he was aware of it as one is aware of a second heartbeat, slower, colder, and strangely consoling. He did not think of what it was for. He thought only of the train, of the road beyond the station, of the house in Kent where he had heard they were hiding, and of the door that would open.

In the carriage a woman opposite him slept with her mouth a little open, and a clergyman turned the pages of a magazine, and fields went by, wet and quiet, with cattle standing in them like things set down and forgotten. How ordinary it was! That was what he could not forgive. That people should read, and sleep, and the hedges go by, while his life lay in pieces. At one station a company of soldiers filled the platform, young men with their caps pushed back, singing, and the sound reached him thin through the glass. Then the train drew out, and the singing went on without them, going nowhere, like a thread left hanging.

By dusk he was walking, and the lane ran uphill between banks of wet leaves. A cart had passed him; a dog had barked from a farm; then nothing. The air had an odd quality, he noticed, a stillness that was not mere evening, as though the world had paused in its sentence. He stopped to find his breath. The revolver was warm now from his body.

Westward the sky had deepened to a strange translucent green, and in it the comet hung, enormous, no longer a smudge but a presence, its tail drawn across a third of heaven. The hedges had taken the colour. His hands, when he held them up, looked like the hands of a drowned man. And he thought, with an extraordinary and irrelevant clarity, of the straw turning on the canal, of Nettie stooping, of Bayley's finger moving along the line; and it seemed to him that all these were one thing, held together by a thread he could almost feel, if only the hatred in him would loosen its grip upon it. But it did not loosen. He went on up the hill, and the light came after him, green and without a shadow.

The house stood at the top, white, with a lamp lit in an upper window, and he saw the gate, and the path, and the gravel pale as bone. He had meant to run. Instead he walked, slowly, and as he walked he felt the air thicken and grow sweet, with a taste on the tongue like nothing he had known, like cold water and apples and the smell after rain. He drew the revolver out and it seemed a small, foolish, absurd thing, a toy of black metal in a hand that shook.

Then the green came down. It did not fall; it simply was, in the lane, in the trees, in the lamp, in his own lungs, so that every breath filled him with a kind of terrible lightness. The hedges seemed to lean and listen. Far away over the dark fields a sound began, which might have been the guns of the frontier, or birds, or laughter, and which resolved itself, as he listened, into no sound at all but a vast and tender silence. He tried to hold his anger and found it like a bright coin slipping through his fingers; he tried to say her name and found his mouth full of something that was almost joy. The revolver dropped. He heard it strike the gravel as if from a great distance.

He sank down upon the path with his face against the cool stones, and above him the lamp in the window burned on, and above the lamp the long green fire of the comet; and it seemed to him, as consciousness lessened and widened together, that he was not falling but being gently put down, as a child is put down, by hands that had always, through all the smoke and the clay and the years, been quietly waiting. The last thing he knew was the scent of the earth, and a great and unaccountable pity for everyone.
