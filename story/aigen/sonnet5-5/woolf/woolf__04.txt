The letter lay on the kitchen table where she had left it, propped against the teapot, and William Leadford, standing over it in his working boots with the clay of the pot-bank still grey in the creases of his knuckles, read it a second time though he had understood it the first. I cannot bear it, Willie. I cannot bear any more of this. This, he supposed, was the street, the smoke, the rows of blackened brick that climbed the hill towards Hanley and gave no sight of anything but more brick; this was the mother's cough through the wall, the thin gas, himself. And Verrall was the other thing, the thing that was not this. He had seen him once, in a motor-car, a man with a fair, easy, careless face, who had looked at the street as one looks at weather.

He sat down. The clock ticked with its usual stupid industry. Somewhere a door banged, a child cried and was hushed, and the whole evening went on exactly as if nothing had been taken from it. That was what he could not forgive: that the kettle should still be warm, that the light should still lie yellow along the oilcloth. He had loved her, he thought, and the word sounded in his mind like a coin dropped in an empty room, rolling and rolling and refusing to lie flat.

Afterwards he went out, because the walls had begun to lean, and climbed the hill to where the chimneys stopped and the sky began. And there it was, as it had been for weeks, a faint smudge low in the north-east, which the newspapers called the Comet and which the men at the works called a good many other things.

It had grown. Each night it seemed to him a little nearer, a pale thumbprint on the dark, with a faint stir about it like breath on glass. In the Potteries they argued about it over their pints with the same relish they gave to the price of coal; it would strike the earth, said one; it would pass and leave nothing, said another; it was a sign, said old Hickson, who had once heard a preacher at Burslem. The papers, meanwhile, had other matters. Germany had done this; France had said that; there were ships, and a crisis, and a word, ultimatum, which men said over to themselves as if it were a spell. Everyone seemed to be waiting, and none of them for the same thing.

William stood among the cold stones with his hands in his pockets. Over the whole of Europe, he thought, men were leaning over tables like his, reading letters they could not bear; it was all one vast apprehension, nations shifting in their sleep. But it did not console him. The great things were too large, and his grief was exactly his own size, and fitted him closely, like a coat.

He did not decide, afterward, on any particular hour to buy the revolver. It came to him as a thing already done, as one finds oneself on a stair. A man in Hanley sold it from under a counter, wrapped in a rag, and asked nothing, and William carried it home against his ribs, where it lay cold and then warm, and then no longer felt like anything but a part of him.

He told his mother he was going to look for work in the south. She said nothing, rocking, and her silence followed him into the passage like a hand on his sleeve. All through the night train he sat in the corner of a third-class carriage and looked at his own face laid faintly over the black fields, and beyond it, wherever the glass was clear, the Comet, larger now, with a greenish pallor. Men slept around him with their mouths open. A soldier, going to join his regiment, had a kitbag between his knees, and twice William caught him staring, not at anything, only staring, as if he too carried something wrapped in a rag.

Verrall, William thought, Verrall, and tried to make the name into a face and a doorway, a lighted window with a woman in it. The revolver knocked against him at every jolt of the train. He would say nothing, he thought; there would be no speech. There would be Nettie turning, and the sound, and then the long quiet which the sound would make.

He reached the place by walking. It lay beyond a village, behind a wall, a pale house with many windows, and a park where the trees stood about in the dusk like people who had been told bad news. He climbed the wall at the dip where the stones had fallen. The grass was wet. Far off on the road a trumpet sounded, thin and ridiculous, and then the tramp of feet; the regiments were moving, then, it had begun or was about to begin, and the whole country turned its face towards the east as the beech-leaves turn towards the wind.

And above the house, the sky. The Comet had swallowed half of it. It was no longer a smudge; it was a vast pale green shining, as of the inside of a shell or of deep water with a light behind, and the trees, the roof, his own raised hand were all stained with it. He had the odd thought that the earth had been lifted out of its place and was passing, quite quietly, into another room. The windows of the house were lit. In one of them a shadow moved, and he began to run.

He had not gone twenty yards before the air changed. It was not that it grew thick; rather it grew sweet, and strange, so that he drew it into his lungs and felt it go through him like a cold, clear note struck on a glass. The revolver was in his hand. He looked at it with great attention. He thought, with perfect lucidity, this is a curious object, and his knees, without any fault of his, folded; the grass came up softly; the green light poured over him and over the park and the lighted windows and the marching men on the road, over the ministers at their tables and the ships in their harbours and his mother rocking in the dark kitchen. Everything was being put out, gently, as one by one the lamps are put out in a house where all have gone to bed.

He woke, and it was morning, and he did not know for a moment who he was.

Then he knew, and was astonished that it did not hurt. He lay on his back in the wet grass and the sky above him was a clear and tender blue, such a blue as he had not known the sky to hold, and every blade near his face was separate and distinct and shone. The air tasted new. Somewhere a blackbird was singing, and the song seemed to him to be going on not in the tree but in himself. He sat up. The revolver lay beside him, small and dull and rather pitiful; he looked at it as one looks at a child's toy found in a drawer, and felt toward it a faint tenderness for the man who had needed it, that other William, grey with clay, with his hard grief. How narrow that man's world had been, how like a street. He laid it down in the grass and left it.

Along the road men were getting up, too. He saw them from the wall: soldiers sitting on the verge with their rifles beside them, looking at one another and laughing, absurdly, like boys let out of school; one of them was weeping and did not seem ashamed. Nobody was going to fight. It was impossible, it was inconceivable now, and they knew it as they knew their own hands.

And in the doorway of the pale house, a woman stood with her hair down, looking at the morning; and beside her a fair man, bareheaded, who took her hand. William saw them clearly. He felt no rage, only a great clear pity and a great clear joy together, and he lifted his own hand, and after a moment, across the shining grass, Nettie lifted hers.
