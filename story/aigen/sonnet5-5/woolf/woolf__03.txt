The evening smoke of the Potteries lay along the valley as it always lay, heavy, yellow, indifferent, and above it, in the one clear space the chimneys left, hung the comet. William Leadford saw it as he came out of the office of Rawlinson's, where he had sat since eight copying figures into a ledger, and he did not for a moment think of it as a thing apart from himself. It was merely one more fact in a day of facts: that Nettie had gone, that her father had stood in the doorway with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead saying nothing, that the kettle on the hob had gone on singing as if nothing had happened.

He walked. The streets of Clayton ran downhill between their blackened brick, and at each lit window there was a life proceeding, a woman bending, a child lifted, a man's shadow thrown enormous on a blind. He envied them with a kind of fury. Her note had said only, I am going with Verrall. Don't follow. I am sorry, Will. Sorry! The word lay in him like a stone in a shoe, and with every step it turned and pressed upon the same tender place.

Above the chimneys the pale smudge hung, with its faint tail brushing the dark, quite silent, quite unhurried.

Once, he remembered, they had walked up by the canal on a Sunday, and she had stopped to pull a bit of willow-herb from the bank and twisted it in her fingers, talking, talking, of a house with a bay window, of a hat she had seen in Hanley. He had not listened to the words. He had listened to the sound of her, quick and bright, going on like water over stones, and thought: this is what I am to have. The light had come low across the slag-heaps and made them almost beautiful. She had laughed at something, and the laugh had gone up into the air and been lost, and he had not known that it was already being lost.

And Verrall. He had seen Verrall only twice: once standing at the gate of the Stantons' with a stick under his arm, once from the pavement as a motorcar passed, a long grey car, and in it a face untroubled as a plate, a face which had never in its life been obliged to calculate. His hands, Leadford recalled, were clean. It was absurd to think of the hands. Yet he thought of them, of how they would lie upon the white cloth of some table in some town where there were no chimneys, and of Nettie's hand near them, and his mind recoiled and returned, recoiled and returned, like a tongue to a broken tooth.

On the Monday the newspapers were full of the Balkans and of Germany. At the works the men stood about in knots at dinner-time, and a word went round, Morocco, the fleet, the Kaiser, passing from mouth to mouth as a coin is passed, worn thin by handling. Nobody quite believed it. Nobody quite disbelieved it. It was a weather in the future, a pressure falling somewhere beyond the hills.

Parload, the little pale draughtsman who had a telescope in his attic, caught Leadford by the sleeve at the gate. His eyes were large and shining. "Have you seen it tonight? It's growing. It's growing, Leadford. They say there's a new line in the spectrum, a green line, something that's never been classified." He spoke of cyanogen, of the tail sweeping toward us, and Leadford, looking at his collar, thought how strange that this man should be happy, should burn so with a mere smear of light.

"It'll not touch us," he said.

"Oh, it will touch us," said Parload. "That's the beauty of it."

All that week the thing grew. At night the people of the district came out on their doorsteps and looked up, a murmur of faces turned the same way, as if some enormous question had been put and everyone were waiting, courteously, for the answer. Children were held up to see. Old women said it was a sign. The publicans said it was good for trade. And Leadford, passing through them with his hands in his pockets, thought: There is a sign, there is a war, and she is somewhere laughing. The three things could not be made to fit, and he did not try. Grief has its own astronomy and puts itself at the centre.

The second letter came on the Thursday, forwarded by her father, who held it out between finger and thumb as if it were hot. It was from a place in the south, a village whose name Leadford had to look for on the back of a railway timetable. She wrote more fully this time. The house was small, the garden went down to a stream. Verrall was kind. She was, she thought, happy; at least she was not unhappy; and would Will please, please not think too hardly of her, because she could not bear it if... The sentence broke off, and began again about the weather.

He read it standing in the passage, and then he read it in his room, and then he sat for a long time with it across his knee while the light went out of the window. The green of the sky came in, a strange pale green, like the underside of a leaf. He had never noticed before how much of a life is spent in rooms. When he rose he found that he had decided, though he could not have said at what moment the deciding had been done; it seemed rather that he had been decided, as a stone is decided by the slope it lies on.

On Saturday he took his savings from the tin and went into Hanley, to a shop with a shuttered front where a man asked no questions. The revolver was heavier than he had expected. It sat in his pocket against his thigh, the one solid fact among so many shadows, and he walked with it through the lamplit market, past the stalls of oranges and crockery and cheap print, and felt for the first time in a week quite calm.

The train south was crowded with men going to their regiments. He sat wedged by the window while they sang, and one of them, a fair boy with a reddened neck, offered him a cigarette and said, "It'll be over by Christmas, mate," and Leadford said, "What will?" and the boy laughed as though that were a very good joke.

Fields went by, and hedges, and the great blank backs of towns. At every junction the posters spoke of crisis. Yet through the dusty glass the country lay absurdly peaceful, a woman at a gate, a horse standing in a pond to its knees, and over it all the sky gathered itself, slowly, toward some change. The light had a quality he could not name. It seemed to be thinning, to be drawn out like a thread. Even the soldiers fell quiet toward evening and looked up through the windows, and the singing guttered and went out.

He thought of Nettie in the garden by the stream. He thought of Verrall's hands. He thought, quite distinctly, I shall go in at the gate, and found that beyond the gate his mind showed him nothing, a blank, a white wall, like the end of a corridor.

It was night when he left the train at the little station, and he walked the last miles by a road he did not know. No one was abroad. The hedges stood black on either side, and the dust underfoot was pale, and the whole sky was lit, not by the moon, which had not risen, but by the comet itself, which now spread across a third of the heavens, immense, and tender, and utterly without malice. Its light fell upon his hands. It fell upon the road, and the road seemed to float. The stars were faint behind it, and over the eastern hills a long fringe of green was lifting, delicate as breath on glass.

He stopped. He did not know why. A silence had come over the country, so complete that he could hear the blood in his ears and, far off, the small continuous noise of water, the stream, perhaps, the one in her letter. The air had changed. It was sweeter, or he was lighter; he could not tell which. Something was passing over the earth, as a hand passes over a sleeping face, and the hedges, the road, the very weight in his pocket, seemed to lean toward it, to wait.

"Nettie," he said aloud, and the name sounded strange, as if spoken by someone else, someone long ago.

He drew a breath, and the breath went down deep into him, cool and green, and all around him the night opened.
