The Potteries lay under their own black weather the night William Leadford learned that Nettie was gone. The bottle-kilns stood along the hills like old men with their hats on, breathing out the smoke of a hundred years. In the narrow street the gas lamps flickered, and the cobbles shone with a rain that tasted of coal.

He held the note in his hand. It was a small thing, one sheet, and it weighed as much as a church.

I am sorry, Will. I cannot go on being poor. I have gone with Mr. Verrall.

He read it until the words came loose from the paper and drifted about the kitchen like moths. Then he went out into the yard and looked up, because there was nowhere else to look.

There, above the chimneys, hung the comet.

It had been a pinprick in the autumn, a smudge, a rumor. Now it was a pale green thumbprint pressed upon the dark, and it seemed to Leadford that it was looking at him. It was the only thing in Staffordshire that was not tired.

"Well," he said to it. "You've come for something, and so have I."

The comet said nothing. It burned on, cold and patient, as if it had all the time in the world and he had none.

Memory is a kiln, and it fired him through the night.

He remembered Nettie at the gate in June, with the hawthorn behind her and her hair full of light. He remembered her laugh, which had once seemed to him the sound of a spoon touching a china cup. He remembered promising her a house, a garden, a life that smelled of bread. Then he remembered his wages, twelve shillings and a clerk's contempt.

Verrall had a motorcar. Verrall had a voice like warm oil. Verrall's hands had never held anything heavier than a glove.

In the morning Leadford went to work because the body goes where it has always gone. In the yard the men stood about with newspapers and talked in low iron voices.

"They'll fight by spring," said one. "The Germans, the French, the lot."

"Let them."

"It's the comet," said another. "Stirs the blood. Mark me. Blood and stars, they're cousins."

Leadford said nothing. In the bright cold noon the comet was still faintly there, a ghost of green. It seemed to him that all of Europe was a house with the gas left on, and everyone in it was striking matches for the pleasure of the light.

He worked. He hated. The two feelings turned together in him like wheels, and the wheels ground a fine white dust that settled on his heart.

On the third night he bought a revolver from a man in Hanley who asked no questions and wanted no names. It was small and ugly and it fit his hand as though it had been waiting there for years.

He took the late train south. The carriage rattled through a country gone strange. At every station stood soldiers, boys with new boots and old faces, loading crates under the lamps. A newsboy ran along the platform shouting of ultimatums. Somewhere beyond the rim of the world, great engines were turning toward one another.

Above it all the comet grew.

It filled half the window now, a vast green plume combed across the stars. The fields lay under it like drowned meadows. The faces of the sleeping passengers turned the color of old coins. A woman across from him woke, looked out, and crossed herself without a word.

Leadford pressed his forehead to the glass. He thought: It is so large. And I am so small. And still I will do this.

That was the terrible thing, the thing he would later recall with wonder. Even beneath that titanic lamp, he had not once thought to put his anger down. A man can carry his small fire through the end of the world and never feel the weight of it.

The train sighed. The night went on.

The Verrall house stood on a hill above a village whose name he never learned. Its windows were lit, every one, as if the house had been waiting for him.

He climbed the wet lawn with the revolver heavy in his coat. The air was strange. It had a taste in it, faint and sweet, like a struck match, like the first apple of autumn, like a word almost remembered. The birds had stopped. The wind had stopped. The whole earth held its breath.

Through the long window he saw them.

Nettie sat at a piano she could not play, in a dress he had not bought her. Verrall stood behind her, laughing at nothing, a glass in his hand. They looked like two figures on a clock, turning out their small hour.

Leadford raised the revolver. His arm shook. In the garden the shadows had gone green, and the green was moving, coming down out of the sky in slow veils, like silk lowered over a cage.

"Nettie," he whispered.

She could not have heard. Yet she turned her head, as though a hand had touched her shoulder, and looked straight at the dark where he stood. For a moment her face was only a girl's face, frightened and lovely and lost.

Then the sky opened.

It did not come as fire. He had always expected fire.

It came as a hush. The green swept over the hill like a tide over sand. It poured through the hedges and down the chimneys and into the open mouths of the sleeping villages. Leadford felt it enter him, cool and sweet, as water enters a dry well. The revolver grew heavy, then impossibly heavy. His fingers opened. It fell without a sound into the grass.

So this is the end, he thought, without fear, almost with courtesy.

The stars wheeled slowly. Through the window he saw Verrall's glass slip and never shatter, because the glass was already hanging in the quiet air, and Nettie's hands folded themselves into her lap like two white birds going to roost. The whole house sank into sleep, and the hill, and the county, and the shuddering continent beyond, with all its trains and crates and ultimatums. Every gun in Europe lay down in the dark.

Leadford sank last. He knelt in the wet grass and watched the green light fall like snow upon the world. He thought of his mother's kitchen, the kettle on the hob, the smell of bread. His anger rose from him like a bird, circled once, and vanished.

"Oh," he said, softly, wondering. "Oh."

Then the night closed over him, gentle as a hand over a candle.

He woke to birdsong.

It was the first thing, and it was so loud and sweet that he thought for a moment he had died and been given back his childhood. He lay in the grass with the sun on his face. The sky above him was a blue so clean that it hurt, as if somebody had washed it in the night and hung it out to dry.

He sat up. The air was new. It tasted of apples and rain and distance. Every blade of grass shone, every leaf, every drop of dew a small, perfect lens holding the whole morning.

And he understood.

He did not understand it as a man understands a sum, in pieces. It came whole, like a window thrown open. The hatred, the wages, the twelve shillings, the little gray lives, the great gray wars: all of it had been a sickness, a fever of the blood, and the fever had broken. The green breath of the comet had gone into the world's lungs and burned out the poison. He saw it plainly. Men had not been wicked. They had only been asleep and dreaming, and now they were awake.

The revolver lay in the grass like something dropped by a clumsy child. He looked at it with tender amazement. How strange, that he had wanted this.

Across the lawn the long window stood open. Nettie came out upon the terrace, her hair loose, her eyes wide, one hand held out as if to feel whether the day was real. Behind her came Verrall, pale and blinking and young. He looked like a boy who had been crying in his sleep.

They saw Leadford. For a long moment no one spoke. The sun climbed. Somewhere a church bell began to ring, not in warning but in joy, and then another answered it, and another, until the whole bright valley rang like a struck glass.

Leadford stood. The tears ran down his face and he let them.

"Nettie," he said. "Oh, Nettie. I'm so sorry. I'm so sorry for all of it."

And she was weeping too, and Verrall, and the three of them stood in the golden morning while the world began again around them, soft and clean and strange.
