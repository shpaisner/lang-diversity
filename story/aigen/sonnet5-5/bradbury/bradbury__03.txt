The night Nettie left, the kilns of Staffordshire were burning as they always burned, like red hearts buried under a hill of soot. William Leadford stood in the doorway of the rented room and read her note three times. The words did not change. They never do. Paper is a patient liar, and a patient truth-teller too.

I am going with Mr. Verrall. Do not follow. I am sorry, Will.

He folded it small and put it in his waistcoat, near the place where something had been torn. Outside, the Potteries lay under their usual quilt of smoke, chimneys standing in rows like black candles on a cake nobody would eat. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Somewhere a train went by with its windows full of yellow, other people's lives sliding past, bright as lanterns, going elsewhere.

He had been poor all his life. He had thought poverty was a coat you wore, and that love was the fire you stood beside to dry it. Now he understood that the fire had walked off with a man who owned good gloves.

He did not weep. He sat on the bed until the lamp guttered. Then, because there was nothing else to look at, he looked up through the grimy window, and saw the thing that had been growing in the sky all month.

The comet hung above the chimneys, small and green and patient, like a seed waiting for rain.

By September the whole world was looking up, and the whole world was also looking sideways.

The newspapers sold themselves on street corners, shouted from the throats of boys with cracked boots. COMET NEARER. GERMAN FLEET AT KIEL. ASTRONOMERS DISAGREE. Men in the pubs of Burslem argued about both, over beer gone flat with argument. Some said the comet would strike. Some said it would pass. A pale clerk swore it was a message. A drunk swore it was a bill.

And all across Europe, like dry grass before a match, the nations leaned against one another. Leadford felt it in the factory, in the way the foreman's voice went tight. He felt it in the long rumor of railways, soldiers and horses and crates moving through the dark toward borders no one could see. The continent was a great clock wound too far, and everyone could hear the spring.

At night the comet was bigger. It threw a thin green light across the slag heaps and turned the canal to a ribbon of old bottle glass. Children stood in doorways, pointing. Their mothers stood behind them, not pointing.

Leadford looked at the sky and felt nothing grand. He was too small for grandness. He was a man with a torn place in his chest, and the torn place had begun to fill with something dark and cold and exact.

He thought: If the world is going to end, let me finish one small thing first.

The revolver cost him eleven shillings and a lie. The pawnbroker did not look at him as he wrapped it, and Leadford was grateful. A man can carry almost anything if no one looks at him while he does it.

He learned where they had gone from a woman who had once sold Nettie ribbons. Verrall had taken a house out past the heath, one of those trim, pleased villas where money goes to look at the countryside. Leadford went by foot, because he had no money left for trains, and because walking made the thing in his pocket seem less like an idea and more like a destination.

The road climbed through fields the color of ash. The hedges were full of small noises, wrens, mice, the secret commerce of the dusk. Above him the comet burned larger than a moon, though far stranger, wearing a veil of green fire that seemed to breathe.

He passed a church where the bell tolled for no reason. He passed a soldier asleep in a ditch with his boots off, one white foot pointed at heaven. He passed a man kneeling in the road, not praying, only kneeling, hands open, as though waiting for the sky to put something in them.

Leadford went on, and the gun knocked softly against his thigh, tap, tap, tap, like a small hand asking to be let in.

He found the house at the hour when the light goes thin as paper. Lamps shone in the windows. A gramophone played somewhere inside, a waltz, sweet and tinny, the sound of a life that had never once been cold.

He stood among the dripping laurels with the revolver in his hand and saw them through the glass. Nettie, in a dress he had never bought her. Verrall, tall, easy, pouring wine as if pouring were a gift he gave to the world. She laughed. Leadford had not heard that laugh in months, and it went through him like a nail through a plank.

Now, he thought. Now, before the sky comes down.

But the sky was already coming down.

It came quietly. The green light thickened and swam, and the air itself seemed to ripen, like fruit. A strange sweetness rode the wind, the smell of rain on a hot stone, of a cellar opened after years. The laurels shivered. A bird dropped out of a tree, not dead, only asleep, wings folded like a closed book.

Inside the house, the waltz slowed, and slowed, and ended on a long, falling note.

Leadford raised the pistol. His arm felt strange, as though it belonged to someone distant, someone kind. He tried to curse. He tried to remember the shape of his hatred. It slid through his fingers like water.

The last thing he saw was the whole sky turned to green glass, and the stars behind it, shining like the lit windows of some far and gentle house.

He woke with grass against his cheek.

For a long while he did not move. He lay and listened, and what he heard was nothing he had ever heard before: a vast, shining quiet, not the quiet of absence but of fullness, like the hush in a church before the first hymn. A thrush was singing. Somewhere a stream was talking to its stones.

He sat up. His head was clear as a washed window. The air tasted new, as though it had just been made and set out for him.

The sky was a pale and perfect blue. The comet was gone. In its place stood a morning so clean that it hurt to look at, every leaf edged in light, every blade of grass holding a small lit world of dew.

The revolver lay beside him in the wet grass. It looked absurd now, a little black toy, a child's mistake. He stared at it, and tried to find in himself the cold exact thing that had carried him over the heath. It was gone. Where it had been there was only a great, aching tenderness, like a bone knitting.

He thought of Nettie. He thought of Verrall. He thought of the soldier with his white foot in the ditch, the kneeling man, the boys with their newspapers, all of them sleeping under the green. He understood, with the plain certainty of a man who has been given a gift too large to doubt, that the war would not come. The clock had been stopped, and no one would wind it again.

He stood. His knees shook. The door of the villa stood open, and in it, blinking in the sunlight like a woman newly born, stood Nettie.

They looked at each other across the dew-bright lawn. Neither spoke. There was no need. The world had been washed, and they were only two small people standing in it, astonished to be alive.

Leadford left the revolver in the grass, and walked toward her with his empty hands open.
