I.

In the Potteries the kilns burned all night, like a row of red mouths that had forgotten the words they meant to say. William Leadford walked beneath them with his hands in his pockets and his supper uneaten in his stomach, and the smoke came down to meet him, as it always did, soft and black and tasting of old coins.

Above the smoke, where the sky should have been honest, the comet hung.

It had been a pale thumbprint in October. Now it was a green eye, an enormous patient eye, and the chimney-tops pointed at it like fingers at a stranger. Men stood in the doorways of the public houses with their glasses held forgotten, looking up. Women hushed their children and looked up too. It did not move that anyone could see. It only grew, a little each night, as a thought grows in a man who has decided not to think it.

Leadford did not look up for long. He had his own sky to be sick under.

Nettie was gone.

He said it to himself in the rhythm of his boots. Nettie is gone. Nettie is gone. Past the bottle-ovens, past the canal lying black and still as a spilled ink, past the little brick house where she had once leaned from a window with her hair undone, laughing at something he had said. He could not now remember what he had said. That was the cruelty of it. He could remember the window, the lamplight, the wet slates shining. He could not remember his own good words.

Behind him the kilns breathed. Ahead, the green eye waited.

II.

She had left a note, but notes are only little coffins for the things people cannot say aloud. He kept it folded in his waistcoat, and it had grown soft as cloth.

I cannot go on as we are, Will. I want to live. Do not look for me.

Live! As though he had been keeping her in a drawer.

And Verrall. Verrall of the long white hands and the clean collars, Verrall whose father owned land enough to bury a county. Verrall had come to the Potteries one summer in a motorcar, an astonishing thing the color of a ripe plum, and Nettie had stood in the street with the dust settling about her like gold, and Leadford, seeing her face, had known the way a man knows the weather in his bones. He had known, and had said nothing, and had gone back to his bench and his clay.

Now the evening papers lay in every gutter, shouting in black type. Germany. The Fleets. The Ultimatum. Europe was a great dry field in August, and every nation held a lit match, politely, behind its back. Men in the taverns spoke of war as they might speak of a holiday. Leadford listened and felt a dark, low kinship with it. Let it come, he thought. Let all the old iron sing. Let somebody else's house burn for a change.

He sat on the edge of his narrow bed that night, with the window open and the green light lying on the floor like water. In the light, his hands looked drowned.

Then he stood and went to the drawer where he kept the revolver.

III.

The train south was a long brass animal dragging itself through the dark. Leadford sat in a third-class compartment with a sleeping sailor and an old woman who clutched a birdcage covered in black cloth, and he watched England pour past the window: farms like sleeping cattle, villages with a single lit window each, like a thought still awake in a sleeping head.

The revolver lay against his ribs, warm as a second heart.

He did not think of what he would do. A man never does, in such hours. He thought of Nettie's wrist, the blue vein in it, the small bone like a pebble under the skin. He thought of Verrall's smile. He rehearsed neither speech nor murder. He rehearsed only the moment of arriving, as a child rehearses Christmas.

At a junction in the small hours, the train stood panting under a lamp. Porters moved along the platform like gray moths. Somewhere a telegraph clicked and clicked, spelling out the end of the old century's patience. The old woman lifted the cloth from her birdcage, and the canary inside, fooled by the green glow, began to sing, sweetly, wildly, as though it were morning.

"It thinks the sun's come," she whispered.

Leadford looked out and up. The comet filled half the window now. It was no longer an eye but a lantern held by some vast hand, and the whole sleeping country lay in its light like a table set for a feast no one had been told about.

The canary sang. The sailor stirred and did not wake.

"Perhaps," said the old woman, "it has."

IV.

He left the train at a place whose name he never afterward remembered, and walked.

The road ran white between hedges. The air had changed. It was thick and strange and sweet, with a taste on it like green apples, like the inside of a struck match, like rain that has not yet fallen. Birds sat silent in the hawthorn. A dog lay in the road with its muzzle on its paws, not sleeping, only attending, and it did not lift its head as Leadford went by.

In a village he passed a crowd gathered about a pump. Men in their Sunday black and women with shawls over their heads were staring upward, and not one spoke. A boy stood on a wall holding a tin trumpet he had meant to blow for the war. He did not blow it. The trumpet hung from his hand and shone green.

"They say it's the end," said a man, as Leadford passed. He said it gently, almost with relief, as if announcing that a long-expected guest had finally knocked.

Leadford walked on.

Somewhere ahead was the house with Verrall's name on its gate. He knew it as the tongue knows the tooth that aches. And the nearer he came, the more the world seemed to lean toward some great, hushed event, the way a room leans toward a violinist who has lifted the bow and not yet drawn it. Even his hatred seemed thinner now, stretched like a membrane over something enormous.

He took the revolver out and carried it openly in his hand.

The light grew. The hedges seemed made of emerald glass.

V.

He saw them on the lawn.

The house stood white and many-windowed at the end of a drive of tall limes, and in the green dusk Nettie and Verrall stood together on the grass, small as figures in a clock. She wore a pale dress. Her face was turned up to the sky, and Verrall's arm was about her, and neither of them looked like a thief or a seducer or anything he had made of them. They looked like two children who had been brought out of bed to see the fireworks.

Leadford stopped at the gate. He raised the revolver. His arm was steady. It surprised him how steady.

"Nettie," he said, and it was not a shout. The word fell on the grass and lay there.

She turned. Across forty yards of green light, he saw her see him. He saw her see the gun. And he saw, with a horror greater than any anger, that she was not afraid. She looked at him with a great sorrowing tenderness, as one looks at a man drowning far out who cannot be reached.

Then the sky opened.

It was not a sound. It was a change in the very grain of things. The green deepened, brightened, poured down like a slow, bright wine, and the lime trees bowed in a wind that did not blow, and the air in Leadford's throat turned to something sweet and cold and vast. His finger tightened. His finger loosened. A thousand small hard things in him, hatred, hunger, years of soot, began to slide away like stones from a melting glacier.

"Nettie," he said again, and could not remember why.

The gun slipped from his hand. The grass came up to meet him, soft as a mother's lap.

VI.

He woke to birdsong.

It was the first thing, and it was so loud, so gloriously, outrageously loud, that for a moment he thought the world had been rebuilt in the night and the birds had not been told to whisper. He lay on his back with the dew soaking his coat and looked up into a sky he had never seen before.

It was blue. Not the thin, washed, grudging blue of the Potteries, but a blue with depth in it, a blue you could have drowned in and been glad. The comet was gone. Only a little pearl of vapor lingered in the east, like breath on a windowpane, and the sun came up behind it like a great gold bell being slowly, silently rung.

He sat up. His head was clear. That was the miracle: his head was clear, as a pond is clear after the last leaf has sunk. He breathed, and the air went into him like clean water into a dry jar. Everything he had thought yesterday stood before him now, and he could see round it, and through it, and to the small, frightened thing it had been hiding.

The revolver lay in the grass, dull and ugly, a bit of iron someone had once believed in.

He looked at it a long moment. Then he picked it up between two fingers, as one lifts a dead mouse, and he walked to the edge of the lawn and dropped it into the lime-shaded pond. It went down with a small round sound, like a word being let go.

Across the grass, Nettie and Verrall were rising from the dew, blinking, laughing, helping each other up. They looked at him. He looked at them. Nobody spoke, for nothing needed saying. Far off, in the mended morning, a church bell began to ring. Beyond it, other bells answered, village after village, over all the green and waking land. Not one rang for war.

Leadford lifted his face to the sun and, for the first time in his life, wept without shame.
