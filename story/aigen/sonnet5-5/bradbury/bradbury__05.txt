The Potteries lay under their own smoke like a congregation of black bottles, and William Leadford walked among them with a letter folded small in his fist. The kilns breathed. The canal lay flat and greasy as a dead man's eye. Above it all, in the east, where the sky should have been plain night, hung a smudge of pale green fire, as if some god had pressed a thumb against the dark and left a print.

"There's your comet, Will," said a man at the corner, and spat. "Come to see us off."

Leadford did not look up. He knew what it looked like. He had looked at it three nights running, and each night it had been larger, a lantern carried slowly toward the house of the world.

Nettie's letter said only: I am sorry. I could not have stayed. Do not look for me.

He read it again beneath the gas lamp, though he had it by heart, as a man licks a wound to be certain of its shape. The words were small and neat and cruel. Behind them he could hear her voice, that bright, quick voice that had once made a back-street kitchen feel like a ballroom.

He walked on. The cobbles shone with rain. Somewhere a dog howled at the sky, and then another, until the whole town seemed full of animals who understood something its people did not.

He had been poor all his life, and had not minded until Nettie taught him how.

She had been a clerk's daughter, with ink on her fingers and a hunger in her eyes for the world beyond Staffordshire. On Sundays they had walked out past the slag heaps to where the fields began, and she had talked of London, of silk, of other lives. He had listened the way a man listens to rain on a roof he cannot afford to mend.

Then Verrall came.

Verrall, with his motorcar, a roaring red animal that scattered chickens and children. Verrall, with his gloves, his easy laugh, his father's money lying around him like warm weather. He had stepped from that car one August afternoon and looked at Nettie as a man looks at a window with the light on inside.

Leadford had seen it happen. That was the worst of it. He had stood in the road with dust settling on his boots and watched her face change, as a field changes when a cloud passes off it.

Now, in his narrow room, he sat on the iron bed and stared at the wall. The wallpaper bloomed with water stains. Through the thin window the comet burned, and he thought: Let it come. Let it fall on all of us. Let it burn the gloves and the motorcar and the smiling mouth.

But the comet only hung there, patient, green, and enormous with silence.

By October the newspapers had grown fat with fear.

In the Dog and Partridge the men leaned over their pints and spoke of Germany, of ships, of treaties torn like cheap cloth. A cattle-voiced man said there would be war by spring. Another said by Christmas. They said it as men speak of weather, with a kind of secret appetite.

"And that thing up there," said the cattle-voiced man, jerking his thumb at the ceiling, "that's a sign. Mark me. Blood and fire."

"It's gas," said a small, pale schoolmaster in the corner. "The astronomers say it's only gas. Cyanogen, they think. Very thin."

"Poison, then."

"Thinner than a breath. Thinner than a thought."

Laughter rolled around the room, and Leadford sat in it like a stone in a river. He drank, and the beer tasted of iron. Across Europe, he thought, ten million young men were sitting in ten thousand rooms like this one, being told the same story. Soon they would be given uniforms and rifles and taught to hate people whose names they could not pronounce.

And he hated too, quietly, with the whole length of his body. Not Germans. One man. One face, golden, careless, bending toward Nettie in the dusk.

When he left the tavern the comet had swollen to the size of a dinner plate. It lit the street the color of old absinthe. His shadow lay beside him, long and strange, as though a second Leadford walked there with murder in his pockets.

The revolver cost him four pounds and nearly all his shame.

A pawnbroker in Hanley sold it to him, wrapped in brown paper like a loaf of bread. "For burglars?" the man asked, not looking at him. Leadford said yes. Both of them knew a lie when they heard one, and both let it pass.

He took the night train south with the weight of it against his ribs. The carriage was nearly empty. Fields slid by, black and silver, and above the fields the comet rode along beside the train as a great pale moth rides beside a lamp. Every farmhouse window seemed to watch him. Every hedge whispered. The wheels beat out their iron syllables: Ver-rall, Ver-rall, Ver-rall.

He had learned where they were from a cousin of Nettie's, a girl who cried while she told it. A house near the Surrey hills, rented for the autumn. Gardens. A terrace. French windows.

Somewhere past Rugby he took out the revolver and held it in his lap. It was small and ugly and astonishingly heavy, as if all the grief of the world had been poured into it and sealed. He thought of his mother's hands. He thought of the pottery wheel, the wet clay rising into a shape under the thumbs, the way a man could make a cup out of mud and call it a morning.

He put the gun away.

The train ran on. In the east, the green light grew brighter, and the stars around it began, one by one, to go out.

He came to the house at midnight, over a wall, through a garden whose roses had gone strange and luminous in that light. Every leaf was edged with emerald. The lawn lay like a lake of cold fire, and the house stood at the far end of it, its windows lit gold.

He saw them through the glass.

Nettie, in a white dress, laughing. Verrall bending to pour wine. A fire in the grate, a piano in the corner, a world arranged for happiness. They looked like figures in a clock, and he, outside, was the weather.

He lifted the revolver. His arm shook. The sky overhead had begun to hum, a low note like a finger drawn around the rim of an enormous glass. The garden brightened until he could see the veins in his own wrist.

"Nettie," he whispered.

She turned, as though she had heard, and for an instant looked straight at him through the window. Her face went white and then, strangely, soft. Not with fear. With wonder. She raised her hand toward the sky.

Then the air changed.

It came down out of heaven like a green curtain falling slowly on the last act of the old world. It had no smell, no weight. It touched his face like a cool and kindly hand. The pistol slid from his fingers into the grass. He thought, quite clearly, Why, how very light I am, and the lawn tilted gently upward to take him.

He woke to birdsong.

For a long moment he lay still, afraid to move, as a man is afraid when he wakes from a sickness and cannot yet tell if the fever has gone. The grass was wet against his cheek. The sky above him was a clean, impossible blue, and the air, the air was sweet. It tasted like water drawn from a deep well. It tasted like the first morning of the earth.

He sat up. The revolver lay beside him, a small dark thing, absurd as a child's toy left out in the rain. He looked at it and felt nothing. No hatred. No hunger. Only a vast, astonished tenderness, as if someone had opened a window in the house of his mind and let out a lifetime of smoke.

"Good God," he said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into weeping, and the weeping into laughter again.

The comet was gone. The green was gone. Everywhere the world shone, every leaf, every drop of dew a small lit lamp. Far off a church bell was ringing, and he understood that it was ringing not for war, nor for death, but simply because bells were made to be glad.

The French windows stood open. On the terrace Verrall was rising slowly from a chair, rubbing his eyes, and beside him Nettie, her hair loose, her face young and bewildered. They looked across the lawn at Leadford, and he at them. No one spoke. All the old grievances lay between them like husks, light and dry and ready for the wind.

Leadford got to his feet. He kicked the revolver beneath the roses, where it could rust in peace.

Then he walked across the shining grass toward them, with his hands open, and the morning opened with him.
