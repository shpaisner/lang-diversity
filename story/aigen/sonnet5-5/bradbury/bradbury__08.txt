The Potteries burned that autumn the color of old pennies. Every kiln along the valley breathed its sulfur into the dusk, and William Leadford stood on the slag heap above Hanley with his hands black to the wrists, watching the sky as a man watches a door he has been told not to open.

The comet hung there. It had been a pinprick in August, a flaw in the glass of heaven. Now it was a pale green thumbprint over the chimneys, and each night it grew a little more, as if someone were pressing harder.

"It'll be the end of us," said the old men at the Rising Sun, and then they laughed, because who in Staffordshire had anything left to end?

William did not laugh. He was twenty-two and had a lean, hungry face, and he carried in his chest the small hot stone that had been Nettie. Her name still lived in his mouth like the taste of a coin. He had meant to marry her in the spring. He had saved four pounds eleven shillings in a tea tin, and he had rehearsed, in the thin hours before the factory whistle, the exact words he would say.

Below him the town lit its lamps, one by one, yellow and patient. They seemed to him the lamps of a country that had forgotten his name.

He went down through the cinders, and the green light followed him, quiet as a cat.

She had left on a Tuesday. That was the part he could not forgive, that it was an ordinary Tuesday, with rain on the cobbles and bread on the table. Her mother stood at the door with her apron twisted in both fists and said, "She's gone with Verrall, William. In his motorcar."

Verrall. The name was a clean white shirt. Verrall, whose father owned the land under three collieries, who had hands like unused gloves and a voice that made even the word good morning sound like a purchase. William had seen him once, idling at the curb, the motorcar throbbing like a great brass heart, and Nettie laughing at something on the pavement.

Now the room where she had lived smelled of lavender and cold ashes. Her ribbon lay on the sill. He picked it up and it weighed nothing, nothing at all.

All that week the newspapers shouted from their stands. Germany, Russia, the Balkans, a name like a dropped plate. Men spoke of war the way they spoke of weather, as something enormous that would arrive whether or not one had an umbrella. In the pubs they argued over maps. In the churches they prayed over the same maps.

And above it all the comet leaned closer, listening.

William bought the revolver from a pawnbroker in Burslem with the money from the tea tin. The man wrapped it in brown paper, tenderly, like a loaf of bread. "For rats?" he asked.

"For rats," said William.

He carried it home against his ribs. It was heavier than the ribbon. It was heavier than anything he had ever owned, and he liked that. A man should have something in his life that weighed as much as his grief.

That night, the comet was so bright that he could read the label on the paper. He sat in his lodging with the gun on his knee and listened to the town. A train screamed somewhere toward London. A dog barked and was answered by another, and another, until all the dogs of England seemed to be passing a single sentence along the hills.

Everyone was waiting. He felt it in the walls. Europe was a great dry field, and someone, somewhere, was striking matches.

Let them, he thought. Let it all go up. But first, Verrall.

They said Verrall had taken her to his father's house at the edge of the Downs, a hundred miles south. William went on foot as far as Stafford, then by the night train, third class, among sleeping soldiers who smelled of wool and beer. Their rifles stood between their knees like patient dark children.

He got down at a small station where the lamps had gone out, and he walked. The road ran white beneath him. The hedges stood black and enormous. Overhead the comet had swollen to a green lantern, and its tail trailed across half the sky like the hem of a bride's dress, and the whole night was lit with a light that belonged to no season.

Strange things happened to the air. It grew thin and sweet. The crickets stopped, then the owls. A cow stood at a gate and did not chew. A breathless hush came down on the countryside, as if the world were holding a note it had never sung.

William walked faster. His shadow fell green on the road, and his breath came silver. In his pocket the revolver knocked against his thigh, a small, dull, faithful knocking, like a man asking to be let in.

"I'll do it," he said aloud. His voice sounded to him like a stranger's. "I will."

At the crest of a hill he saw the house, white among its elms, every window blazing. And beyond the house, rolling down the long slope of the sky, came a pale vapor, soft as breath on a window.

He ran. The gate was open. The gravel shouted under his boots. He saw, through a lit window, a woman's shape turn, and he knew the shape as he knew his own hand, and he lifted the gun, and the air went sweet as apples.

The first breath was like swallowing a bell.

It rang through him, clear and cold, and the gun grew strange in his fingers. He saw it was only iron, only a small hard animal made by men in a hurry. His knees folded. He sat on the gravel as though someone had gently pulled out a chair for him, and the green came over him like water over a stone.

Oh, he thought. Oh, it's only the sky.

He saw the sky was falling in feathers. He saw the whole world leaning toward sleep, the cows and the cities, the soldiers in their trains, the kings in their rooms of maps. A vast soft hand was passing across the face of Europe, closing its eyes.

The last thing he heard was the dogs. All over England they had stopped, and in that silence he thought he heard, for the first time in his life, the earth itself exhaling.

He woke to birdsong, and for a long moment believed he had died and been forgiven for it.

The sky above him was a blue so new it seemed to have been poured that minute. The air tasted of rain and cut grass and something else, something he had no name for, as though every breath were a gift handed to him by an unseen friend. He lay in the gravel and laughed, softly, helplessly, and the sound frightened him, for he had not laughed in a year.

The revolver lay beside him, small and ridiculous. He picked it up and turned it over in the morning light, and he felt a great tenderness for the man who had bought it, that thin, frightened, furious boy with four pounds eleven in a tin. He set it in the grass, the way one lays down a sleeping child.

Footsteps crossed the gravel. He rose.

Nettie stood on the step in a borrowed shawl, and behind her came Verrall, bareheaded, his fine face washed clean of its old smugness. They looked at William, and he at them, and no one spoke. There seemed no need. It was as though a fog had lifted from three separate countries and revealed they had always been one land.

"William," Nettie said, and her voice shook, not with fear, but with wonder. "You came all this way."

"I came to hurt you," he said, and the truth of it was so plain and so small that it hung in the air between them like a moth. "I don't know why. I can't find the reason now. I look for it, and there's only the morning."

Verrall held out his hand. It was not the hand of an owner, but of a man. "I've been awake since dawn," he said. "I was thinking I should come and find you. I was thinking I owed you everything I have."

William took the hand. It was warm. Over the Downs the last green threads of the comet were unraveling into gold, and from far away, from every village, from every town, he heard the sound of bells beginning, not in warning, not in war, but in a kind of astonished joy, as a whole continent woke up and remembered how to be glad.
