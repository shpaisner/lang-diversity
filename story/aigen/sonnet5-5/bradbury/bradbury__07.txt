I. The Pot-Bank Town

In Staffordshire the kilns breathed all night, and the sky above the Potteries was never quite black, only a bruised and smoking orange, as though the world were a great dish forever being fired and never finished. William Leadford stood at his window above the chandler's shop and watched the new star.

It had been a pinprick in the autumn. Now it was a small green coin hung over the chimney pots, with a pale feather of light trailing behind it. The men at the works called it the Visitor. The women crossed themselves, or did not, and spoke of it over their washing in lowered voices.

He held Nettie's letter in his hand. It was a small letter, a few lines in her round schoolroom script, and it weighed more than the kilns.

I cannot go on as we were. Do not look for me. Verrall says…

Verrall. The name was a slick coin, the kind you bite to see if it is false. Verrall with his clean cuffs and his father's money, his motorcar sighing at the curb like a great black animal. Verrall, who had come to the Potteries to see how the poor lived and had taken one home with him.

Leadford folded the paper and folded it again, until it was a hard white seed in his fist. Outside, the comet burned on, unhurried, a green eye that had traveled a hundred million miles to watch him lose everything.

II. The Rumor of Wars

The newspapers came down the hill that week like black birds. Ultimatum. Mobilization. The Powers Gather. In the Rose and Crown, men who could not spell Serbia argued about it with their fists on the table. The beer went flat in the glasses. Over every head, the same rumor hung: that Europe was a dry field, and that somewhere a boy was walking toward it with a match.

"It'll come to guns by Michaelmas," said an old potter, his hands white with clay dust, so that he seemed a statue that had learned to drink.

"Comet'll get us first," said another, and laughed, and nobody laughed with him.

Leadford sat in the corner and heard none of it. He was listening to a smaller sound: the tick of his own anger, patient as a clock in an empty house. He thought of Nettie's hair, which smelled of rain even in summer. He thought of Verrall's hands.

That night he went to the pawnbroker's on Wedgwood Street and came out with something heavy wrapped in brown paper. It was a revolver, cold as a fish, and it lay against his ribs all the way home as if it had always lived there.

Above the roofs the comet had grown. It no longer looked like a coin. It looked like a door, standing slightly open on a green and silent room.

III. The Southbound Train

He rode south with the world going dark and bright around him. The train was a long iron sentence the engine kept trying to finish. Fields went by, and hedges, and towns where the lamps were lit early because people wanted company against the sky.

At every station there were soldiers. They were boys, mostly, with their collars too large and their mothers on the platform holding handkerchiefs like small white flags of a surrender no one had yet declared. Leadford watched them through the smeared glass and felt nothing, or felt a thing so large it was indistinguishable from nothing, the way the sea is indistinguishable from the sky at the far edge of the world.

A woman across the compartment said to no one, "My sister says the comet's going to touch us. Says the air will turn to wine."

"Or poison," said a man behind a newspaper.

"Or nothing," said the woman. "That's what frightens me. That it will be nothing, and we'll go on exactly as we were."

Leadford touched the shape beneath his coat. Go on exactly as we were. Yes. That was the horror, wasn't it? Not the end, but the dull, grinding continuance: the smoke, the wages, the quarrels, Nettie's laugh going out through another man's door.

Near midnight the train crossed the dark spine of England, and the comet rose over the engine's steam, immense now, green as the heart of a bottle.

IV. The House Beyond the Hedge

He found them at the end of a long road, in a house with its windows open to the strange light. A lawn ran down to a hedge of black yew. He stood behind it with the revolver in his hand, and the night smelled of cut grass and rain that had not yet fallen.

Through the window he saw her. Nettie sat at a piano she did not play, her hand resting on the keys, and Verrall stood behind her, saying something that made her turn her face up toward him. Even from here Leadford could see that she was not unhappy. That was the knife. He had come prepared for tears, for guilt, for a little ruined girl in a gilded cage. Instead there was only the lamplight on her throat, and ordinary happiness, a thing he had never once been able to buy her.

He stepped through the gap in the hedge. The gravel spoke under his boots. He raised the revolver, and it was very heavy, heavier than iron had a right to be, as if it held every quarrel since Cain.

And the sky changed.

It came without thunder. The stars went out one by one, like candles pinched by a patient hand, and a green dusk poured over the lawn, over the house, over the yews. Far away, to the east, a sound began, a low flat grumble that might have been guns along some frontier, or the earth clearing its throat. The war had begun, or the world was ending. He could not tell, and found that he did not care.

Nettie came to the window. Her eyes were wide and her mouth was open, and she looked, in that light, like a girl of twelve, like the girl he had met by the canal with a bunch of cowslips in her fist.

V. The Green Breath

The air sweetened. That was the first wonder. After a lifetime of smoke it smelled like apples, like the inside of a church, like the first morning of the world. Leadford breathed it in, and the breath went through him like a river through a dry bed, and the revolver slid in his fingers.

I came to kill, he thought, quite clearly, as one remarks upon the weather.

On the lawn, a gardener had fallen on his side, one hand curled under his cheek, like a child tired out by play. A dog lay sleeping on the step. In the house, Verrall sank down beside the piano, and Nettie folded across the keys with a soft, chiming discord, a last note hanging in the green air and then dissolving.

Beyond the hedge the road was full of the sound of the whole country falling asleep. It was a vast, tender sound, a sigh of cities, of barracks, of ironworks and ships and trenches not yet dug. The grumble in the east faltered, and stopped. The guns, wherever they were, lay down in their own smoke like tired cattle.

"Oh," said Leadford, softly, to the sky.

The comet filled it all now, a great green sea, and he felt himself tilting into it, not falling but being carried, as a leaf is carried, as a boat slips its mooring on a windless night. The pistol dropped from his hand into the grass. He did not hear it land.

His last thought was of the kilns, those orange mouths, and how they too would be sleeping now, cooling, every pot inside them ticking as it turned from fire to stillness.

VI. The Morning After the World

He woke in grass wet with dew, and the light was so clean it hurt.

For a long moment Leadford lay looking up at a sky he had never seen. It was blue, but not the blue of Staffordshire, which was a rumor behind smoke. This was a blue with depth in it, a blue you could drown in and be glad. Birds were shouting somewhere. The air tasted of water and of bread.

He sat up. His head was clear, startlingly clear, like a window washed after years of soot. He looked at his hands, and then at the revolver lying in the grass, small and black and absurd, a child's toy abandoned by a child who had grown up in the night. He looked at it for a long time, and tried to find in himself the anger that had carried him four hundred miles. He felt for it as the tongue feels for a pulled tooth. The place was there, and the ache was there, but the thing itself was gone.

What was I going to do? he wondered, and the wonder was gentle, and appalling.

On the lawn others were stirring: the gardener, rubbing his eyes and laughing without knowing why; the dog, stretching, yawning; a maid at an upper window with her hair down, staring at the sky as if she had never been introduced to it. And from the house came two figures, slowly, hand in hand, blinking like people who had slept a hundred years.

Nettie saw him. He saw her see him. Verrall's face was open and astonished and young, with all its cleverness washed away, and Leadford understood that his own face must look the same.

No one spoke. There was nothing to forgive that was not already forgiven, nothing to explain that the morning had not already explained. Far off, along the road, bells began to ring, not for war, not for death, but simply because the world was new, and bells were what you did with a morning like this.

Leadford stood up. He left the revolver where it lay, for the grass to take. He walked toward them across the shining lawn, and the green dust of the comet glittered on every blade like the first dew on the first day, and behind him, quietly, the old world went on being over.
