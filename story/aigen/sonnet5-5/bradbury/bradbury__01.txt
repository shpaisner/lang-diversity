The kilns of Staffordshire stood against the dusk like a congregation of black bottles, each one breathing its small, sour prayer into the sky. William Leadford walked among them with Nettie's letter folded in his fist, and the paper had gone soft as a dead leaf from the squeezing.

I am going away with Mr. Verrall. Don't hate me, Will. I could not breathe here.

He read it again beneath a gas lamp, though he knew it by heart. Could not breathe. As if the air of the Potteries were something he had personally manufactured and handed her in a cup.

The street was full of ordinary evening. Women scrubbed doorsteps that would be black by morning. A boy rolled a hoop through the puddles, and the puddles were the color of tea. Everywhere the clay dust rose and settled, rose and settled, a gray snow that never melted.

Above the chimneys, where the smoke thinned, hung the comet.

It was not much yet. A smudge, a pale thumbprint pressed upon the glass of heaven by some enormous finger. Men pointed at it from the pub doorways and laughed. In a month, said the papers, it would be a lantern. In two, a lamp. Leadford looked up at it without wonder. Let the sky fall, he thought. Let it come down the chimney and finish us all. He folded the letter small and smaller, until it was a white seed in his palm, and walked on.

The Crown and Anchor smelled of wet wool and old tobacco. The men leaned over their glasses and over one another's newspapers, and their talk was of Germany.

"They'll have the Navy out by Christmas," said a man with a face like a baked potato.

"Let them," said another. "Do us good. Clear the lanes."

Leadford sat in the corner with his untouched beer and listened. Across the table, a boy of nineteen was reading aloud about ultimatums and mobilizations, rolling the long words in his mouth like boiled sweets. Somewhere beyond the sea, he said, men were oiling rifles. Somewhere, the trains were already being counted.

It seemed to Leadford that all of Europe had turned in its sleep and begun to dream one dream, a red one. The whole continent leaned toward the edge like a drunk toward a door. And nobody, nobody, reached out a hand.

"Comet'll sort it," said the baked-potato man, and everyone laughed, and the laughter was thin and strange, the laughter of people whistling past a graveyard they have not admitted is there.

Leadford went to the window. The smudge in the sky had sharpened, just slightly. A tiny green flicker lived at its heart, like a match struck far away in a vast, cold room.

He had nothing. He knew it as plainly as he knew his own hands: the cracked nails, the clay in the creases. He had a room, a trade that was killing him by inches, and a name nobody would remember. Verrall had a motorcar and a father with land. Verrall had a voice like a warm hallway.

And Nettie had gone out through the door of the world, and left him in the draft.

He pawned his father's watch that Thursday. The pawnbroker, a small, tidy man, wrapped the pistol in brown paper as gently as if it were a loaf of bread.

"Hunting?" the man asked.

"Yes," said Leadford. "In a manner of speaking."

He walked to the station with the package under his arm, past the shuttered bakery, past the chapel with its one lit window, past the long wall where someone had chalked WAR in letters tall as a child. The chalk was already blurring in the damp. Even the word seemed tired.

It was the nights that changed first. They grew strange and bright. The comet no longer hung; it burned, and the burning had a color no one could name at first, then everyone could. Green. The green of deep bottles, of pond water with the sun behind it, of a world seen through the wrong end of a jewel.

The dogs knew. They would not go out. The sparrows sat in rows along the rooftops, not singing, heads turned all one way, like a choir waiting for a conductor to lift his arms.

Leadford stood on the platform with the pistol heavy in his coat. The lamps along the track had gone pale and ridiculous beside that light. His hands were green. The porter's face was green, and his own shadow lay on the boards, a long green stain.

He thought: This is the end of something. He did not think it was the end of him. He had not yet grown that large.

The air tasted different. There was a sweetness in it, faint and cool, like the first breath after a fever breaks. He drew it in and it went down through him like water through dry ground. His anger, that hot black coal he had carried for three days, hissed and shifted.

"Do you smell it?" the porter whispered. "Like apples."

Then the stationmaster's bell stopped ringing, though no one had touched it.

Leadford felt the gas come down the way a hand comes down upon a sleeping child's hair. There was no pain. His knees folded politely. The platform tilted, and the green sky wheeled, enormous, slow, benevolent. He saw the porter sink down against a pillar. He saw a woman lower her basket to the ground as if it were an infant. He saw, high in the glass roof, one sparrow let go of its perch and fall, not dead but sleeping, spinning softly down through the lantern light.

Nettie, he thought, and the name was only a name now, a small round stone in a stream. The water went over it and over it.

The pistol slid from his fingers and clanged once on the boards, a dull little sound, very far away.

Then there was no sound at all.

He woke to birdsong.

For a long moment he did not know it for what it was. It seemed too clean a sound, a thing poured, not played. He lay on the platform with his cheek against the boards, and above him the sky was a blue he had not seen since he was a child, a blue like the inside of a shell. The chimneys still stood. But their smoke had gone, and they were only brick, only tall, patient shapes, as innocent as trees.

He sat up. The air went through him like music.

All along the platform, people were stirring. The porter rose on one elbow and looked at his own hands as if they had been given to him that morning. The woman picked up her basket and then simply held it, smiling, tears running down her face for no reason that she could have said, or needed to. Nobody spoke. Nobody needed to. It was as if the whole town had been dipped in a river and drawn out new.

Leadford looked at the pistol. It lay where it had fallen, a small black ugly thing, and what he felt looking at it was not hatred or shame but a vast, startled tenderness, the way one regards a toy that frightened one as a child.

He picked it up and walked to the canal. The water was clear. He could see the stones on the bottom, the slow weeds combing the current. He let the pistol go, and it vanished with a quiet, satisfied sound.

Then he thought of Nettie, truly, for the first time. Not the Nettie who had wounded him, but the girl who could not breathe, and had gone looking for air.

"I hope you found it," he said aloud, and meant it so completely that he laughed, and the laugh rang out across the shining water, and a thousand voices, from every doorway of the waking town, laughed back.
