The kilns of Staffordshire burned all night, and William Leadford had lived beside them so long that he thought their fires were his own blood. They breathed on the sky, and the sky came back black. Rain fell soot-colored on the slate roofs, and the canal lay like a dull knife through the town.

On the night Nettie left, he came home to a kitchen that held its breath. The kettle was cold. A single glove lay on the table, a gray cotton glove with a darned thumb, and he stood looking at it as one looks at a small animal that has died.

There was no letter. She had been too happy for letters. Somewhere out in the dark was Verrall, with his good coat and his easy laugh and his money that smelled of cedar and distant rooms. Somewhere out there a train was carrying her, and every wheel on every rail said never, never, never.

Leadford picked up the glove and put it in his pocket. Then he went out into the street, where the kilns glowed like the doors of some enormous, patient hell, and he walked until morning without knowing he walked.

In the weeks after, he saw the comet.

It came first as a rumor, a smudge the astronomers whispered over. Then it was a pale thumbprint on the evening, and then a green eye opening in the north. Men stood in the pub doorways and pointed with their pipes. Women hushed their children and looked up and did not say what they thought.

Leadford worked at the pottery and turned clay on the wheel, and the clay was cold and wet and wanted to be a cup. He would not let it. He crushed it down and began again, crushed it down and began again.

The newspapers grew thick with other news. Ships were moving in northern waters. A minister had said a thing in a foreign parliament. Men with moustaches shook hands in photographs and did not smile. All Europe seemed a great dry field in August, and somewhere a match was being lifted.

"Let it come," said a man beside him at the wheel, tipping his chin toward the window and the green star. "Let the whole business come. It's all rotten, anyway."

Leadford said nothing. But that night the comet's light lay across his table like a cold hand, and he thought the man was right.

He bought the revolver from a pawnbroker in Hanley, a small black thing wrapped in oilcloth. The pawnbroker did not ask why. In those days no one asked why of anything. The sky had made them all incurious, or all afraid.

He carried it home against his ribs. It was heavier than a thing so small had any right to be. In his room he unwrapped it and set it on the table beside the glove, and the two lay together like a marriage: the soft thing and the hard.

Outside, the evening was strange. The air had a taste, faint and metallic, like a coin held under the tongue. The smoke from the kilns rose straight up and then bent, all of it, toward the north, as though the green star were drawing it upward on a thread.

He had learned where they were. A sister's letter, a careless word. A house in Kent, white and tall, with lawns where Verrall could be gracious to the world. A house with a long window, and in the window, perhaps, Nettie, brushing her hair.

He packed a bag. He packed the glove. He packed the revolver last, as a man packs the one thing he is going to need.

On the platform the stationmaster whispered of mobilization. Soldiers sat on their kit with faces like unbaked bread. Over all of them hung the comet, huge now, no longer an eye but a vast green flower opening.

He went south through a country that seemed to hold its breath.

The train stopped in fields for hours. Through the window he saw villages lit like ships, every door open, every person out in the lanes with faces turned up. Church bells rang without reason. A dog howled at a gate, and the howl went on and on across the meadows, and nobody called it quiet.

Toward midnight the guns began, far off. They were not loud. They sounded like furniture being moved in a house beyond the hills. Someone in the carriage said, "It's begun," and someone else said, "Where?" and nobody knew. The war had started somewhere in the great dark, like a fire in a granary, and it did not matter where.

Leadford sat with his hand on the oilcloth in his coat. He thought of Nettie laughing, and the laugh had changed. It had grown a third voice, and the third voice was Verrall's. He thought of how a man could carry one small thing so long that it became his whole spine.

He left the train at a dark junction and walked. The roads were white under the green light, every hedge and gatepost edged in a ghostly fur. The night smelled of apples and of something else, something sweet and strange and enormous, coming down the wind.

He reached the house near dawn, though dawn did not come as dawn should.

The sky had gone green from rim to rim. It was not the green of leaves or bottles but a green that has no name, the green of the inside of a wave. The comet had touched the world. The air shimmered with it. Birds fell silent in the hedges and then, one by one, dropped from their branches like ripe fruit.

There was the white house. There was the long window. And there on the lawn, as if the whole stage had been set for him, stood Verrall in his shirtsleeves with his face turned up, and Nettie at his side, her hand at her throat.

Leadford drew the revolver. His arm shook. He had rehearsed this a thousand times in a thousand kitchens, and in none of them had the grass been moving like water, or the light poured down like a slow green rain.

"Verrall," he said, and his voice came out small.

They turned. Neither looked afraid. They looked, he thought, like people who had just been told something wonderful and impossible.

He raised the gun. He could not tell whether he pulled the trigger. The air thickened and sweetened, and the world tilted gently on its axis, as a cradle tilts. His knees went soft. The last thing he saw was the sky coming down, kindly, like a great green hand, to close his eyes.

He woke to birdsong.

For a long moment he did not know his own name. He lay on his back in wet grass, and the sky above him was blue, a washed and innocent blue, as though it had been scrubbed in the night. The air was cool and tasted like water from a deep well. Every blade of grass beside his cheek stood separate and shining, and he looked at one for what seemed a century.

Then he sat up, and the mind that sat up was not quite the one that had lain down.

It was clear. That was the wonder of it. Something that had been smoked and tangled in him for years had been lifted out, like a stain washed from a cloth. He thought of the kilns, and the war, and the revolver, and the thoughts came to him without their old teeth. They were only things that had happened to a frightened man.

The revolver lay a few feet away in the grass, small and ridiculous, beaded with dew. He looked at it as one looks at a toy found in an attic, and he felt no wish to touch it.

Across the lawn, Verrall sat up too. He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he was weeping, and smiling, and he looked at Leadford with open, naked, shining eyes. Nettie stirred beside him. She was young. She was only young.

Leadford rose. His legs held. Far off, over the hills, no guns spoke. A great quiet lay over England, and in it a thousand small sounds were waking: a gate, a pump handle, a woman's laughter, the long exhale of a world that had put down something heavy.

He took the gray glove from his pocket and held it out, and Nettie took it, and none of them said a word. There was no need. The morning was too large for words, and they stood together in it while the light climbed, green gone from the sky forever, and the whole bright, forgiven earth turned slowly toward the sun.
