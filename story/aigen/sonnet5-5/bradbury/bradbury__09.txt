The kilns of Staffordshire burned all night, and William Leadford had lived beside them so long that he thought of them as the lungs of the earth, breathing red and black over the slate roofs. Tonight he stood in the yard behind the pottery with his hands hanging empty, and the lungs breathed, and he did not.

Above the chimneys the comet hung like a green thumbprint on the dark glass of the sky.

It had been a pinprick in March. By September it was a lantern. The newspapers called it a visitor, and the vicar called it a sign, and the men at the works called it a bloody nuisance because it kept them out of bed. Leadford had not looked at it for weeks. He had looked at a letter instead. It was a single page, folded small, in Nettie's rounded hand, which had once written Dear Will on the backs of pressed violets.

I cannot stay and be poor. Do not follow. Verrall says you will understand.

He did not understand. He understood only that the paper smelled faintly of her lavender, and that the smell was the cruelest part, because it had not yet learned she was gone.

Behind him the kiln door groaned. Heat rolled across the cobbles. And the green eye in the sky watched him as a bored god might, waiting to see what a small man would do with so much silence.

He remembered Nettie as a season, not a girl. She had been the first week of May, with the hedges white and the canal holding the sky as if it were a plate. She had walked beside him on Sundays with her hand just touching his sleeve, never his hand, so that he walked like a man carrying a bird he was afraid to crush.

Then came Verrall, with his motorcar and his cuffs and his easy, golden laugh, a laugh that had never once been hungry. Verrall's father owned the land under half the town. Verrall stepped out of that machine one afternoon the way a prince steps out of a story, and the street leaned toward him like wheat.

Leadford had loved her with all the clumsy furniture of his heart. He had brought her what he had: a cracked cup he'd glazed himself, a poem he'd copied out wrong, a promise of a cottage with one window facing east. Verrall brought her the whole east.

Now Leadford sat in the kitchen of his lodging, and the clock struck eleven with a sound like a spoon dropped in a well. On the table lay the evening paper. AMBASSADORS RECALLED. FLEETS AT SEA. THE CONTINENT HOLDS ITS BREATH. The words were black and heavy, and under them the ink seemed to tremble, as if Europe itself were a great dark animal turning in its sleep.

He read it twice. Then he laughed, one dry note, because it seemed that the whole world was being jilted at once.

On Thursday he bought the revolver.

The shop was in a back lane behind the market, where a man with a yellow moustache sold knives and fishing line and, from beneath the counter, the small cold arguments that end quarrels. The man did not ask a question. Perhaps in those weeks no one asked questions. The whole country wore the same stunned, feverish look, like people at a railway station waiting for a train they could hear but not see.

The gun was heavier than a heart and about as warm. Leadford put it in his coat and walked out into the streets, where the lamps had gone pale and silly because the comet had stolen their office. The sky was a vast green dusk. Faces turned up in the gutters. A child pointed. A dog, a brown mongrel with one ear, sat in the middle of the road and howled at the thing, a long, rising note that seemed to climb the sky like a ladder.

"End of the world," said a woman at a doorway, almost cheerfully.

"No," said Leadford. "Only the end of something."

He walked the road to Verrall's father's house. He walked it as one walks in dreams, where the distance is not measured in miles but in the weight of what you carry. Hedges slid by like dark waves. The air had a strange sweetness, as if somewhere an enormous orchard stood in bloom, though it was autumn and the fields were stubble.

He passed a camp of soldiers by the crossroads, boys mostly, sitting around a fire and singing something sad and brave. A bugle lay on a drum like a sleeping brass bird. One of them looked up at him and nodded, and in that nod Leadford saw the whole coming war: ten thousand polite young men saying good evening to their own graves.

At an inn he stopped for water, and the room was full of men who spoke in low, quick voices of Germany and France, of treaties and mobilizations and the price of bread. A clock ticked above the hearth. No one looked at the window, where the green light pressed its face against the glass like a patient ghost.

"It's coming for us," one old man said, "all of it. Comet and cannon both."

"Let it come," said another, and drank.

Leadford set down his cup. His hand was on the pistol in his pocket, and he felt a shame so sudden that it was like cold water. He was no different from them. The whole world was a house with its doors being locked one by one, and everyone inside was looking for someone to hurt.

He went out again into the shining dark.

It was near midnight when he reached the long hill above the Verrall house, where the lights of the great windows lay below him, yellow and small, like coins in a dark palm. The comet filled half the sky now. It no longer looked like a light but like a presence, a vast green tide rolling slowly over the roof of the world.

He drew out the revolver. The metal glittered. He thought, Here is the whole of me, then, a man on a hill with a small black answer.

The wind dropped. Every sound in the country stopped at once: no dog, no owl, no far-off train. Even the stars seemed to hold their places. Then the air above him shivered, and he saw the green come down.

It fell as softly as snow falls on a sleeping town. It poured over the hedges and the hilltop, and it was not a flame or a smoke but a kind of shining breath, as though the sky had leaned close to whisper to the earth. It touched his hands. It touched his face. He tasted it, and it tasted of rain and apples and the inside of a church bell.

He tried to lift the gun, and the gun was suddenly so absurd that he could not remember what it was for. His knees folded. The grass came up to meet him, cool and welcoming, and the last thing he saw was the great green light wheeling over the sleeping fields, and the dark roof of the Verrall house, small as a toy beneath it.

He thought, Nettie, and the name was only a name.

Then there was nothing, and the nothing was kind.

He woke to birdsong.

Not the thin, nervous chirping of the smoky mornings, but a flood of it, as if every bird in England had been saving up for this one hour. The sky above him was the palest, cleanest blue he had ever seen, the blue of a new-washed bowl. He lay on his back in the wet grass and breathed, and the breath went down into him like water into dry ground, and it was sweet, so sweet that he began, helplessly, to weep.

He sat up. The hills were gold and green and shining. Dew hung on every blade like small lamps. Far below, the chimneys of the Potteries stood quiet, and the smoke that had hung over them all his life was gone, as if some great hand had wiped the window.

The revolver lay beside him in the grass. He looked at it for a long time, and it seemed a strange, sad little tool, like a toy left behind by a child who had grown. He did not hate it. He could not even hate Verrall. He tried, in the new light, to find the old fury, and found instead a vast and tender sorrow for them all: for himself, for Nettie, for the golden young man in the great house, for the soldiers singing at the crossroads, for every frightened person who had ever mistaken a wound for a world.

Down the slope, a figure came walking through the shining grass. It was a man, bareheaded, and he was laughing, quietly, the way a man laughs who has just remembered something wonderful. He lifted his hand to Leadford, and Leadford, astonished, lifted his own.

"Good morning," the stranger called. "Isn't it a morning?"

"It is," said Leadford. His voice cracked on the word like a boy's. "It is, it is."

He stood. He left the gun in the grass, where the dew would find it and the roots would, in time, take it home. And he walked down the hill into the morning, toward the house, toward whatever waited there, and for the first time in his life the road felt like something that was walking with him.
