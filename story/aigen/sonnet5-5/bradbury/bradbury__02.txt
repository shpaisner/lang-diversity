The Potteries burned all night, and above the burning, the comet hung like a green thumbprint on the dark glass of the sky.

William Leadford stood in the yard behind the crockery works, a clerk's pen still inked on his finger, and read the letter again by the light of the kilns. Dear Will. I am sorry. I could not have lived the way you wanted. Do not look for me. The paper smelled of lavender, which was Nettie's smell, and of nothing else, which was the smell of her leaving.

Staffordshire lay around him in its black lace of chimneys. Bottle ovens squatted in the dusk like old gods with their mouths on fire. Soot fell as softly as snow, and the people who lived under it had learned to call it weather. He had been born in this weather. He had been certain he would die in it, and that Nettie would be beside him, her hand in his, both of them gray and quiet as cups on a shelf.

He folded the letter small and smaller, until it was a white seed in his palm. Then he looked up at the comet. It was larger tonight, swollen and patient. The newspapers said it would pass and the astronomers said it would pass, and Leadford, who believed nothing now, did not believe them.

Nettie had always liked looking at the stars. That was the cruelty of it. She had always wanted something higher than a clerk's wage.

He remembered the canal in June, the barges sliding by with their painted roses, and Nettie walking ahead of him with her face turned toward the one clean patch of sky. She had said, "Will, don't you ever want to be somewhere else?" and he had said, "Where else is there?" He heard it now as the stupidest sentence ever spoken.

Verrall had been the answer. Verrall had come up from London in a motorcar the color of wine, with a tweed coat and white hands that had never held anything heavier than a glove. He had a laugh like ice in a glass. He had stood in Nettie's father's parlor among the antimacassars and the stuffed canary and looked at everything as if it were behind a window. Nettie had watched him the way a woman watches a door opening onto a lit house.

Leadford had seen it, and had said nothing. He had gone on carrying his little sums to the ledger, and his small hopes home at night like bread. Every evening he was poorer than he had been that morning, though his pay never changed. It was the poverty of a man who looks at what he cannot have until the wanting wears a groove in him.

Now the motorcar was gone, and the parlor was dark, and the canary sat stiff on its perch, still singing its one silent song.

By autumn the whole world seemed to lean toward a cliff.

In the public houses, men spoke of Germany as they might speak of weather coming up the valley. Newspapers lay open on the tables like wounds. There were ultimatums and mobilizations, ministers with grave faces, and cartoons of lions and eagles baring their teeth. Leadford heard it all through a pane of smoked glass. What was a continent's quarrel to him? He had his own small war, with one soldier and one enemy, and he meant to win it.

The comet grew every night. It was a green eye now, with a faint tail behind it like breath on a cold window. Children pointed. Old women crossed themselves. A preacher on the corner of Hope Street cried that the heavens were writing, and the crowd stood under his torch with their mouths open, their faces turned the pale color of fish.

On a Thursday Leadford walked to a pawnbroker's near the station and bought a revolver for four pounds and a promise not to ask. It was heavier than he had expected. It lay in his coat like a stone someone had hollowed out and filled with a single idea.

Outside, the sky was waiting. He thought: Let it come. Let it all come. I will be finished before it does.

The train carried him south through a country that seemed to hold its breath. Fields lay under a strange, greenish dusk, though it was only afternoon. Cows stood in a row at a hedge, all facing the same direction, like congregants. A station clock said three, and the light said evening.

At every platform there were soldiers. They were boys, mostly, with their new boots creaking and their rifles held awkwardly, the way a child holds a bird. Mothers walked beside them and did not speak. Leadford looked at their faces and was surprised to find something in his chest that was not hatred. It was lighter than hatred and a great deal more painful. It was the sense that everyone on this train, in this country, under this sky, was being carried toward something none of them had chosen.

He pressed his hand against the revolver. Verrall, he said to himself. Nettie. The names had gone strange, as words do when repeated, until they were only noises, like pebbles in a tin.

Night came down. The comet filled the window now, so near and bright that the carriage seemed lit from beyond the world. The faces of the passengers turned green, then silver, then the color of the dead.

Someone whispered, "It's touching us."

He left the train at a village whose name he never learned. The road ran white between black hedges. Somewhere ahead, past the hill, was the house where Verrall had taken her, and he walked toward it with the revolver in his fist and the sky pouring over him like cold green milk.

The air changed. He felt it first in his throat, a sweetness, as if the world had been rinsed. The hedgerows shivered. A dog barked once, far off, and then stopped, as though it had forgotten what barking was for. On the hill the sky had thickened into a curtain of luminous mist, drifting down in slow folds, soundless as silk.

Far to the east a gun spoke, then another. The war had begun, or was beginning, a low and sullen thunder at the rim of the earth. Leadford heard it and thought, with great calm: Yes. Now. Both ends of the world at once.

He lifted the revolver toward the stars like a man offering a toast.

Then the green came down over the hill and over the road and over him. It filled his lungs like a cool hand, and the thunder in the east grew faint and kind and far away. The gun slid in his fingers. He thought of Nettie on the canal path with her face turned to the sky, and he thought, quite clearly, She was only looking up.

The road rose to meet him, soft as a pillow, and he slept.

He woke to birdsong.

It was the first thing, and for a long while the only thing: a thrush somewhere above him, pouring out its notes as though it had been saving them for a thousand years. Leadford lay on his back in the grass of the road's edge, and the sky above him was blue. It was a deep, rinsed, astonishing blue, with the comet gone as completely as a dream.

He sat up. The air tasted like the first morning of the world. His head was clear, clearer than it had ever been, as if some fog he had carried since childhood had been lifted off him. He looked at his hands, and then at the revolver lying in the dew. It looked foolish, a small black toy, a thing made by frightened men. He could not remember why he had wanted it.

He remembered everything, in fact. He remembered Nettie, the letter, the kilns, the barges with their painted roses. But the pain had gone out of the memories, the way the poison goes from a wound when it is finally washed. What was left was only sadness, and a strange enormous tenderness, as if he were looking at the whole of his life from a high hill and seeing that every figure on it had been lost and stumbling in the same fog.

Down the road, people were rising from the fields. They stood and stretched and looked at one another. Soldiers laid their rifles in the hedges and wept, and then laughed. A woman gave her bread to a stranger. Nobody spoke of war.

Leadford stood up and left the revolver where it lay. Then he began to walk toward the hill, toward the house, toward Nettie and Verrall, with his empty hands open at his sides and the morning pouring gold over everything. He wanted to tell them that he understood, that he wished them well, and that the world had been made new.

Behind him, the thrush sang on.
