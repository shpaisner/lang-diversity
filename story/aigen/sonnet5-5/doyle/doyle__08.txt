The letter came on a Tuesday, and I read it standing in the yard of Clowes's pot-bank, with the kilns breathing red smoke behind me and the clay dust whitening my sleeves. It was a short letter, and Nettie had never been a hand at spelling, but there was no mistaking its meaning. She had gone. She had gone with Verrall, the master's nephew, who had a motor-car, a sovereign for every shilling of mine, and a trick of laughing at me with his eyes while his lips were perfectly civil. "Do not look for me, Willie," she wrote. "You would never have made me happy."

I folded it very small and put it into my waistcoat. Then I went back to my bench and finished the day's work, for a man of my station cannot afford to break down in the middle of a shift. But when the whistle blew and I walked out into the evening, the whole sky over the Potteries seemed to me the colour of an old bruise.

Above the chimneys, a little to the east of the evening star, hung the thing which all the newspapers had been shouting about for a month. It was no more than a faint green smudge, as though some careless hand had rubbed a thumb across the glass of heaven. But I looked at it that night with a sort of sullen kinship. It, too, was rushing toward some catastrophe, and it, too, appeared not to care who stood in the way.

I found Parload that same night in his lodging above the chandler's shop in Hanbury Street. He was a lank, untidy, enthusiastic fellow, a clerk by day and an astronomer by every other hour, and he had trained a battered brass telescope out of his window at the comet since the first night it was seen.

"You have heard, then?" said he, when he saw my face.

"Heard what?"

"Why, that Nettie is gone." He looked away. "The whole street knows it, Leadford. I would have come to you, but I did not know what to say."

I sat down heavily upon his bed. For a time neither of us spoke, and the only sound was the creak of the telescope as he shifted it on its stand.

"Look at it," he said at last, with that curious softness men use when they wish to change a painful subject. "It is nearer by a million miles since Sunday. They say the spectroscope shows cyanogen in the tail. Think of it, Leadford! A poison gas, spread across a hundred thousand miles of space!"

"Let it come," said I. "It may poison the lot of us, and welcome."

"You do not mean that."

"I mean every word."

He gave me the eyepiece, and I looked, and saw the pale, ghostly head of the stranger, trailing its veil behind. I confess it gave me no awe. I could think only of a white shoulder and a pair of grey eyes that had once looked at me as no one else's ever had.

Parload had a newspaper on the table, and my eye fell upon its headlines while he fussed with the lamp. There was trouble in the Balkans, and a note from Berlin, and a speech from some member of Parliament who talked of "the inevitable reckoning." The paper was full of ships and ultimatums. The whole of Europe, it seemed, was in the same fever as myself, and was drifting, with open eyes, towards the same sort of folly.

It was Parload's landlady, a thin, kindly woman with a face like a withered apple, who told me where they had gone. Her sister was a cook at a house near Pyrford, in Surrey, which Verrall had taken for the autumn, and there, so the gossip ran, the runaway pair were installed in what the servants called "great style."

I did not sleep that night. At six in the morning I drew eleven pounds from the Post Office, which was every penny that I had laid by in four years, and at nine I stood in a Hanley pawnbroker's shop, where a stout, wheezing Jew laid upon the counter a heavy revolver of American make.

"For a gentleman's protection?" said he, with only the faintest raising of his eyebrows.

"Aye," said I. "For protection."

He sold me six cartridges with it, and I walked out into the street with the weapon cold against my ribs. I have thought since that no man should ever be allowed to buy a pistol in the first rawness of a grief. It makes of him a different creature, and not a better.

The train to London was crowded to the doors. I stood in the corridor from Stafford onwards, pressed between a commercial traveller and a woman with a hamper of fowls, and I listened to the talk.

It was all of war. A bagman in a check suit declared that the Germans had mobilised on the frontier; a clergyman maintained, with dignity, that no Christian nation could go to such lengths; and a red-faced farmer cried out that he had sold his whole stock of horses to a Government agent at double the market price, and that a man did not do that for a mere alarm. At Rugby a company of Territorials stood upon the platform with their rifles, looking very young and a little sheepish, and a crowd had gathered to cheer them. At Willesden, the placards bore one word in black capitals, and I need not say what it was.

Through the grimy window, as night closed in, I saw the comet again. It had grown. No longer a smudge, it was a pale and definite lamp, with a streaming tail that reached a quarter of the way to the zenith, and its greenish light lay upon the fields like frost. People in the carriage fell silent when they looked at it. I alone, I think, was glad to see it, for it seemed to me the only thing in England that matched my mood.

I reached Pyrford at dusk on the following day, after a long tramp from the station through wet lanes and pinewoods. The air had a curious stillness, and the light, though the sun was down, had a strange green tinge in it, as though the whole countryside lay under the surface of some enormous pond. A dog barked once, far off, and then ceased as if ashamed.

The house stood back from the road behind a screen of rhododendrons, a pleasant red-brick place with a lawn and a conservatory. Lights shone in the lower windows. I crept along the shrubbery until I could see into the lamp-lit dining-room, and there, as plainly as I see this page, sat Nettie at a table with a glass in her hand, and Verrall opposite, leaning back with a cigar, laughing.

She was prettier than I remembered. That was the bitterest part of it. She wore a gown of some pale stuff that I could not have paid for with a year of my wages, and there was a colour in her cheeks that I had never put there.

I drew out the revolver. My hand, I noticed with a curious detachment, was perfectly steady. I thought: I shall wait until he rises. I shall do it at the window, so that she may see. And I tried to feel the hatred which had carried me two hundred miles, and found that it burned hard and bright and small, like the flame of a spirit lamp.

I do not know how long I stood there. A slight breeze had begun to stir the rhododendrons, and with it came a faint, sweetish odour, like the smell of new-mown hay mingled with something I could not name. The green light in the sky had deepened until the lawn looked like the floor of a cavern under the sea. A kind of drowsiness crept over me. I saw Verrall rise and push back his chair, and I lifted the pistol, and saw the barrel waver and grow strangely heavy, as though made of lead.

Then the breath of the comet reached me.

I cannot tell it as it was. I can say only that the air seemed to grow thin and bright, and that my lungs drew in a sweetness which was past all describing, and that a great stillness came down upon the garden. The pistol slid from my fingers and fell among the leaves. I sank upon my knees, and thought that I was dying, and found that I did not mind it in the least.

Through the window I saw Verrall stagger and lay his hand on the table, and Nettie rise, her face bewildered. Then a sort of golden mist swam between us, and my senses went from me altogether.

When I woke, the dawn was in the sky, and the whole world seemed washed clean. The wet grass shone about me. A bird was singing in the thorn tree, and I lay a long while listening to it, with tears upon my face, and with no more hatred in me than a child.
