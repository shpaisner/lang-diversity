I have been asked to set down the plain facts of that wretched autumn, and I shall do so without excuse. I was William Leadford, a clerk in a pot-works at Clayhanger, in Staffordshire, earning a sum I am ashamed to name, and I was in love with Nettie Stuart.

Of the comet there was already much talk. It had been a faint smudge in the telescopes of the astronomers in the spring. By October it was a thing the whole town gazed at from the ends of the streets, where the chimneys of the potbanks stood black against a sky that no smoke could wholly hide. The newspapers I could not afford I read over other men's shoulders. They spoke of the comet on one page and of the Balkans on the next, and I cared for neither. My own heaven and earth were bounded by the garden gate of a small house in Bursley Road.

That week I had walked out with Nettie on the Sunday, and I had said to her what a poor man says: that I had prospects, which I had not, and that I would wait for her, which was the only true word of the three. She had looked at me strangely, with her gray eyes full of something I took for pity. I know now what it was.

I called at the house on the Thursday evening. Her mother opened the door, and I saw in her face, before she spoke, that the thing had happened.

"She is gone, William," said the old woman. She held out a sheet of paper with a hand that shook like a leaf. "She has gone with young Mr. Verrall. They took the afternoon train."

I read the letter standing on the step. It was short and kindly and abominable. She said she could not bear the life that lay before her, that she had tried, that I should forget her. She said nothing of love, not even for him.

I do not recall leaving the house. I recall only that I was presently standing in the road with the paper crumpled in my fist, and that over the roofs the comet hung, enormous and pale, with its tail spread like the fan of some indifferent goddess.

For three days I went to the works and added columns of figures. They came out right, which astonished me, for my mind was elsewhere and wholly occupied. The Verralls were people of property, and the son was one of those smooth, pleasant, idle young men who take what they want as a matter of course. I had spoken with him once. He had been courteous to me, and I had hated him for it.

In the evenings I sat in the bar of the Red Lion, where the talk was all of war. A red-faced commercial traveller declared that the Germans would be in the Channel by Christmas. A schoolmaster maintained that the Powers would never dare. A man in the corner, a reservist, said little, but he sharpened a clasp-knife on his boot and smiled. Their voices seemed to come from a great distance, and I understood that the world, like myself, was sliding toward some brink and that no one had the will to stop.

"It's the comet," said the traveller. "It gets into the blood. Mark me, there'll be trouble."

I thought that there would, and that I knew where it would begin.

On the Saturday I drew my few savings from the Post Office. In a pawnbroker's shop in Hanley I bought a revolver of Belgian make, with a box of cartridges, from a man who asked no questions and gave me none of the courtesy of a pretended doubt. I carried it home wrapped in a newspaper that told of mobilization on the Danube. The weight of it in my pocket was strangely comforting. It made me, for the first time that week, a man who had a purpose.

I knew from Nettie's mother that Verrall's people had a house near Lorraine Heath, in Surrey, and I was sure he would take her there, or near it. I went up by the night train, third class, among soldiers and sleeping women. At every station there were crowds upon the platforms, standing with their faces turned to the sky. The comet had grown greater by the hour. Its head was a disc of cold green fire, and a strange lividness lay on every field and hedge, so that the whole countryside looked like a landscape seen through bottle-glass.

At dawn I was walking up a lane between dripping hedges, with a labourer's directions in my head. A newsboy ran past me, shouting something I could not catch. Then a second, and a third. A man at a cottage gate called out to me, with a face quite white, that the Germans had crossed the frontier, and that the fleets were at sea. He seemed to expect me to be moved. I nodded and went on, for my war was a smaller one, and nearer.

I found the place toward noon: a villa of red brick and creeper, standing in a garden with a cedar upon the lawn. I stood in the road for a long time, looking at the windows. The light had become very strange. The sun was but a dim yellow wafer, and the air had a faint, sweetish and metallic taste, like a coin held upon the tongue. The birds had stopped singing.

I saw her presently. She came out upon the lawn alone, in a white dress, and stood looking up at the green sky with her hands clasped before her. She looked thinner. She looked, I thought, very young, and not at all happy.

I took the revolver from my pocket. I will confess that my hand was steady, and that I was cool, with the dreadful coolness of a man who has ceased to reckon consequences. I opened the gate. I walked up the gravel. She turned at the sound of my steps, and her face changed, but she did not cry out.

"Willie," she said. "Oh, Willie, what have you done?"

"Where is he?" I said.

Before she could answer, the whole garden seemed to quiver. A fine film of green crept across the lawn, as a mist creeps over a pond, and the cedar faded behind it. The air grew sweet and thick, and my ears sang with a high, clear note. I tried to raise the pistol, and found my arm had no more strength than a child's.

"Nettie," I said, and my voice seemed to come from the bottom of a well.

I saw her sink upon the grass, quite gently, like one who lies down to rest. Then the earth tilted beneath me, and the green light swallowed everything, and I fell.

I do not know how long I lay there. It might have been a night, or a thousand years. I knew only that I returned to myself slowly, as a swimmer rises through clear water, and that the first thing I felt was the cool grass against my cheek.

The sky above me was blue. It was a blue I had never truly seen before, deep and tender and washed quite clean, and the sun stood in it, golden and kind. The air was sweet as a spring morning after rain. I lay for a while without moving, and I was aware of something extraordinary in myself, a quietness where the fever had been. I sat up. The revolver lay beside me in the grass, small and black and foolish, and I looked at it with an astonishment that was almost laughter. I could not conceive how any man could have carried such a thing for such a reason.

A little way off, Nettie was sitting up with her hands in her lap, looking at me. There were tears upon her face, but she was smiling, and in her eyes I read no fear at all. At the window of the villa a figure appeared, and I knew it for Verrall. He too stood looking out at the morning, bareheaded, with his hand upon the sash and the wonder of a child upon his face.

"Willie," said Nettie softly, "do you feel it too? As if we had been ill, and were suddenly well?"

"Yes," I said, and found my own eyes wet. "I think the world has been ill a long time."

I rose and went to her, and I held out my hand, and she took it. The anger was gone. I looked at the lawn, the cedar, the shining dew, and I understood that the comet had passed over us and taken with it every poison that we had mistaken for ourselves. Somewhere beyond the hills, I knew, soldiers were waking in their trenches and finding no hatred left to fight with. I picked up the revolver, walked to the garden wall, and dropped it into the deep green water of the ditch beyond. Then I turned back to the two who had wronged me, and to my surprise, there was nothing I wished to say to them but good morning.
