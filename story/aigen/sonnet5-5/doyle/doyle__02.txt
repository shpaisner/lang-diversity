I.

It was on a wet Thursday in October that I first understood Nettie was lost to me. I had walked from the Clayton works to her father's cottage, as I had done on three evenings in every week for two years, with the clay still grey under my fingernails and a ring in my pocket that had cost me eleven weeks of saving. The door was opened by her mother, and I knew from the woman's face, before a word was spoken, that something was wrong.

"She's gone, William," said Mrs. Stuart. "She went by the afternoon train. There was a letter on the mantel."

I took the paper from her. It was a poor little letter, written in Nettie's round schoolgirl hand, and it said that she could not bear the smoke and the poverty any longer, that Mr. Verrall had been very kind, and that I was not to follow her. The name of Verrall was not new to me. He was the young gentleman from the great house beyond Trentham who had been seen too often that summer on the lane behind the gasworks. I had warned her against him once, and she had laughed at me.

I remember that I folded the letter very carefully and put it in my pocket beside the ring. Then I said good-night and went out into the rain. The smoke of a hundred potbanks lay low across the valley, and the furnaces glowed red beneath it, like sullen eyes.

II.

My father's cottage in Bursley Row had one room below and one above, and in the lower room my father sat reading the Staffordshire Sentinel by the light of a single candle. He looked up as I came in and asked no questions, for the Leadfords are not a talkative race.

"There's a deal in the paper about that comet," he said at last, tapping the page. "The astronomers say it will come close by Christmas. They've found something queer in the tail of it, a gas that none of them can name."

"Let it come," said I. "It can burn the Five Towns to cinders, and welcome."

"Aye, you'd say that tonight," said he, with a sort of rough kindness. "But sleep on it, lad. A man does foolish things with a wound fresh upon him."

I did not answer, but went up to my bed. I did not sleep. I lay listening to the rain on the slates and the distant clank of the night shift, and I turned over in my mind every kindness that Nettie had shown me and every slight that I had ignored. It is a curious thing that in such hours a man will remember the trifles best: the blue ribbon she wore at the Whitsun treat, and the way she would laugh with her head thrown back. Towards dawn the pain hardened into something colder and more dangerous, and I knew that I should not rest until I had looked once into Verrall's face.

III.

There was little in that district to distract a man from his troubles, and at the works on Friday there was less. The foreman, Mr. Boomer, a lean and sour Methodist, called me into his office and informed me that trade was falling off and that a number of hands must go.

"It's the war, Leadford," said he. "The German orders are all cancelled. They say the Kaiser's ships will be at it with ours before the spring."

"There'll be no war," said I. "It is only talk."

"It's talk that has closed the Burslem kilns," he retorted, "and will close ours. You may call at the counting-house for your money."

I took it, eleven shillings and some pence, and went out into the street. At the corner by the Red Lion a knot of unemployed potters were arguing loudly over a newspaper. One declared that the Russians were mobilising; another, that the whole business was a plot of the capitalists to thin the ranks of labour. I stood on the edge of them and listened, and I thought how small and far away it all seemed. Nations might drift toward the abyss as they pleased. I had a private war of my own, and a very definite enemy.

That afternoon I walked into Hanley and went into a pawnbroker's shop in Market Street. When I came out, a revolver weighed down the pocket of my coat, and five shillings of my eleven were gone.

IV.

It was not difficult to learn where they had gone. The station porter at Stoke, a talkative fellow who had known me from a boy, told me with a wink that the young lady and a gentleman had taken first-class tickets to London, and that the gentleman had tipped him half a crown.

"Very free with his money, sir, was he," said the porter. "A real swell."

"Did you hear where they were going?"

"Ah, now, I did catch a word of it. Something about a house at Pinner, I think, or Pinnock. A friend's house, as I understood him."

It was little enough, but I have heard Sherlock Holmes himself declare that the smallest fact is worth a mountain of speculation. I put it away in my memory, and I drew from my father, with great difficulty, a loan of two pounds. He gave it without a word, and held my hand a moment longer than was needful.

"Don't do murder, William," said he, very low. "There's no woman living worth the rope."

"I don't mean to do anything, father," I answered, and I was lying, though I scarcely knew it myself. I walked out through the dusk of the Potteries with the bright new weight of the weapon against my ribs, and I did not look back.

V.

That night, as I waited upon the platform for the London train, I saw the comet plainly for the first time.

I had read of it, and heard it talked of, but I had never lifted my eyes to look. It hung low in the north-east, above the furnace-smoke, and it was no longer the faint smudge of the early newspapers. It had a hard bright head, like a pin of white fire, and from it there streamed a pale and tremulous tail that seemed to quiver as though stirred by some wind of its own. The very air upon the platform seemed to hold its breath beneath it.

"It's a fine sight," remarked a stout commercial traveller beside me. "They say it's a hundred million miles away yet. I don't hold with it myself. Comets mean wars, my mother always said."

"Your mother may have been right," I said.

"Ah, you've got the blues, young fellow," he said, and offered me a cigar. I took it, for no better reason than that I wished to be spoken to by someone who did not know my trouble. As the train drew in with a roar and a shower of sparks, I looked once more at the pale star and wondered idly whether it saw the little black ant-hills of men beneath it, and what it made of them: their wars, their furnaces, and their love affairs.

VI.

I reached the house at Pinner a little after ten on the following night, having spent a whole day in questioning ostlers, postmen and village shopkeepers. It stood alone at the end of a gravel drive, a pleasant villa behind a screen of laurels, with lamplight glowing yellow in two of its lower windows. Over the roof the comet burned, and it seemed to me larger and greener than it had been in Staffordshire, as though it had crept nearer during my journey. It threw a faint colourless glimmer across the lawn, so that the shadows of the shrubs lay double upon the grass.

I stood in the shadow of the gate with my hand upon the revolver and I examined myself as honestly as I could. I wished to say that I was there to rescue her, or to demand justice; but the truth was that I hated him, with a plain, sick, physical hatred, and that I had walked two hundred miles in my heart to meet it.

A figure passed before the lighted window: a young man in evening dress, laughing, with his head turned over his shoulder to someone within. It was Verrall. I knew him at once, with his handsome careless face and his easy confidence. And then a second figure joined him, slight and fair, and put a hand upon his arm.

I drew the revolver and found that my fingers were quite steady. Overhead the strange light deepened, and a hush fell over the garden, as though the whole world were waiting. I stepped forward upon the gravel, and it crunched very loudly under my boots.
