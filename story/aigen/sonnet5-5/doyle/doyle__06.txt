I.

I was twenty-two years of age that autumn, and I earned eighteen shillings a week as a clerk in the pot-works at Clayton, in the heart of the Staffordshire Potteries. It is a country of smoke and slag-heaps, where the very sky seems to have been fired in one of the bottle-kilns and come out the colour of a dirty saucer. I mention it because a man is shaped by his surroundings. I had no money, I had few prospects, and I had one possession of any value to me in the world, which was the belief that Nettie Stanton cared for me.

We had walked together on Sundays for the better part of two years. She was a slight, dark girl with a quick, impatient way of speaking and eyes that were always looking over my shoulder at some better thing. I did not see that last point then. I see it plainly now.

It was in these days that the newspapers first began to speak of the comet. A telescope in America had picked it up, a pale smudge among the stars, and the learned men differed greatly as to its meaning. The same newspapers, upon other pages, spoke of the German navy, of Balkan quarrels, and of the Foreign Office in a state of nerves. Looking back, I am struck that I read of such matters with perfect indifference. A man in love sees the end of the world as a small affair beside the closing of a door in a particular house.

II.

My friend Parload lived in two rooms over a chandler's shop, and in the attic above them he had rigged a six-inch telescope with a great deal of brown paper and more devotion than money. He was a lean, stooping, short-sighted fellow, a clerk like myself, but with a mind that went travelling among the planets.

I climbed to him one night in October, because I wished to be spoken to by somebody who would not mention Nettie. He took me by the sleeve and put my eye to the instrument.

"There," said he. "Do you see it?"

I saw a faint, ghostly brush of light, with a hard bright point at its heart.

"It is coming straight at us," said Parload softly. "Not to strike us. The mathematicians are agreed upon that. But we shall pass through the tail of it, and what is in the tail nobody can say. There is a green band in the spectrum, Leadford. It is a gas that no chemist has ever handled."

I was hardly listening. At last, with the awkwardness of a man who dislikes his errand, he cleared his throat.

"I ought to tell you something. I do not know that it is my business. But I saw Nettie Stanton on Thursday, in Trentham Road, in a motor-car. The man beside her was young Verrall."

The name was known to me. Verrall was the son of the great manufacturer and a gentleman of leisure, with a smooth, fair, careless face. I heard the clock in the room below strike nine, and I remember counting every stroke.

III.

I did not go home. I walked the four miles to the Stantons' cottage in a thin, cold rain, and found the old man sitting by a dying fire, with a paper in his hand.

"She is gone," he said, without looking up. "She was gone when I came in from the yard. She has left this."

The note was brief and not unkind. She had written that she could not endure the life before her, that she did not blame me, and that I should be happier in the end. She did not say where she had gone, but she had no need. The perfume of a rich man's cigar still hung faintly in the passage, where no cigar of mine had ever been.

I think old Stanton wept. I had no tears in me, only a cold and growing clearness of mind. I looked about the little room, at the cheap china dogs on the mantelpiece and the cracked glass over the dresser, and I understood what she had seen in them. I understood, too, for the first time, what I felt towards the man who had bought her out of them.

"Where would he take her?" I asked.

The old man shook his head. But upon the table lay a railway guide, open, with a pencil mark against a name in Essex. It was a small thing, and one which a cleverer man might have concealed. Verrall was not clever. He was only rich, which in this world serves nearly as well.

IV.

Next morning every placard in Hanley bore the same word: ULTIMATUM. Germany and England had exchanged notes of a kind that does not end in compliments. Men stood about the street corners in knots, and a recruiting sergeant, who had hitherto been a lonely figure, found himself surrounded.

I took no part in it. I went to a pawnbroker in Burslem, and there, for two pounds ten and my silver watch, I bought a revolver of Belgian make, together with a box of cartridges. The pawnbroker, a sallow man with a ledger face, glanced once at my expression and filled in his book without remark. I believe that was the most honest transaction of my life.

On my way to the station I passed beneath the open sky, and there for the first time I saw the comet with the naked eye. It was not yet night; the sun was scarcely down. But there it hung in the east, a pale and watchful star with a feather of light behind it, and I fancied that it had grown since Parload showed it to me. A woman beside me crossed herself. A man laughed, and his laugh stopped short.

I took a third-class ticket for Essex and sat with my hand upon the weapon in my pocket. The train carried me south through the dusk, past furnace-glare and sleeping fields, and always the green star kept pace with the window.

V.

I reached the place a little after midnight. It was a village among low hills, with a single inn, closed and dark, and a church whose clock had stopped at a quarter to four. A carter who was loading his dray told me, in a thick voice, that there was a new tenant at the Grange. A gentleman from London had taken it for the autumn, with a young lady. He added that the folk thereabouts thought it a poor season for pleasure, with the sky as it was.

The sky was indeed strange. The comet had climbed high, and it lit the lane with a hue that I can only call a deep, sea-green pallor, so that the hedges and my own hands looked like things seen under water. There was no wind. A dog howled in some distant farmyard and was suddenly still. The air had a thin, sweetish quality, as though a window had been opened upon some other world.

I do not say that I was calm. I was cold, which is a different matter. I went up the drive between two rows of black firs, and I saw a light in the lower window of the house, a warm yellow light, which seemed to me the most insolent thing I had ever looked upon.

Within, a piano was being played, softly and badly. I knew the touch. Nettie had learned that air from me.

VI.

The French window yielded to my hand. I stepped through it into a room of lamplight and flowers, of deep chairs and silver, such as I had known only from the pages of magazines. Nettie sat at the piano in a gown of some pale stuff. Verrall stood by the mantelpiece with a glass in his hand, and when he turned I saw his face go white, then red, then assume that careless, indulgent expression that I hated more than either.

"My dear fellow," said he. "This is really too absurd."

Nettie rose and said my name. There was no shame in her voice, only fear, and the fear was not for herself.

I drew the revolver. I remember the weight of it, and the click of the hammer, which sounded very loud in the room. I remember that Verrall did not move, though his glass shook and a little wine ran down his wrist. I said something. I have never been able to recall what it was, for the words were thin and foolish, and the thing in my heart was neither.

Then the lamp flame changed. It sank, wavered, and turned from yellow to a vivid emerald, and the whole room was washed in that unearthly light. At the window the garden shone like glass. Nettie cried out and pointed upward. Over the black firs the comet had swollen to the size of the moon, and from it, silent and soft as snow, a green vapour was falling upon the world.

My finger tightened upon the trigger, and I felt the strength run out of it. A strange sweetness crept into my lungs. I saw Verrall sink into his chair with a wondering smile, and Nettie's hand fall upon the keys with one faint chord. The revolver slipped from my fingers and struck the carpet without a sound. Then the green light closed over us all, and I knew no more.
