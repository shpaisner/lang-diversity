I.

It was on a raw evening in October, with the smoke of the potbanks lying low over Staffordshire like a dirty fleece, that I learned Nettie had gone. I remember that I had come in from the works with my hands still grey with clay, and that my mother stood in the kitchen doorway with an open letter, her face quite white.

"It's from the Rawlinsons," she said. "The girl has left them. She went off on Tuesday, William, with young Verrall in his motor-car."

I read the thing standing. I did not cry out or break anything, as a man in a novel might have done. I merely set down my cap upon the dresser, very carefully, and looked at the fire. Yet I knew in that instant that something had gone out of me which would not return. For three years I had stinted myself, saved my shillings, and read my books by a tallow candle, all that I might one day be worthy of her. And Verrall, who had never done an honest hour's labour, had but to lift a finger.

"You will do nothing foolish," said my mother.

"I shall do nothing at all," I answered, and I believe I even smiled. It was the smile of a man who has already determined upon something.

II.

All that autumn there had been talk of the comet. It had been discovered by an observer at the Cape, and the newspapers, having little else to fill their columns, made much of it. Night after night it grew, a faint smear of light in the north-east, and then a definite brush, and at last a pale, unwinking eye that stared down upon our chimneys.

But it was the other news that men read with set faces in the smoking-rooms and the public bars. The Continental powers had been quarrelling over their frontiers and their fleets. Ultimatums crossed and recrossed. In the Potteries, as everywhere, men who could not have found Alsace upon a map spoke confidently of mobilisation, and of what "we ought to do to the Germans."

I took no part in it. I walked home beside Tom Hobbs, a fellow-workman, who held that the comet and the war were one portent.

"The world's going to the devil, Leadford," said he.

"It may go where it pleases," said I. "It has been a poor place enough for me."

He glanced at me sidelong. "You've been brooding, man. She wasn't worth it."

"That," I said, "is the one thing nobody can know but myself."

And I looked up at the green-white wisp above the chimney-pots, and felt neither awe nor fear, but only a dull, comfortable hatred of everything that lived.

III.

I lost my place at the works within the fortnight. I had grown careless, and sullen with the foreman, and a master potter has small patience with a man who spoils a firing. I took my wages without complaint and walked out into the yard.

It was there that I came upon old Rawlinson, Nettie's employer, who had followed me from the office. He was a stout, anxious person, much distressed.

"I'll tell you what I know, Leadford," said he, lowering his voice. "They are at a house near Sevenoaks, belonging to Verrall's father. It's no secret. He means to leave her when he tires."

"And she?"

"She's a fool, but she is not a bad girl. I thought you ought to know."

I thanked him and went on. My road to the station took me past a pawnbroker's, and in the window, among the flutes and the wedding rings, lay a revolver with a mother-of-pearl butt. I stood a long while before it. Then I went in. The man behind the counter asked no questions, for it was a day when half England was buying weapons against the invasion that was to come.

With the thing wrapped in a handkerchief in my pocket, I felt for the first time in weeks a clear purpose. It was almost like happiness.

IV.

I reached London in the late afternoon of the next day, and crossed to Kent by a slow train that stopped at every halt. The comet had risen before the sun was fairly down, and from the carriage window I saw it hang above the Weald, vast now, and strangely beautiful, trailing behind it a veil of light like the train of some celestial bride.

There was no one at the little station save a porter, who told me that the Verralls' place was a mile and a half upon the hill road. I walked. The hedges were silver with the odd radiance, and the air had a stillness that I had never known. No dog barked. No wheel turned. Once I passed a labourer leaning upon a gate, his face lifted to the sky, and he did not so much as turn his head at my step.

I thought of Nettie as I had last seen her, in her grey Sunday dress at the chapel door, the light upon her hair. I thought of Verrall's easy laugh. I put my hand upon the revolver, and found, curiously, that my fingers were cold and perfectly steady.

Far off to the east there came a dull sound, like a door slammed in some enormous house. I did not know then whether it was thunder or guns.

V.

The house stood back from the road behind a screen of beeches, a long white villa with lighted windows. I stood among the trees for a quarter of an hour, watching. A gramophone was playing within, very thin and distant. Once a shadow crossed a blind, and I knew the set of those shoulders, and my heart gave a single great blow.

It was then that I became aware of the change in the air.

I can describe it only by saying that the night grew clear. A faint greenish tinge stole across the lawn, and the beech leaves took a colour I had never seen, an emerald with no earthly sun behind it. There was a taste upon my tongue like that of cold water after fever. The gramophone ran down in the midst of a bar, and no one wound it again. Somewhere in the house a man laughed uncertainly, then stopped.

I drew breath, and the breath seemed to go to the very bottom of my lungs, and to carry a sort of light with it. My head swam. I leaned against a tree-trunk, and the revolver slipped from my fingers into the grass.

"It is the end of the world," I thought, quite calmly. "The comet has struck us."

And the strangest part was that I did not mind.

VI.

I do not know how long I lay there. When I came to myself the stars were out, and the green had paled to the faintest mist along the ground. My limbs were light, my head perfectly clear, and I felt as a man might who had been ill for years and wakened on the first morning of health.

I looked about for the revolver and found it. I turned it over in my hand, and I stared at it with a kind of wonder, as one stares at a child's toy found in the pocket of a man's coat. Why had I come? The reason lay in my mind like a dead thing, and I saw it without the least passion, and saw also how small and thin and mean it was.

The front door of the villa stood open. A figure came out upon the step and stood in the lamplight, and it was Verrall. His face was pale, his collar torn open, and he looked up at the sky with the expression of a man who has had some great confusion lifted from his brain. He saw me. He did not start nor reach for a weapon. He came slowly down the gravel.

"You are Leadford," he said. "I know you. She told me of you." He held out his hand, unsteadily. "I have been a scoundrel to her and to you. I cannot think what possessed me."

I looked at the hand, and then, with a sense of release that I cannot put into words, I dropped the pistol into the dew and took it.

"Nor can I," I said, "what possessed me."

And together we turned to the lighted door, where Nettie stood waiting, her face wet and shining.
