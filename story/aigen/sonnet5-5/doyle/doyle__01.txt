I.

I have been asked to set down how the thing came upon me, and I shall do it plainly, as a man gives evidence. My name is William Leadford. At the time of which I write I was a clerk of twenty-three, living in a brick row at the edge of the Potteries, where the bottle-kilns stood against the sky like the chimneys of some vast, ill-tempered kitchen.

I had loved Nettie Stewart since we were children together. I had not much to offer her: thirty shillings a week, a mother's good opinion, and a head full of books I could not afford to finish. Yet I had believed she understood me.

On a Tuesday in October I came home and found her father at my door with a face like wet clay.

"She's gone, Willie," he said. "She went by the afternoon train. There was a gentleman. Verrall, they call him. He has the big house beyond Bishop's Heath."

I remember that I did not speak. I stood looking over his shoulder at the yellow sunset, and above it, pale and enormous in the east, hung a faint green smudge, the comet that all the papers had been chattering of for a month. It seemed to me no more than a thumbmark on a window, but I could not take my eyes from it. Nettie was gone, and that thing in the sky was still coming nearer.

II.

For three days I went about my work like a machine. Then the anger came, slow and cold, and with it a clearness I had never known before.

In the evenings I sat in the Crown and Anchor, where the men read out the newspapers in turn. The news was all of the Balkans, of mobilisations and ultimatums, and of a German Emperor who rattled his sabre in every second paragraph. Old Hammond, the wheelwright, struck the table with his fist.

"There'll be war before Christmas, mark me. And what's the comet to it? A star can't do worse than a king."

"They say it's bringing a gas," said a young glazer.

"They say anything," said Hammond. "Let it come."

I laughed with the rest, but my thoughts were elsewhere. On the Friday I walked into Hanley and, in a pawnbroker's back room, bought a revolver and a box of cartridges for two pounds ten. The man asked no questions. I fancy he had seen that look on other faces.

I told myself it was justice, that a man who steals what another has lived for forfeits his own claim to live. I put it to myself in a dozen shapes, and all of them were reasonable, and under all of them lay a dull, sick wish to see Verrall's face when he understood.

That night the comet stood higher, and the whole east was tinged with a poisonous green.

III.

I travelled south on the Sunday with the revolver in my breast pocket and a small bag at my feet. The carriage was nearly empty. Opposite me sat a clergyman reading a pamphlet upon the end of the world, which he held rather tightly, I thought, for one who professed himself at ease about it.

"You do not fear it, sir?" he asked at last.

"I am not thinking of it," I said.

"A great many are thinking of nothing else. The stationmaster at Rugby told me that the Continental trains are crowded, and that the Reserves are being called out in France."

I looked out of the window. Fields went by, and church towers, and farmhouses with their lamps just lit. All the world looked placid, and I thought it strange that a country so quiet could hold so many forces ready to tear it. And I thought it stranger still that I, the most ordinary of men, should be carrying a weapon to do murder with.

By dusk we were in Surrey. The comet now covered a quarter of the heavens, no longer a smudge but a ragged, shining fan, and the clergyman had ceased to read. When I stepped down at the little station the porter was standing with his face turned upward and did not so much as ask for my ticket.

IV.

It was seven miles to Verrall's house, and I walked them. There was no moon, but the sky gave light enough, a dim bottle-green radiance, in which the hedges and the road seemed carved from some strange stone. No wind stirred. Once a dog howled far off, and then I heard nothing but my own boots.

I had a curious sense that the world was waiting. I have since heard others describe it in the same words. The very leaves hung as if listening, and a hush lay over the heath that I could almost feel upon my skin.

I thought of Nettie as I walked. I remembered her at fourteen, laughing on a stile with her hair blown across her mouth. I remembered the shy way she took my arm. Then the picture would break, and I would see Verrall's long, pale, assured face beside hers, and my hand would close upon the butt of the revolver.

About ten o'clock I reached the lodge gates. They stood open. Between the black shapes of the pines I could see lighted windows, and the great house sitting in its lawns like a ship at anchor. A faint, sweetish smell was on the air, and I noticed that my head swam a little as I breathed it.

V.

I crossed the lawn at a stumbling run. The French windows of the library were unlatched, and I thrust them apart and entered.

They were there. Verrall stood by the fireplace in his evening dress, one hand on the mantel. Nettie sat in a low chair with her face white and startled. At the sight of me she half rose, and I saw that she was afraid, not of the revolver, which I had already drawn, but of me.

"Leadford," said Verrall, quite quietly. "I rather thought you would come."

"Then you know why."

"I know what you imagine." He did not move. His calm maddened me. "Put it down, man. You are ill. Look at yourself."

I raised the weapon. My arm shook, and the lamp seemed to swim in a greenish halo. I wished to pull the trigger, and I could not. A heaviness crept along my limbs. The room tilted; Nettie's cry came to me thin and far away, like a voice down a well.

"It is in the air," said Verrall, and now his own voice faltered. "Nettie, the window. Shut—"

The revolver slid from my fingers and struck the carpet. I saw the lamp flame turn from yellow to a shimmering emerald, saw Verrall sink slowly into a chair with a look of wonder on his face, and then a great soft darkness closed upon me, and I knew no more.

VI.

I woke to a sound of birds.

For a time I lay without thought, on my back on the library carpet, and looked up at a ceiling that seemed to me lovelier than any I had seen. Daylight, clear and golden, poured through the open windows. The air was sweet and cool, as if the whole world had been washed in the night.

Then I remembered, and sat up with a start of horror. The revolver lay a yard from my hand. I looked at it as one looks at a dead rat. I could not conceive what had possessed me to carry it. The anger, the bitter reasoning, the long cold hatred were all gone, not stifled but gone, like a fever that has broken. What remained was a vast, quiet astonishment, and under it a great shame.

Verrall lay in his chair, still asleep, his face softened like a boy's. Nettie was curled upon the sofa, her cheek against her arm. I rose unsteadily and went to the window.

The lawn glittered with dew. Beyond the pines the sky was a pure and tender blue, and the comet was gone. I stood there with my hands on the sill, breathing deeply, and found that my eyes were wet. A thrush was singing on the cedar. In the distance I heard the church bell of some village, and for the first time in my life it sounded to me like a thing that was meant.

Behind me someone stirred and sighed. I turned, and Verrall was sitting up, passing a hand across his eyes. He looked at me, and at the revolver on the floor, and then, to my utter amazement, he smiled, and held out his hand.
