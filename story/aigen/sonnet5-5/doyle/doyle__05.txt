I came home that evening to the narrow house in Bursley Street, smelling of clay and coal-smoke as every house in the Potteries smells, and found the landlady waiting in the passage with a letter held out at arm's length, as though it might go off in her hand.

"It's from Miss Stuart," she said. "The boy brought it at noon."

I took it up to my room and read it by the window, where the bottle-ovens stood black against a smoky sunset. It was a short letter, and I have never forgotten a word of it. Nettie wrote that she was sorry, that she had never meant to hurt me, and that she had gone away with Mr. Verrall, who could give her the things that I could not. She begged me not to follow. She asked me to think of her kindly.

I was twenty-three years old, a clerk at nineteen shillings a week, and I sat there with the paper trembling in my fingers until the light went out of the sky. Then I lit no lamp. A man in such a state does not want to see his own face.

I must tell you how it had been, or you will not understand what came after.

Nettie Stuart and I had known each other since we were children, and I had never thought of any other woman. Her father kept a small chandler's shop, and I had a standing welcome there on Sunday evenings. We had spoken of marriage in that vague and hopeful way of the very poor, who always mean to be married next year.

Then Verrall came. He was the son of a rich manufacturer, and he had the easy manner and good tailoring that cost nothing to a man who has never been without them. He drove up to the shop one afternoon on some pretext of buying candles. He came again, and again. By the autumn I could see how she looked at him, and I said nothing, because I had nothing to offer against him but myself.

I think I was aware of my defeat long before the letter came. It is a curious thing that a man may know the worst for weeks and yet be knocked flat when it is written down.

My friend Parload found me next morning, still in my clothes of the day before. He was a long, stooping, sallow fellow, who worked in the same office as I did and spent every night he could spare on the roof of his mother's house with a brass telescope of his own making.

"You'll be sacked if you're late," he said. "But I dare say you know it. Is it the girl?"

I gave him the letter. He read it twice with his lips moving, and handed it back with surprising gentleness.

"I'm sorry, Leadford. Come up on the roof tonight. It will do you good to look at something larger than yourself."

I went, for I had nowhere else to go. The night was clear and bitterly cold, and he swung the little telescope upon a faint smudge of light low in the northern sky.

"There," he said. "A new comet. They have found it only this month. It will come very near us, nearer than any in history, and I tell you, it is a strange one. The spectroscope shows a green band that nobody can account for."

I looked, and saw a pale blur like a thumbprint on glass. I remember thinking it ridiculous that I should be asked to care about it.

I was dismissed from the office a week later, and with justice, for I had made a ruin of the ledgers. Mr. Gedge, the manager, was not an unkind man, but he had a newspaper open upon his desk, and his thoughts were plainly elsewhere.

"It's this business in the Balkans," he said, tapping the print. "The Germans have mobilised on the frontier, and we shall be dragged in, mark me. You had better find another place, Leadford, while there are places to find. When the guns begin there will be no trade at all."

I walked out into the street. Every corner had its knot of men reading the evening papers and arguing in low, angry voices. There was talk of ultimatums, of fleets at sea, of reserves called to the colours. A Socialist orator was haranguing a dozen listeners from a barrow, and beside him a clergyman was declaring that the comet was a sign. Between them the whole town seemed to have been seized by a feverish gaiety, as men are when they feel that the ordinary rules are about to be suspended.

I heard it all as one hears a noise in another room. I had no thought for Europe. I had one thought only, and it had grown cold and definite during the sleepless week.

I learned from a cousin of Nettie's that the pair had taken lodgings in a villa near Sevenoaks, in Kent, and that Verrall's father had washed his hands of the whole affair. I sold my watch and my few books. In a pawnbroker's window in Hanley I bought a revolver, a heavy old Webley with a worn butt, and a box of cartridges.

The man who sold it looked at me closely. "You're not going to do anything foolish, young fellow?"

"I am going to shoot rats," said I.

"Ah," said he. "There's plenty of them about."

I took the night train to London. The carriage was crowded with reservists in civilian clothes, big silent men with bundles, and a few young clerks who talked too loudly. Somewhere beyond Rugby a stout commercial traveller told us that the Kaiser would never dare, and that the whole thing was bluff. Nobody answered him.

Through the window I could see the comet now, grown in a week to a bright hazy star with a pale tail like a drawn feather. Once, when I looked up, I fancied the light of it was faintly green. I told myself it was the dirt on the glass, and I felt the weight of the revolver in my pocket, and was glad of it.

It was past midnight when I came up the lane to the villa, having walked the last nine miles from the station. The road was white and empty. The hedges stood very black, and the air had a strange stillness, as if the whole country were holding its breath. Overhead the comet filled a quarter of the heavens, and its light, which I could no longer mistake, lay over the fields with a greenish, unearthly brightness, so that I cast a faint shadow on the road.

There was a lamp burning in an upper window of the villa. I stood at the gate with my hand upon the cold butt of the pistol and saw, quite clearly, what I intended to do. I would knock, and he would come down, and I would shoot him on his own doorstep. After that I did not care.

Then a curious thing happened. A kind of sweetness came into the air, faint at first, like the smell of a field after rain, and I breathed it in without thinking. My head swam. The hedgerows seemed to lean closer, and the stars, or what I took for stars, rang out in a thin clear note that I felt rather than heard. The anger in my breast, which had been a hard and living thing for a fortnight, began to loosen and melt away. I tried to hold it, as a man tries to hold a dream upon waking, and could not.

The revolver slipped from my fingers and fell into the dust. I did not stoop to pick it up. I stood there looking at the lamplit window, and at the green sky above it, and I began, to my own amazement, to laugh, softly, and then to weep, and I knew in that moment that something vast and gentle had passed over the whole world, and that nothing would ever be quite as it had been before.
