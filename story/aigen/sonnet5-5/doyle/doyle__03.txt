I had better set down at the outset that I was not a man whom any one would have chosen for a hero. I was William Leadford, a clerk out of place, living in a mean street at Clayton, in the Staffordshire Potteries, with a mother's small pension and my own ill temper for company. Yet I had one possession which I valued above the world, and that was the promise of Nettie Stewart.

I remember the evening when I first took notice of the comet. I was walking home from the Bursley road, where I had been refused a situation by a man who did not trouble to look up from his ledger. The sky over the bottle-ovens was for once clear of smoke, and low in it, like a pale thumb-mark upon a window, hung a small, unwinking smudge of light.

"That is the thing the papers are full of," said a collier beside me. "They say it'll strike us."

"Let it," said I, and I meant it.

I had no thought for stars. My mind was upon Nettie, who, for a month past, had grown strange to me: her letters short, her smile absent, her hours unaccounted for. I told myself that it was my poverty that chilled her, and I was nearer the truth than I knew.

I had the truth on a Thursday. I called at her father's house, and found Mr. Stewart standing in the kitchen with a sheet of note-paper in his hand and a face the colour of clay.

"She is gone, Leadford," said he. "Gone to London, and not alone."

I took the paper from him. It was written in her round, schoolgirl hand, and it was very brief. She said that she was not made for a life of pinching; that she was sorry; that Mr. Verrall had been kind, and that she would be happy. That was all. There was not a word of me.

"Verrall?" I said. "Who is Verrall?"

"The young gentleman who has the big house at Lympton Park. Son of the old ironmaster. Surely you've seen him motoring through the town."

I had seen him: a slim, well-brushed young man, with the languid insolence of those who have never been refused anything. I folded the paper very small and put it in my waistcoat pocket. Mr. Stewart spoke to me, I believe, but I heard nothing. There was a singing in my ears, and the room seemed to tilt.

The next fortnight is a blur of grey hours. I lost my last employment, for I would not answer a foreman's insolence with civility, and I took to sitting in the taproom of the Turk's Head, where the talk was all of two things: the comet and the Germans.

"It's the Kaiser's doing," said a pattern-maker, striking the table. "Mark me, there'll be war before the year's out."

"Or the comet will settle it for us," said another, laughing.

I had a newspaper before me, and I read, without interest, of fleets assembling, of ultimatums and counter-notes, of an astronomer at Greenwich who spoke of a "gaseous envelope" and of the earth's passing through the comet's tail. The Powers, it seemed, were creeping towards each other like blind men in a narrow lane, each with a knife behind his back. It struck me that the whole world was as desperate and as ill-tempered as myself, and I found a bitter comfort in the thought.

"It would be a fine thing," I said aloud, "if somebody finished the lot of us."

The pattern-maker stared. "You're not yourself, Leadford."

"On the contrary," said I. "I never was so entirely myself."

That night I resolved what I should do. I had in a drawer a small revolver, which I had bought years before from a retired sergeant, and I took it out and examined it by the light of my candle. It was an ugly, serviceable little weapon. I cleaned it with great care, as a man might who has a duty to discharge, and I loaded every chamber.

I left Clayton by the night train, with my mother asleep and ignorant, and a few shillings in my pocket. I changed at Crewe and again at Rugby, shivering upon platforms where the lamps burned dim, and each time I looked up I saw the comet higher and larger. It was no longer a smudge, but a definite, glowing head with a faint veil trailing behind it, and its light had a curious greenish tinge that I disliked.

At a junction I overheard two porters.

"They say the Germans have crossed the frontier," said one.

"They say a good many things," said the other. "I'll believe it when they've shot me."

I turned my face to the window and thought only of Verrall's throat and Nettie's eyes.

It was evening when I reached the neighbourhood of Lympton Park. I had walked from the station, some seven miles, and the sky as I went was a spectacle I shall never forget. The comet filled a quarter of the heavens. The sun had set, but there was no true darkness; the whole vault was suffused with a pale, unearthly green, so that the hedgerows, the elms, and my own hands looked like things seen under water. The birds were silent. Not a dog barked. In a lane I passed a labourer kneeling by a gate, with his cap in his hands, and he did not stir as I went by.

I felt no fear. Fear had been burned out of me. I had one clear purpose, and the strange light seemed merely a fitting lamp for it.

At the lodge gates I found a little crowd gathered, gazing upward, murmuring. A woman was weeping quietly. I pushed through them as though they had been shadows.

"You can't go up there, mister," said a boy. "There's something in the air."

There was indeed. As I climbed the long avenue, I noticed a faint, sweetish taste upon my tongue, and a lightness in my head. The trees seemed to lean toward me, and the green above had taken on a luminous, palpitating quality, as of a vast breathing thing. I walked more slowly, and my hand upon the revolver in my pocket seemed to belong to somebody else.

Then through the trees I saw the house, with a lamp lit in one window, and a figure moving behind the curtain: slender, graceful, unmistakable. It was Nettie.

I strode forward with the weapon drawn, and at that moment the first thin wreaths of vapour came drifting across the lawn, silvery-green, like the smoke from some enchanted fire. I drew breath to call out Verrall's name, and the breath was never finished.

A great and tender weariness settled upon my limbs. The revolver slipped from my fingers and lay glimmering upon the gravel. I remember thinking, with perfect calmness, "So this is the end of it," and then the ground rose gently to meet me, and the green light closed over my head like water.

I do not know how long I lay there. It might have been an hour, or a night, or an age. But I woke, and the first thing that I knew was that I was cold, and the second that I was breathing air such as I had never breathed before. It was light, sweet, and keen, like the air upon a high hill after rain. I lay upon my back looking at a sky of the clearest, palest blue, and in it hung not a trace of the comet, only the early sun, and a lark rising.

I sat up. My limbs ached, but my mind, to my astonishment, was clear: clear as it had never been in all my embittered life. I looked at the revolver where it lay upon the gravel, a little black, foolish object, and I felt towards it only a faint, wondering disgust, as one might feel towards a bad dream remembered at breakfast.

"What was I going to do?" I said aloud, and the voice that answered in my head was not my own anger, but something quieter and older. "Murder," it said. "Murder, for a girl who never loved you, and a man who never harmed you."

I stood up. All about me the park was washed in a golden freshness. The grass sparkled; the very trees seemed to stand more nobly. And from the house came two figures, walking slowly, hand in hand and bareheaded: Nettie and Verrall. They were pale and wondering, as I was. They looked at me with no fear and no anger, and I looked at them with none.

"Leadford," said Verrall gravely, "I think we have all been very mad."

"Yes," said I, and found, to my own amazement, that I was holding out my hand.
