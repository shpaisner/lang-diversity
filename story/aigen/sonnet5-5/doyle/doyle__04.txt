I.

It was upon a raw evening in October that I learnt Nettie had gone. I remember that the rain had ceased at last, and that a thin yellow light lay along the ridge above Clayton, where the chimneys of the potbanks stood up like the stumps of a burned forest. I had walked home from the works with my mind full of nothing more serious than a leaking boot, and I found my landlady waiting for me in the passage with a letter held out at arm's length, as one holds a thing that may go off.

It was short, and she had not troubled to be kind in it. She was tired, she said, of a life that was all grey streets and small economies. She was going away with Mr. Verrall, who could give her what I could not, and I was not to follow, since it would do no good. That was all. I read it twice standing under the gas-jet, and then I went out again into the street without my cap.

I am William Leadford, and I was then twenty-three years old, a clerk at nineteen shillings a week, with a mother in Hanley and a head stuffed with half-digested books. I mention these dull facts because they explain a great deal. A man who has nothing is apt to put all he has into one place, and I had put all of mine into Nettie.

High over the western roofs, as I stood bareheaded in the wet, there hung a faint smear of light, like a thumb-mark on a darkened window. It was the comet. I had read of it for a month in the papers, between the columns about the Balkans and the German naval estimates. I looked at it without interest and went on walking.

II.

I had known Nettie since we were children. Her father kept a small garden-ground beyond the canal, and I had carried his cabbages to market before I could well carry my own books. She grew into a slight, quick girl with dark hair and a trick of lifting her chin when she was displeased, and I had supposed, with that blind confidence that belongs to the poor and the young, that the matter was settled between us.

Then came Verrall.

He was the son of a man who owned half the land on the Trentham side, and who had lately taken it into his head to be a county personage. Young Verrall rode, shot, and wore good clothes with the careless air of one who has never paid for them. He came down to the garden-ground one summer afternoon to quarrel about a right-of-way, and stayed to talk to the daughter. I do not think he meant very much by it at first. That is the cruelty of such men; their lightest amusements are other people's ruin.

I cannot say that I understood all this at the time. I understood only that Nettie's letters became shorter, that she laughed at my plans, and that on three successive Sundays she was "engaged." I had told myself it was a passing fancy.

Now, walking by the black water of the canal, I saw plainly how foolish I had been. A girl does not forgive a man for being poor. She forgives him anything else.

III.

I should have done something desperate that night had I not run into Parload.

He was a lean, stooping, earnest creature, who had been my companion at the night-school and who now worked in the laboratory of a pottery firm, grinding oxides and dreaming of the stars. He was standing at the corner of Brick Kiln Lane with a pocket telescope, and he hailed me as though nothing in the world had happened.

"Leadford! Come and look. You'll never see the like again."

I told him I had no heart for it, but he took me by the sleeve, and presently I found the little brass tube thrust into my hand. The smear of light became a pale, swollen head with a tail streaming behind it. There was something about it, even then, that I disliked.

"It has altered," said Parload eagerly. "They have got a new line in the spectrum, a line nobody can place. Something is coming to us out of space, Leadford, something that has never touched the earth before."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Dangerous?" He laughed. "The newspapers say there will be a collision, and the newspapers are fools. But it will be wonderful."

I gave him back the glass. It seemed to me a poor sort of wonder that left a man's own small trouble exactly where it was. I said as much, and he looked at me with a sudden shrewd kindness and asked what had happened. When I told him, he was silent. Then he said only, "Don't be a fool, Will," which was precisely what I intended to be.

IV.

The days that followed were strange and feverish. In the works men spoke of nothing but war. The Germans had done this, the French had done that; there were ultimatums, mobilisations, and fleets steaming to unknown stations. In the public-houses of Burslem I heard sober artisans declare that a good war would clear the air, and the same men looked up nervously at the comet, which grew larger and more sullen every night.

Through all this I went about my duties like a man in a dream, and in the evenings I walked. I walked to Trentham, and stood outside the gates of the great house, and was driven off by a gardener. From a servant at the lodge I learnt, for a shilling, that Mr. Edward Verrall had gone to a cottage in the Dane valley, near Congleton, and that he was not alone.

I went that night to a pawnbroker in Hanley and bought a revolver, a heavy, ill-balanced thing with a cracked butt. The man did not ask my business. In those days, I think, nobody asked anybody anything. There was a feeling abroad that the old rules were wearing thin, and that something was about to be settled, one way or another, for the whole world.

I took the first train in the morning. I am not proud of what was in my mind. I had a loaded pistol in my pocket, and a single hard idea in my head, as bare and bright as the blade of a knife.

V.

The cottage stood alone upon a hillside, with a thicket of birch behind it and a thread of stream below. I reached it a little before sunset, having walked from the station along lanes that smelt of wet leaves and woodsmoke. A smoke of its own rose from the chimney. Someone had planted chrysanthemums by the door.

I stood a long time among the birches, and I remember that I was surprised to find my hands perfectly steady.

The light was fading, but it did not fade as light should. A strange coppery glow gathered in the east, and the comet, now a terrible white lantern in the zenith, cast faint double shadows across the grass. The birds were silent. No wind stirred. I have since heard others say that they felt, that evening, as if the earth were holding its breath, and I can only say that it was true.

A lamp was lit in the cottage window. Through the glass I saw Nettie.

She was sitting at a table with her chin on her hand, and she looked very pale and very small. Verrall was not in the room. She was not laughing. She was staring at the lamp as if she had forgotten what it was for.

I drew the revolver and stepped out of the trees.

VI.

The gate gave a little creak as I pushed it, and in that silence it sounded like a shot. I saw her head turn. Then the door opened, and Verrall stood upon the threshold with the lamplight behind him, bareheaded and in his shirt-sleeves, a tall, easy figure with a face I had long wished to strike.

"Who is that?" he called.

"Leadford," I said. My voice was hoarse and strange.

He looked at me, and then at the pistol, and I will say this for him: he did not flinch. He only frowned, as one who is vexed by a tradesman at the wrong hour. "You are a damned fool, Leadford," he said. "Put that away."

"Stand clear of the door."

"Will!" It was Nettie, behind him, with her hand at her throat. "Oh, Will, don't!"

I raised the weapon. I could see the dark centre of his white shirt, and the barrel did not shake. I had rehearsed this moment a hundred times upon the road, and in every rehearsal there had been a joy in it. There was none now. There was only a great weariness and the cold weight of the metal.

Then the light changed.

It began at the edge of the sky, a pale and delicate green, like the first leaf of spring seen through a window. It spread across the heavens with a dreadful beauty, tinting the birches, the cottage roof, the faces of the two before me. Verrall's lips parted. Nettie looked past me, upward, and her eyes grew wide. A faint sweet odour came on the air, unlike anything I had known.

My finger was still upon the trigger, but I found I could no longer remember why.
