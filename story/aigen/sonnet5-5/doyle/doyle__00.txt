I.

I have set down many things in my life which I would gladly forget, but I will begin with the evening when Nettie's letter came, for it was on that evening that I first understood how small a man may be made by his own misery.

I was then twenty-two, a clerk in a pot-bank at Clayton, in the Staffordshire Potteries, earning a pound a week and sleeping in a back room where the smoke of the bottle-ovens lay upon the window like a grey blanket. I had one hope in the world, and her name was Nettie Stanton. We had walked out together for two summers. I had given her what little I had of ambition, and she had listened to it with her grave, bright eyes and had said that I should one day be somebody.

The letter was brief. It lay upon my table beside the cold tea my landlady had left, and I read it standing, with my cap still on.

Dear William,—By the time you read this I shall be gone. Do not look for me. You have been very good, but I cannot go on being poor. Mr. Verrall has been kind, and I am going with him. Forgive me if you can.—N.

I read it three times. Then I sat down upon the bed, and I remember that the ticking of the little clock seemed to grow louder, until it was like a hammer upon an anvil.

II.

Her father kept a small greengrocer's shop in Hanbridge, and I went to him on the following morning. He was a stooping, anxious man, who received me among his crates of cabbages with the air of one who had expected a summons from the bailiff.

"I can tell you nothing, William," said he. "She was always a girl for fine things. I have heard the name of Verrall, no more. A young gentleman, they say, whose people have the great house beyond Newcastle."

"They shall hear of him," said I.

"You will do nothing foolish?"

"I do not know what I shall do," I answered, and that was the truth.

I made inquiries through the day, and by evening I knew enough. Edward Verrall was the only son of a man who had made a fortune out of iron. He had been seen at the railway station with a veiled young woman, and a porter, mindful of a sovereign, had put them upon the London express. That was all. It was enough to turn my sorrow into something harder and more dangerous, as a lump of clay in the kiln is turned from soft earth into something that will cut the hand.

III.

It was my friend Parload who first drew my eyes to the sky.

He was a lean, eager fellow, employed in the same works, who spent his nights upon the hill above Clayton with a battered telescope. I found him there on the third evening, hunched against the wind like a heron.

"You look ill, Leadford," said he, without turning.

"I am well enough. What have you there?"

"Come and see."

I stooped to the eyepiece. Against the dark lay a faint smudge of light, with a bright core at its heart, and from it a feathery stain drawn out across the stars.

"It is a comet," said Parload. "A new one. It has been growing for weeks, and it is coming straight for us. The learned men say it will pass wide, but they are not so sure as they were. There is something queer in its spectrum, a green band that none of them can name."

"What is that to me?" said I bitterly.

"It is something to everybody," said he. "Have you seen the papers? The Germans and the French are at each other's throats again over some wretched colony. There will be war before the year is out. Men are fools, Leadford. They will be fighting when the sky falls on them."

I looked at the pale smear above the chimneys and felt nothing, neither awe nor fear. My own trouble stood between me and the heavens like a wall.

IV.

On the Saturday I drew out the seven pounds that were all my savings. I went into a pawnbroker's in Longton and came out with a revolver wrapped in brown paper, and a box of cartridges that rattled in my pocket like dry peas.

I will not pretend that I was then a sane man. I had worked myself into a fixed idea, as a man in a fever will believe that the pattern of the wallpaper is crawling. I told myself that I was a man wronged, that justice was a thing a poor man must carry in his own hand, and that Verrall and Nettie must be made to understand what they had done. I did not ask what I should do when they understood.

I learned from the railway clerk that Verrall's father had a lodge near the Surrey hills, and that the young man was believed to be using it. I took the night train. The carriage was crowded with talkative men, and every one of them spoke of war, of the Kaiser and the Tsar, of ultimatums and mobilisation. A stout commercial traveller declared that England would be dragged in by Michaelmas. I sat in the corner with my hand upon the parcel in my pocket and looked through the window at the comet, now plainly visible, hanging above the black fields like a lamp swung from a distant ceiling.

V.

I came to the place in the late afternoon of the following day, after a long tramp from the station. The country was strange to me, soft and wooded, with a stillness that seemed almost to listen. The sky had acquired an odd quality. The sun, low in the west, burned with a pale and sickly yellow, and the comet, though it was scarcely evening, shone out in the east with a greenish radiance that I had not seen before.

A labourer was leaning on a gate, staring upward with his pipe dead in his hand.

"Be you for the Lodge?" said he. "Nobody there but the young master and his lady. Queer weather, sir. The birds have stopped."

It was true. I had not noticed the silence until he named it, but there was not a sound in all those woods, not a thrush, not a rook. The very leaves hung motionless.

"They say it is the end of the world," said the man, with a kind of dull cheerfulness.

"Then it is a pity we shall not all be shot first," I said, and I went on, leaving him gaping.

The road climbed between dark pines. A thin, pale vapour was beginning to creep along the ground, and where the light struck it, it gleamed faintly green, like the scum upon a stagnant pool. I drew the revolver from its paper and walked on with it in my hand, and I remember that my heart was beating slowly and heavily, as though it too were waiting.

VI.

The Lodge stood in a clearing, a low house of red brick with a veranda, and a light burned in one window. I crossed the lawn with my feet making no sound upon the dewy grass. Through the glass I saw a table laid for two, and a lamp, and beyond the lamp a woman's figure with its back to me. I knew the set of those shoulders and the way the brown hair was gathered. For a moment my resolution wavered; then I saw a man's hand come into the light and rest upon her arm, and the old fury leapt up in me hot and clear.

I stepped upon the veranda. As I did so the whole sky changed.

It was as though a great green curtain had been drawn across the heavens. The light that poured down was neither day nor night, but something clear and luminous, and it lay upon the garden, the house, and my own raised hand, so that the barrel of the revolver shone like a bar of emerald. The vapour that had crept about my ankles rose in slow, delicate coils. I smelt a keen, sweet freshness, like that of a cold morning upon the moors, and I drew it in with a long breath before I knew what I did.

Inside the room the two figures had turned toward the window. I saw Nettie's face, white and startled, and Verrall's, with his mouth a little open. They did not look afraid. They looked as men look who have heard a great bell sounding far away.

I tried to lift the pistol. My arm would not obey me. A strange lightness crept up through my limbs, and the anger that had held me for three days seemed to loosen its grip, finger by finger. The veranda tilted gently. The green radiance deepened until it filled my eyes.

My last thought, before the darkness took me, was a bewildered wonder that I should feel, at such a moment, so little like a murderer, and so very like a man who is about to wake.
